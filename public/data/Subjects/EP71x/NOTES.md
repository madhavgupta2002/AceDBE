***Startup Dilemmas: Founding or Joining***

# Module 1: Founding or Joining?

## 1.2.1 Wanting to Found vs. Being Ready to Found

### 1.2.1.1 Framing the Decision

#### Core Question
- Assume a committed entrepreneurial career (new ideas, uncertain markets, fast learning, creating something that does not exist).
- Question: **found a venture now, or join a startup first?**
- **Wrong framing:** "Am I an entrepreneur or an employee?"
  - Turns a career decision into an **identity test**.
  - Suggests only founders are entrepreneurial and everyone else stands outside entrepreneurship.

#### Key Concept: The Better Next Move
- **Founding** and **joining** are both routes into an entrepreneurial career.
- They differ in **position, responsibility, learning, risk, ownership and control**.
- The decision is about **what to do next**, based on what you know, what you care about and what opportunities exist **now**; it is **not** a lifelong identity choice.
- **Careers are sequences, not labels:**
  - Join a startup, learn, build a network, discover a problem, found later.
  - Found, close the venture, join another company, found again.
  - Work in an established organisation, build deep domain knowledge, then build.

#### Objective
- Evaluate the **personal career and market circumstances** that shape the founding-or-joining decision.
- Articulate **motivations and their implications**.

#### Decision Under Uncertainty
- **No formula** tells every ambitious student to found; **no research finding** tells every young person to wait.
- Evidence improves judgment; expert opinion challenges instincts; other people's lives give useful constructs; **your own interpretation remains necessary**.

#### Possible Drivers Behind an Initial Choice
| Driver | Question to test it |
|---|---|
| Problem worth solving | Do you already see a real problem? |
| Autonomy | Do you want to decide what is built and with whom? |
| Learning | Do you want fast exposure? |
| Dislike of a boss | Is this a reason or an escape? |
| Founder stories | Has founding become an "ambitious career" image? |
| Perceived safety | Does founding feel safer? |
| Family circumstances | How much certainty can you absorb? |

#### Types of Claims
| Phrase | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **"Research suggests"** | Claim supported by a systematic study |
| **"X argues"** | Opinion of an influential practitioner |
| **Named people (e.g., Falguni Nayar, Kunal Shah)** | Live illustration, **not** proof |
| **"One way to interpret this"** | One data point; a practical implication for the choice |

- Entrepreneurial careers are vulnerable to **stories**: stories are vivid, evidence is not always available; **good judgment needs both**.

### 1.2.1.2 Wanting to Found vs Readiness

#### Key Concept: Wanting Is Not a Reason
- **Wanting to be a founder** is **not** the same as **having a reason to found now**.
- The founder identity is attractive: it signals **ambition, independence, creativity, courage**.
- Faulty sequence: "I want to be a founder, therefore I need an idea."
- A venture does **not** exist to complete the founder's identity; it survives only if it **creates enough value for customers, users and other stakeholders**.
- A **startup** is a **temporary organisation in search of a sustainable business model**; having started does **not** mean business-model elements are in place.

### 1.2.1.3 Paul Graham: Before the Startup

#### Source Profile
- Founder of **Y Combinator** (accelerator behind Reddit, Airbnb; Indian startups such as Razorpay and Meesho have also gone through it).
- Sam Altman (OpenAI) was part of and later headed Y Combinator.
- The essay is **one experienced person's interpretation**, **not** an empirical study.

#### Warnings and Advice
| Point | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Warning** | Young people can become too knowledgeable about the **outward mechanics** of startups and too weakly connected to a **real problem** |
| **"Playing house"** | Imitating startup appearance (fundraising language, valuation, pitch decks) without building anything |
| **Fall in love with the problem, not the solution** | Mechanics cannot substitute for **making something people want** |
| **Failure rate** | About **90%** of startups fail, many because they made what the founders wanted rather than what customers want |
| **Advice 1** | Work on problems that **genuinely interest** you |
| **Advice 2** | Work with people you **like and respect** |
| **Domain expertise** | Grows from genuine curiosity and sustained contact with important problems, **not** from forced brainstorming titled "find a startup idea" |
| **Maggi illustration** | Brainstorming was about **problems** people have (matched to capabilities), **not** a startup idea |

- Startup mechanics are **not useless**; learn them when they become relevant.

#### Timing Position
- For most people around **20**, favours **broad exploration** over the all-consuming depth of a startup.
- **Exception:** if a **side project genuinely takes off**, pursuing it can be reasonable.
- Position is **not "never found young"**; it is **do not force founding merely because you like the title**.

## 1.2.2 Founding or Joining: Motivations, Trade-offs and Biases

### 1.2.2.1 Advantages of Youth

#### Key Concept: Youth as an Asset
- Fewer fixed commitments.
- **Lower opportunity cost**.
- Proximity to new technologies and emerging behaviour.
- Energy for rapid experimentation.
- Peer network that sees problems before established organisations do.
- Some knowledge becomes obsolete quickly; in a fast-changing market, **being new can mean being close to the business**.

#### Experience Without Employment
- Experience is not acquired only through employment.
- Building a real product, serving real users, making and repairing mistakes is an intense form of experience.
- If a **credible opportunity exists now, delay can destroy it** rather than improve it.
- The alternative to Graham is **not** the slogan "start as early as possible" but the question: **what do you know, see or have access to that gives a reason to found now?**

### 1.2.2.2 Valid Reasons to Found Now

| Reason | Illustration / Detail |
|---|---|
| **Painful problem encountered repeatedly** | A person overcame a severe skin condition using micronutrients, understood absorption through research, and started a nutraceutical company |
| **Complementary co-founder with mutual trust** | Capabilities fit and trust exists |
| **Small solution already in use** | Users are using what you built |
| **Technological or regulatory shift** | Creates a temporary opening |
| **Life situation allows experimentation** | Unusual room to experiment |

- **"I always wanted to call myself a founder"** is a motivation, **not** a reason to start now.

### 1.2.2.3 Genuine Readiness

#### Definition: Readiness
- **Readiness does not mean complete knowledge.** No founder is fully ready; if readiness required every answer, nobody would found (a unicorn such as Uniphore began without having the answers).
- **Readiness** = the combination of **opportunity, people, motivation and capacity** is strong enough to justify **learning through action**.
- The task is **not to eliminate uncertainty**, but to decide:
  - whether the uncertainty is **worth entering right now**, and
  - whether **founding is the best position from which to learn**.

#### Problem vs Founderhood Test
- Am I attracted to a **problem** or mainly to **founderhood**?
- If the founder title disappeared and nobody admired the choice, would I still spend years on this problem?
- The test does not settle the decision but **removes noise**.

### 1.2.2.4 What Are You Optimising For

#### Key Concept
- Replace "which option is more prestigious?" with **"what am I optimising for?"**
- No motive is automatically noble or ignoble; each has different implications.

| Priority | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Autonomy** | Decide what gets built, whom to work with, which trade-offs to accept |
| **Learning** | Exposure to customers, products, technology, hiring, sales, operations at a speed a classroom cannot reproduce |
| **Financial upside** | Willing to accept a low-probability, high-payoff exit |
| **Stability / family responsibility** | Need predictable income; cannot expose savings to an open-ended experiment |

### 1.2.2.5 Founding vs Joining: Trade-offs

| Dimension | Founding | Joining |
|---|---|---|
| **Direction** | Greater initial scope to shape direction, but the **burden of direction transfers to you** | Less control over the organisation |
| **Ownership** | Meaningful ownership, but **ownership is not liquid wealth** | Less ownership |
| **Autonomy** | Autonomy, yet dependent on customers, employees, investors, regulation, cash flow | Focus, colleagues ahead of you, salary, access to a system already in motion |
| **Learning** | Fast, but the **learning agenda is set by survival**, not a template | A good role **compresses years of learning**; a poor role forces repetitive execution with little context |

### 1.2.2.6 Wasserman: The Founder's Dilemmas

#### Key Concept: Rich or King
- Noam Wasserman, **The Founder's Dilemmas** (chapter 2): the **career decision to become a founder**, and the **personal circumstances and motivations** around that leap of faith.
- Central tension: **create a lot of wealth ("rich")** vs **retain control of the organisation ("king")**.

## 1.3.1 Founding Decisions: Control, Passion and Bias

### 1.3.1.1 Control vs Wealth

#### Key Concept: Recurring Tension
- A founder who **insists on control** may have very **limited resources** to create value.
- A founder who **brings in resources** can build a **larger kingdom** but **surrenders part of the throne**.
- This is a **recurring tension**, not a rule that every decision has two options.

#### Correcting the Fantasy: "If I found, I will own the company and be free"
- Customers can say no.
- Co-founders can disagree.
- Employees can leave.
- Investors and the board can demand changes.
- Growth creates obligations the original founder may not anticipate.

#### Motive Determines Fit
| Central motive | Implication |
|---|---|
| **Control** | High-growth founding keeps asking you to **exchange control for resources** |
| **Wealth or scale** | **Shared authority** must be acceptable |
| **Learning** | Joining a strong team may give **more learning with less existential pressure** |

#### Illustration: Preserving Control
- Sanjeev Bikhchandani left employment in **1990**, built under financial constraint and **bootstrapped for years** before Naukri started to grow.
- Lesson is **not** "bootstrap for 10 years"; it is that **preserving control has consequences: patience, constraint and a different growth path**.

#### Benefit Test
| Benefit sought | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| **Autonomy** | Are you prepared for **responsibility without boundaries**? |
| **Wealth** | Are you prepared for **dilution and illiquid equity**? |
| **Learning** | Have you compared it with the learning available in a **strong startup role**? |

- If unsure of your own motive, ask 2-3 friends who know you well what kind of person you are.

### 1.3.1.2 Passion

#### Key Concept: A Double-Edged Sword
| Helps | Distorts |
|---|---|
| Supplies **energy** | Becomes part of identity |
| Supplies **persistence** (you do not give up) | Acts as a **filter**: we read the world to support choices already made |
| Directs **attention**; work does not feel heavy | Founders may refuse worldviews different from theirs |

### 1.3.1.3 Cognitive Biases in Founding Decisions

| Bias | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Confirmation bias** | Seek information that supports what we want to believe |
| **Overconfidence** | Overestimate our judgment or control |
| **Planning fallacy** | Underestimate time and effort required |
| **Escalation of commitment** | Commit more because we are already invested |
| **Survivorship bias** | Entrepreneurial stories come from survivors; equally passionate founders who shut down are unheard |
| **Hindsight bias** | "I would have joined Flipkart in a heartbeat" is easy now; at the time few joined, many similar startups failed, and prestige titles (e.g., McKinsey) were preferred over tiny startups; few joined Amagi when it recruited on campus |

### 1.3.1.4 Countering Bias with Evidence
- Replace a slogan ("building the future of education") with explicit beliefs about the **user, problem and solution**.
- **Search for disconfirming evidence**; try to prove yourself wrong.
- Define in advance **what would make you stop the search or change direction**.
- Talk to people **willing to disagree**; people tend to tell you what you want to hear.
- Passion must be **supported by evidence**: does it help you attend to reality or avoid it?
- Decide on **evidence**, not on hunch, identity or passion.

## 1.3.2 What Experience Gives You

### 1.3.2.1 Research Evidence

| Study | Setting | Finding | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gupta and colleagues** | Immigrant first-time entrepreneurs in the US; green-card waiting-period variation used to estimate the effect of extra work experience | Startups of more experienced founders do better on **funding, patents and workforce growth**; mechanism is **team formation** (recruit former colleagues, start with a larger team) | Specific US immigrant-founder setting; does **not** prove every student should wait, that age creates wisdom, or that any job gives useful entrepreneurial experience |
| **Azoulay and colleagues** | Data across long periods covering a large part of the universe of founders | Successful founders are **not very young**; **mean founder age among the fastest-growing 1 in 1,000 ventures was 45**; **industry-specific experience** strongly associated with success | 45 is **not** an ideal age; young founders and dropout outliers are over-represented in media |

### 1.3.2.2 Assets Experience Can Build
- **Domain knowledge**
- **Exposure to problems**
- **Networks**
- **Credibility**
- **Pattern recognition**
- **Self-knowledge**

### 1.3.2.3 Experience as a Double-Edged Sword
- A comfortable job makes **inertia** hard to break.
- Experience can **harden into assumptions** that may not be true.
- Large organisations teach **processes designed for scale, not discovery** (good for 1 to N, not necessarily for 0 to 1).
- **Years worked are not the same as years learned**: ask what you are learning and how.

### 1.3.2.4 Different Career Paths, Different Capabilities

| Founder | Path | Capabilities developed |
|---|---|---|
| **Falguni Nayar** | 18 years at Kotak Mahindra | Business understanding, networks, credibility, exposure to managerial tasks; accumulated **social and knowledge capital**; may have lacked tech and online knowledge |
| **Kunal Shah** | Freelance work, programming, repeated ventures, pivots, Freecharge, Sequoia, CRED | **Varied** experiences across ventures, in contrast to one long institutional career |

## 1.4.1 Building Experience Before Founding

### 1.4.1.1 Learning Gap Analysis
- Ask: **what must I learn to be unusually well-positioned to found?**
  - Technical depth, customer access, regulation, sales, operations.
- Identify assets you lack, then ask **where you can learn them quickest and most transparently**.
- If jumping in is the best way to learn, do that; if working at a startup is better, do that.
- **Waiting is not preparation.**

### 1.4.1.2 Joining First as an Entrepreneurial Strategy

#### Key Concept
- Joining is **not a fallback** for people lacking an idea or courage; it can be a **deliberate investment in entrepreneurial capacity**.
- You learn **inside someone else's experiment while receiving a salary**:
  - How hypotheses become products.
  - How customers resist.
  - How teams coordinate.
  - How limited cash shapes priorities.
- You can build experience in product, engineering, sales, design, operations or people.

#### Learning Is Not Automatic
- Startup may be **chaotic without being educational**.
- Role may be **too narrow**.
- Founders may not be **transparent**, limiting exposure.
- You may see poor organisational practices.
- Equity can become **worthless** if the startup folds.

### 1.4.1.3 Risk-Return Profile: Founder vs Joinee

| Aspect | Founder | Joinee |
|---|---|---|
| **Stake** | Responsibility, identity and ownership concentrated in one venture | Receives **cash** rather than control |
| **Control** | Holds control | No control |
| **Exit option** | Committed to venture | **Can leave**; may have bigger value outside |
| **Upside** | Ownership value | If the startup succeeds, a strong brand label on the CV |

### 1.4.1.4 Choose the Role, Not Just the Company

#### Key Concept: Company Success vs Individual Learning
- Company success and personal learning are **connected but not identical**.
- Do **not** ask "is the startup promising?"; ask **"which role lets me learn, contribute and become the person I want to be?"**
- A successful startup can offer a role with little learning; a failed startup can give rich capabilities at a great pace.

#### Illustration: Joining Flipkart
- The useful question is **how people assessed it at the time**: seriousness of founders, early customer traction, being the only Indian player experimenting at scale with a model emerging in the US.
- They joined **with a purpose**, not mainly expecting equity to convert into wealth.

#### Practical Pointers
- In a company, ask for roles that give the learnings you need before founding.
- A startup that has just raised **seed or Series A** has some investor buy-in that it solves an interesting problem; still **examine the role**.

### 1.4.1.5 Found Now vs Join First

| Choice | Entrepreneurial when |
|---|---|
| **Found now** | It rests on an **opportunity, not an identity**: you have customers, users or traction, and joining elsewhere would mean leaving that learning behind and coming back |
| **Join first** | It is chosen **intentionally** for learning, not as a default |

- Found if the **opportunity has substance** and you can pursue it.

### 1.4.1.6 Affordable Loss Test

#### Definition: Affordable Loss (effectual principle)
- Decide in advance how much you can lose and **time-box** the trial.
- **Found test:** give yourself 3 or 6 months; if nothing happens, go join a startup.
- **Join test:** join a startup for 6 months and check: am I learning? Is it what I thought?
- Affordable loss applies to **both paths**.

### 1.4.1.7 Four Types of Evidence

| Evidence | Key question |
|---|---|
| **Opportunity evidence** | Is the problem **real, frequent, costly or urgent**? |
| **Capability evidence** | Do you have access to what the next stage requires (what the product needs, ability to win many customers, marketing capability)? |
| **Commitment evidence** | How do you obtain commitments from your **team and from yourself**? |
| **Life-context evidence** | Your **affordable loss, family situation and responsibilities**, where you come from |

### 1.4.1.8 Family and Life Context
- Do **not** ignore family and life context because "starting is mature".
- Stories such as "my family said no, I lacked experience, my team left, yet I succeeded" are **survivorship** accounts; those who failed with equal passion remain unseen (like a stone dropped in a pond leaving brief ripples).
- Talk to family and friends; seek **buy-in** for a time-boxed trial ("if nothing happens, this is an affordable loss and I will join a company").

### 1.4.1.9 The Best Next Move

#### Key Concept
- The decision is **not** "Am I a founder or a joinee?"
- Ask: **Who am I? What do I know? What do I need to learn? What resources are available today? Therefore, what is my best next move?**

## 1.6.1 Interview with Vibhanshu Golia

### 1.6.1.1 Background and Career Path

#### Profile
- Education: IISc (Bachelor of Science Research, a 4-year programme started in 2012; first 3 terms common courses, major chosen after term 3), then IIM Bangalore (via CAT).
- Moved from **physics** to **material science** (biomaterials and implants), published a paper, declined a Rolls-Royce material-scientist offer: he draws energy from talking to people and expected to be "a good scientist, not the best".
- Pure-science industry ecosystem in India described as very nascent.
- Then **consulting at McKinsey**, then left to build a venture (YouTube/community presence, then Dhanda School).

### 1.6.1.2 Three Career Options

| Path | Features |
|---|---|
| **Large company / MNC** | Good learning and exposure, but "a small fish in a big organisation" |
| **Join a startup** | Early stage, small team; for a 20-21-year-old, considered the best day-0 answer: guidance from a founder, a cushion since you are not the entrepreneur, like an "entrepreneur in residence" picking up skills while watching a team of 10-12 |
| **Start your own** | Very courageous, but "playing in the dark" with many unknown unknowns that you must figure out |

### 1.6.1.3 Mimetic Desire and Campus Choices

#### Definition: Mimetic Desire
- **Desires are mimetic**: people want what others want, not objectively (kindergarten analogy: one child picks a toy and every child wants it).
- On an MBA campus, everyone wants **consulting** because it is what toppers chase.

#### Illustration
- Pre-MBA expectation was driven by salary figures (about 15 LPA vs about 30 LPA post-MBA) with no knowledge of career avenues.
- IIMB is consulting-heavy: about **150-200 of a batch of 500** are placed in consulting; MBB firms carry an aura.
- Strong academics (gold medal) led to shortlists everywhere; the outside view of the job differs from the lived reality.

### 1.6.1.4 Reality of Consulting

| Expectation | Reality |
|---|---|
| Exposure to top MNCs and diverse problems | True and valued |
| Satisfaction from the job | **Intense hours** and **ambiguity of deliverables** |
| Stable topic | Rapid switches (a 4-week insurance financial-planning study, then a B2B heavy-machinery plant project) requiring fast content mastery |
| Client respect | Young consultant (24-25) not taken seriously initially; must speak client's language and build rapport |

### 1.6.1.5 The Extrapolation Test

#### Key Concept
- Extrapolate what you are doing now: **does it lead to a place where you would be happier?**
- Look at **who is at the top of a career line** and ask whether you want that lifestyle.
- At 6-8 months he examined partners' lives: decent wealth but little family time; not entrepreneurial-scale wealth.
- Same effort invested in your own venture may offer **more upside**.
- **Wealth is only part of the objective function**; also friends, family, health, sport, community, value generation and impact.
- He felt consulting impact on the ground did not match the impact shown on slides.
- Wealth routes are now broader than only high-paying jobs.

### 1.6.1.6 Peers and Risk Appetite
- The IIMB batch's **risk appetite is described as very low**; very few start companies, some join family businesses, most want corporate jobs.
- Reasons: fear of leaving a job, using savings, and having worked hard to crack the entrance exam; talent is **not** the bottleneck.
- His own openness came partly from arriving at IIMB by chance in his fourth year.
- Observed **lack of reflection**, but many peers are happy (e.g., a TCS consultant with similar entry pay, about 35 vs 30 LPA, and light hours).
- **Do not glamorise entrepreneurship**; it is not everyone's ballgame.
- **"The itch to do something happens when you have no time to do it."** If time exists and you still do not act, the company is not to blame (the Zomato founder worked on FoodieBay alongside a job).
- Colleagues jokingly urging him to work on his own thing planted the idea.

### 1.6.1.7 Why Joining a Startup Is Under-Chosen

#### Context
- Startups at **Series B or C** show product-market fit and are scaling, so they are more validated than Series A.
- Early joiners of Flipkart (after Series B), CaratLane and Amagi saw equity value grow.
- **You cannot become wealthy selling hours; wealth comes from owning equity.**

#### Three Reasons Students Do Not Choose Startups
| Reason | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Information asymmetry** | Seed/Series A startups are focused on their work and do not market to campus; about **200 seed or Series A companies** exist in Bangalore; even excluding 40-50 weak ones, about 150 can be approached; students must research funds, family offices, where capital flows and form their own view |
| **Value-add to founder** | Busy founders respond to a **clear proposition** ("I can do X, my expertise is Y") far more than a cold "I like what you do, can I help?" |
| **Prestige** | Names like Goldman Sachs or McKinsey feel better than an unknown AI/tech startup (a smaller reason) |

- BBA-level talent is cheaper and more open-minded than MBA talent, so startups can value it.

### 1.6.1.8 Why He Founded Instead of Joining

#### Reasoning
- Wished to **capitalise on a network/community** (YouTube, Instagram) that is a distribution system which fades if he "goes dark".
- Could get first leap of faith now (students signing up, investors reaching out); chose to "take the plunge" rather than keep a backfoot.
- Admits it may not be objectively a good idea and that his answer may change in 2-3 years; set **6-8 months** to test and **course-correct early**.
- Loan: EMI extended to 15 years at 7%; views debt as normal **leverage** and considers himself "rightly levered".

#### Who He Consulted
| Source | Role |
|---|---|
| **Parents** | Conveyed rather than asked; they trust his education; he stated "worst case I can get a job"; he only had to win them over on YouTube |
| **Builder network** | Founders of a young VC fund (Localhost HQ) and co-working peers |
| **Friend (later co-founder)** | Discussed leaving a demanding job together |
| **Colleagues** | Teased him to go work on his own work |

- Mindset: ask **"why not?"** rather than "why?" and fix a long horizon (a marathon, not a sprint).

### 1.6.1.9 MBAs, Builders and BBA Graduates

| Point | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Builders' view of MBAs** | Technical founders meet MBAs mostly as VCs asking standard questions (5-year revenue, channels, moat), missing technical depth |
| **MBA still matters** | Selling, capital markets, how money moves |
| **Tech must become business** | Founders must shift from entrepreneur to **manager and institution builder** (coordination, smooth bureaucracy) |
| **Illustration** | A fast-grown deep-tech firm (Sarvam) lacked processes, clear communication and points of contact, so it recruits ex-consultants and FMCG leaders |
| **BBA advantage** | Younger, **trainable, lower cost, open-minded and malleable**; MBA thinking is more deterministic ("which button to press") |

### 1.6.1.10 Choosing a Company and Presenting Yourself

#### Where to Play
- Assess **market size** and attractiveness.
- Ideally start in a **niche** where you have a strong **right to win**.
- Past experience shapes how you see the industry (his own long study of education gave him understanding of ed-tech and buyer decisions).
- The same analysis applies to deciding whether to found.

#### Proof of Concept
- Recruiters look for **proof of concept**, not resume claims.
- Maintain a searchable online space: portfolio website, YouTube channel, LinkedIn.
- At BBA level, club projects and course work **adjacent to the founder's work** count.

#### Communication
- Sound **authentic**, avoid generic LinkedIn templates ("I'm thrilled to announce").
- Be **top-down**: start with the insight or ask, then explain.
- Role-play the founder: what would he be evaluating?

### 1.6.1.11 Insight-and-Ask Framework

#### Key Concept
- Reach out by cold call, cold email or cold inbox using **insight(s) plus an ask**.

| Element | Guidance |
|---|---|
| **Research flavour** | About 2 lines on what they do; do not explain their business to them |
| **Insights** | One or two insights (non-obvious or **counter-intuitive** is better) that show deep thinking and that only someone in the space would know |
| **Ask** | Close clearly with what you can do or want from them |
| **Delivery** | Keep it precise; subject line matters |

- Insight can arise from adjacent study (knowledge of handloom led to knowledge of cotton and then cotton seeds).
- Without clarity on insights and ask, luck will not give outcomes.

### 1.6.1.12 Solo or With a Co-founder

#### Key Concept: Do Not Start Solo
- Solo start was his mistake.
- Timeline: left job in October; Nov-Jan rest and travel; took YouTube seriously; brand deals paid **more than his McKinsey salary** but were volatile and made him complacent; from May invested heavy hours.
- Solo building becomes **lonely**; a co-founder most importantly supports **mental health**, enables co-decisions and confidence.
- Co-founders usually come from **college or early jobs**, rarely networking events; you must enjoy their company.
- After the co-founder joined full time (July-August), more was done in 2 months than in the first months.
- Risk illustration: Wozniak started partly because he loved hanging out with Jobs, but later differences in views of justice and building a company emerged.

#### How the Co-founder Was Recruited
- Existing close friend; helped rehearse how to leave his bank job; the friend weighed a competing big-name offer and a CFO role, then committed by July.

### 1.6.1.13 Equity Split and Affordable Loss in Practice

#### Equity
| Point | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Split used** | Co-founder holds **10% for now**; to revisit as the company scales |
| **Rationale** | A "fail-safe" stake he deserves while the working relationship is untested |
| **Better practice** | Add equity later rather than dividing $\frac{100}{N}$ upfront, since life situations change |
| **Gap** | Understanding is only verbal; **best to document equity split and decision-maker on paper** |
| **Sensitive topic** | Must be discussed with co-founder |

#### Affordable Loss
| Dimension | Limit |
|---|---|
| **Time** | 6 more months, 3 more cohorts (first free, others paid); March 2027 as the litmus test |
| **Capital** | Own savings from McKinsey and Barclays; first cohort online before committing to a physical centre; applying for grants to avoid VC equity |
| **Outcome** | If enough sign-ups do not come, change the offering or shut down |
| **Lean model** | Avoid rapid VC-fuelled expansion (lesson from failures such as Unacademy) |

### 1.6.1.14 Case: Dhanda School

#### Concept
- **Practical, builder-first, affordable business school** for first-time founders.
- Triggered by the view that Indian education is mostly competitive-exam preparation; trends: high graduate unemployment and rising AI automation.
- MBA costs about 25-30 lakh and 2 years; Dhanda School costs about **1/100** (about ₹25,000).

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Practical** | Indian business case studies (Ghadi detergent, Balaji Wafers) |
| **Builder first** | Admits people already doing something, any scale or age |
| **Affordable** | Fraction of MBA price |
| **Four philosophies** | Wealth comes from ownership; no degree needed; no right age; network is the moat |

#### MVP and Validation
- Test: a 4-hour paid finance masterclass at ₹999.
- Result: **200 sign-ups in 24 hours**, so $200 \times ₹999 \approx ₹2$ lakh.
- Recorded video on YouTube reached **120K views in 3 weeks** with zero marketing spend.

#### Cohort Design
- 4-week cohort with two MECE tracks: **product-led / direct-to-consumer** and **service-led / B2B**.
- Intended price ₹20,000; first cohort free as an experiment.
- **600 applications in week one**, 70 selected, waitlist about 900-950.
- Unit economics: about $40 \times ₹20{,}000 \approx ₹8$ lakh per month of working capital.
- Online sessions (19 September to 29 October), recordings on YouTube ("you can't gatekeep knowledge"); participants in pods of 4 execute simple cash-generating businesses; leaderboard on sales; winners flown to Bangalore to meet the network.
- Paid value is the **experience, collective learning and network**.

## 2.1 Welcome to the Module

### 2.1.1 Welcome to the Module

#### 2.1.1.1 Core Question of the Module
- Choosing to start or join a startup is only the beginning; the next question is **who you are going to build with**.
- The question applies to both seats:
  - **Founding:** you choose co-founders actively.
  - **Joining:** you choose the team from outside, **before you have signed anything**.
- Module question: **"Who should I build with, and how do I tell before it is too late whether it will work out?"**

#### 2.1.1.2 What the Module Covers
| Theme | Focus |
|---|---|
| Team formation | How founding teams form; why founders choose people they already trust; how this creates **blind spots** |

| Team-formation strategies | Interpersonal attraction vs. resource-seeking |
| Personality and role diversity | How complementary roles strengthen a founding team |
| External evaluation | Signals visible from outside: role clarity, equity agreements, handling of disagreements, how founders talk about each other |

#### 2.1.1.3 Nature of the Approach
- It is **not a checklist**: ticking 3 or 4 boxes does not make a team "safe".
- **Team formation is very hard to predict**, even with research.
  - Example: some investors reportedly passed on funding Infosys because they doubted the co-founders would stick, yet its founders were among the strongest teams ever to set up a company in India.
- **Hindsight is always 20-20**; the aim is to act cautiously while things are still uncertain.
- Seeing the risk **does not eliminate it**; it means you do not walk in blind. You may still join the team.
- A set of **heuristics** lets you enter a team and relationship aware rather than naive.
  - Analogy: driving in dense fog by following very close to the road separator; a simple heuristic shows where the road is.

## 2.2 Who Should I Build With

### 2.2.1 Who Should I Build With

#### 2.2.1.1 The Zepto Story (Zepto Case)
- Dominant narrative: two teenage Stanford dropouts (**Palicha and Vohra**) pursued quick commerce and became a **unicorn in record time**.
- Part left out: before it was Zepto it was **KiranaKart**, and in the early months a **third person**, student **Ansh Nanda**, was in the room.
  - Claimed deep involvement in founding work: developing the product and technology, and lining up early retail partnerships.
  - Claimed he was **verbally promised a 20% stake and a co-founder title**, conditional on staying through a particular date.
  - He did not receive the equity.
- Aftermath (years later, when Zepto was worth billions): a **legal dispute, a police complaint, allegations on both sides**, then a **private settlement** (terms unknown).
- The case is not about who was right or wrong; it illustrates what research on founding teams predicts will happen **at scale, across ecosystems and countries**.

#### 2.2.1.2 Structural Blind Spot (Key Concept)
- Nothing in the outcome required anyone to be a bad person.
- It resembles a **structural default**: what happens unless the founding team **actively works against it**.
- Implication:
  - You **cannot screen for dishonest founders**; asking whether someone is trustworthy is not enough.
  - Inputs from others can help, but the **final decision-maker is you**, so you must know what to look for.
- Founding-team conflicts arise from **structural blind spots, not bad intentions**; informal agreements become fragile.

#### 2.2.1.3 Why Co-founder Relationships Fail
- Co-founding relationships do **not fail as random acts or freak issues**.
- They fail **predictably and for the same reasons**.
- There are **signals** of fragility that the early team navigates before it blows up, and these are **visible from outside even before you accept an offer**.
- Reflection case: what could Ansh Nanda, in January 2021, deciding whether to join KiranaKart, have looked for to see the agreement was fragile? (Answered by the external signals in 2.4.)

## 2.3 Trust, Complementarity and Homophily

### 2.3.1 Trust, Complementarity and Homophily

#### 2.3.1.1 Solo vs. Team Founding
| Aspect | Team founding | Solo founding |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome (on average) | **Outperforms solo on survival and scale** | Lower on average |
| Resources | More hands, skills and capital at the table | Limited to one person |
| Resilience | Handles emotional rollercoaster, burnout, illness, personal issues | **Single point of failure**; no one to consult or correct mistakes |
| Judgment | **Distributed judgment**: less likely that 2 or 3 people are wrong about the same thing at the same time | One viewpoint |
| Risk | **Distributes risk** | Concentrated in one person |

- Teams are better **on average**, but the average hides large **variance**.
- **A bad team is worse than a good solo founder**: time and energy go into easing friction instead of building.
- The **team premium shows up only when the team actually functions**.
- Correct advice is **not** "always get a co-founder" but **"get the right co-founder, or start alone"** (broadly speaking).
- Next question: not whether to have a co-founder but **who to co-found with and why that specific person**.

#### 2.3.1.2 Choosing Deliberately: Founding vs. Joining
| Seat | Nature of decision |
|---|---|
| **Founding** | An active choice; choose **deliberately** rather than defaulting to the first available, willing person |
| **Joining** | An **inheritance**; you cannot pick the co-founders, but you can **evaluate whether they picked well** |

- Co-founder "speed dating" is described as a bad idea; think carefully about how you evaluate.
- Keep asking which seat you are imagining yourself in.

#### 2.3.1.3 Homophily
- **Homophily:** the tendency to bond with people who are like you ("birds of a feather flock together"); it is ingrained.
- Typical shared markers among co-founders: **same college, hostel, previous-job team, city, school, language**.
- Why it feels sensible:
  - You **trust people who feel familiar**; you may already have been tested together (hackathon, project, job).
  - Things are **efficient**; no structured search at 2 a.m. under a deadline, just "do you want to start?"
- Why it backfires: **homophily optimizes for comfort, not complementary skill**.

| Cost of homophily | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Overlapping strengths and blind spots** | Same background means agreement is fast, but both lack the same skill (e.g., iteration, sales, finance); like "taking decisions with a twin" |
| **Compressed network** | Co-founder moves in the same circles, so you double up on the same network instead of adding new groups |
| **Survivorship bias** | Visible Indian success stories with similar founders (Flipkart, Meesho) are seen; the many that blew up are not |
| **Misaligned mission** | Why you are starting and what each is staking must also be aligned |

- Research finding: **teams of friends have higher co-founder turnover than teams of strangers**, not lower.
  - Existing comfort does **not** protect a partnership when things go bad.
  - Friends are more reluctant to have **blunt conversations** that would prevent a future blow-up (the elephant in the room): not wanting to seem a bad person or the first to raise it.
- A founding team that "has known each other forever" should **lower** your confidence **unless** they have explicitly discussed **who does what** and **conflict resolution / who takes the final decision**, well before starting.
- Summary: homophily gives **speed and comfort early**, but does **not** reliably give **breadth of skills or stability under pressure**.

#### 2.3.1.4 Trust vs. Complementarity (Key Concept)
| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Trust** | "I believe you will act in good faith, will not cheat me, and I feel safe being vulnerable with you." |
| **Complementarity** | "You bring skills, networks and judgment I do not have; together we cover more ground than either alone." |

- **Common confusion:** you deeply trust someone (e.g., known 10 years) and **infer complementarity that was never tested** ("I trust him, so we will make a great team").
- The two are **different claims**:
  - **High trust, zero complementarity:** two people who never betrayed each other but share **identical skill sets and blind spots**.
  - **Complementarity with limited trust:** two people who cover each other's gaps but are **functionally strangers under pressure**; this does not automatically make it a worse decision to build a company on.
- Complementarity needed in: **skills, perspectives, networks** (e.g., sales, finance), plus aligned mission.

#### 2.3.1.5 The Practical Test
- **"Would I have chosen to work with this specific person on this specific problem if I had not already trusted them?"**
- If the honest answer is **no** (the only reason is that you like them), that is **not disqualifying**; it means you must **deliberately look for the skill gaps** that trust is covering up.
- Example: Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak
  - Wozniak said he simply liked hanging out with Jobs, which is why they started.
  - They were complementary in skill set, but their missions were not aligned.

#### 2.3.1.6 Zepto Revisited: High Trust, High Homophily
- Palicha and Vohra were school students and childhood friends who left Stanford together: about as **high-trust and high-homophily** as possible.
- It likely helped them **move fast and skip early traction friction**.
- It says nothing about the **third person**: someone brought in later **for a skill** rather than pre-existing trust.
  - Trust between the original two **did not automatically extend fairness** to the outsider.
- The outsider lacks the same trust level, so things must be **spelled out**; otherwise a person who works hard on a startup that takes off may feel **cheated**.
- This is **not malice; it is a structural blind spot** that high-trust, high-homophily founding pairs are prone to.

### 2.3.2 How Founders Find Co-founders

#### 2.3.2.1 Two Team-Formation Strategies
- Founders choose one of two strategies, often **without realizing they are choosing**.

| Feature | Interpersonal attraction | Resource-seeking |
|---|---|---|
| Basis of choice | You **like, trust and get along** with them | You **lack something**: capital, technical skills, distribution networks, domain credibility |
| Priority order | **Relationship first, business logic second** | **Capability gap first, relationship second** |
| Typical pattern | Zepto pattern; most early founding | Conversations such as "what will you bring?" (easier with strangers than friends) |
| Strengths | Moves fast; fights less early | More likely **genuinely complementary on paper** |
| Risk | **Homophily trap**: skill overlap, blind-spot overlap | Has **not tested working together under pressure**, as relationship was never the selection criterion |
| Failure mode | Same skills and blind spots | Looks perfect on a pitch deck (one technical, one commercial, one domain) but **falls apart in year 2** because they never liked each other enough to survive a bad phase |

- Neither is always correct; they **carry different risks**.

#### 2.3.2.2 Diagnostic Question for a Prospective Joinee
- Ask (directly or indirectly): **"How did the founders actually meet, and why did they pick each other?"**
- The answer reveals which strategy, and therefore which risk profile, you are entering.

| Answer heard | What to do |
|---|---|
| Warm friendship story, **no mention of skills** each brought | **Probe gently** for the skill question yourself |
| **All skills and credentials, no warmth** | Worth noticing; relationship strength is untested |

#### 2.3.2.3 Counterexample: Razorpay
- **Harshil and Shashank**: young engineering students at **IIT Roorkee**, ran a student tech group together before Razorpay existed; same college, circle and starting conditions as Palicha and Vohra, so **high homophily on paper**.
- Yet roles **split cleanly very early**:
  - **Harshil:** strategy, external relationships, outward-facing vision.
  - **Shashank:** product and technical build.
- The split came from **different working styles and strengths**, and honesty to divide along those lines rather than both reaching for the same jobs.
- Research point: **personality and working-style diversity, even in a homogeneous pair, gives better outcomes than sameness**.
- The dangerous version of homophily is not "same college" but **"same college and we think and work identically"**: nobody covers blind spots; an **echo chamber**.

#### 2.3.2.4 Why Razorpay Pulled It Off
- The split was not discovered easily or by accident.
- Before finding its footing, both founders **were rejected by over 100 investors**.
- The company **started as a different idea** (a **crowdfunding platform**) and was abandoned after **listening hard to what early users needed**.
- **Shared adversity** plausibly forced **role clarity**: when nothing works, "doing a bit of everything" stops being a survival strategy and you must quickly work out who is better at what.

## 2.4 Founding Team Roles and External Signals

### 2.4.1 Founding Team Roles and External Signals

#### 2.4.1.1 Personality and Role Diversity
- "Opposites attract" is not precise or actionable. The useful version is **decision orientation**, not general temperament.

| Role orientation | Focus | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Outward-facing** | Partnerships, fundraising, market positioning | The story the company tells; the **company's face** |
| **Inward-facing** | Product, technical architecture, operational details | What nobody outside sees; the **company's engine** |

- This specific split **recurs in co-founding teams that work**.
- When evaluating a company to join, the question is not only "do founders get along?" but **"which of them actually owns the thing I will report to, and does that ownership look real or assumed?"**
- Same-background pairs work (Razorpay) **only when they consciously build complementary roles** on top of the shared background, usually under pressure that makes alternatives unaffordable; it **does not happen automatically**.

#### 2.4.1.2 Example: Amagi
- Founders **Bhaskar, Srividya and Srini**: very different people who met in college, saw through one successful startup and then another that got listed and is doing well.
- Different roles:
  - **Bhaskar:** CEO; talks to media and people; holds the vision.
  - **Srividya:** inside, technical, works on internal matters.
  - **Srini:** marketing and people; the market is the US, so he moved to the US to build it.

#### 2.4.1.3 Pressure on Co-founding Balance (Incubator Startup Example)
- An unnamed NSRCEL startup won a business plan competition (supported by Intel) and went to the Valley for a global challenge round; it did not win.
  - Reason: the problem was seen mainly in **emerging economies**; most listeners were developed-economy (Silicon Valley) investors.
- One listener (from Iran) saw it as a problem **everywhere** and offered money: **one founder stays and the other(s) move to the US**, with equity decided later.
- The founders declined because some had just relocated back to India for **family reasons**; this eventually **led to the split of the company**.
- Lesson: external pressures can **shake up the co-founding balance**, even for a team that "had everything".

#### 2.4.1.4 External Signals of Founding-Team Health
- A prospective joinee with no inside access can use **digital footprints** (YouTube interviews, podcasts) and interview cues.

| Signal | What to observe | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| **How co-founders talk about each other** | Do both tell the **origin story the same way**? Is the answer about the other's role **specific and generous**? | One **centres themselves** and omits the other; answers **vague or minimizing**, especially when differences emerge |
| **Role clarity** | Is there a **visible, named role split**? | "We both do everything together" / "sometimes he goes, sometimes I go" = **vague division of labor**; roles not settled |
| **Equity agreement** | Is there a **documented, vetted equity agreement** or only a **verbal understanding**? | Verbal understanding; **equal split within the first month with no vesting** |
| **Handling of disagreement** | Comfortable, open, even **gentle sparring** about disagreements | "**We never fight**" is **more concerning** than founders who openly say "we fight all the time" |
| **Treatment of early/third person** | Is an early hire or third person **talked about and respected** by founders? | Early contributor ignored or unmentioned |
| **Fit of roles to background** | Does who-does-what **match their actual backgrounds**? | Both seem to **describe the same job in different ways** (a mismatch) |

- Research data point: across tens of thousands of founders, roughly **70% of founding teams split equity within the first month, often equally, with no vesting attached**; this set-up left **Ansh Nanda exposed at KiranaKart**.
- Open disagreement signals comfort: founders who know each other long can vent in public and spar gently without damaging the relationship.
- Third-person example: **Mohandas Pai** joined the Infosys founding team after asking many interesting questions at the AGM; founders recognised his logic and brought him in **at founder level, not just executive level**. This is an indicator of how good a founding team is.

#### 2.4.1.5 Limits of the Signals
- The signals **guarantee nothing**.
- They cannot be asked directly in an interview without seeming to turn the interview around; they must be **read indirectly**.
- A joinee may knowingly accept the risk if the role builds **capability** (sales, marketing, building agents, dealing with investors) that stays useful even if the team breaks up.
  - Then the decision is a **conscious choice**; but staking on a team **for long** requires care.
  - Early in a career, there is little to lose if things blow up, so roles and skills built matter.

## 2.5 Interview with Pradeep

### 2.5.1 Interview with Pradeep

#### 2.5.1.1 Profile
- **Pradeep Varadaraja Banavara**, Head of Product at Grab; career spans a large corporation, a startup and starting again.
- Path: electronics and communication engineer (graduated 2001; Siemens offer withdrawn after the dot-com bust) -> Karnataka IT department (project scientist, EDUSAT telemedicine and distance education) -> eGovernance Foundation -> **Mapunity** (co-founded, incubated at NSRCEL) -> Yahoo -> Commonfloor -> Flipkart -> Ola -> Grab -> Swiggy -> **Obvious** (crypto wallet startup) -> Grab again.

#### 2.5.1.2 Start vs. Join: Two Mindsets
| Mindset | Question asked | Suited to |
|---|---|---|
| **"Will this work for me?"** | Outcome-oriented, evaluates fit | A **job** is the usual fit; startup success is hard to predict |
| **"How can I make this work for me?"** | Builds something into existence that did not exist before | **Startup** |

- Start-versus-join is a **moot point**: it depends on the scenario and the outcome wanted.
- Start-and-join also exists **inside a large company** (he changed three teams in Yahoo).

#### 2.5.1.3 Mapunity: Forming a Founding Team
- Idea: **information can drive better choices**; give people detailed traffic information in pre-smartphone Bangalore.
  - Co-founder Ashwin proposed using **cell-tower connections as a proxy for traffic**; first iteration showed traffic **at a node** (e.g., Silk Board), not along roads.
  - Delivered via **SMS short code 555**, traffic-police and BMTC integrations, bus arrival ETA systems and LED boards.
- Team formation: the **co-founders chose him**; equity was a straightforward three-way split (about **33/33/34**, with Ashwin 1% higher); no one thought deeply about what equity meant at the start.
- **Roles** were complementary:
  - **Ashwin:** leader who dealt with governments and got things done.
  - **Pradeep:** project manager / coordinator.
  - **Pandian:** "deep geek" who built the system solutions.
- He **worked a full-time job in the mornings and on Mapunity in the evenings** for about 6 months (expecting first child, no startup funding), then joined full-time after the first cheque at a **50% salary cut**.
- Purpose: called itself a **social lab** building technology for social good.

#### 2.5.1.4 Evaluating Co-founders and Aligning Ambitions
- In later startups he asked co-founders **3 or 4 high-level questions**:
  - **What are you in this for?**
  - **How long will you be here / when do you plan to exit?**
  - **What is the ideal outcome for you?**
  - **What does the vision for your wildest success look like?**
- Purpose: understand each co-founder's journey, aspirations and ambitions, and **how deep they are in it and how hard they will push**.
- Visions vary widely: build a unicorn, change an industry, build a meaningful business with good returns, or build a clever solution. A short-term exit is also valid.
- You look for **people who complement you** (e.g., in a later startup: product person, architect owning engineering, and a business/operations person from a business family; all had met at Ola).
- Need to be **clear about your own journey and the journey others are after** so you can plan.

#### 2.5.1.5 Why Co-founders Part Ways
- Leaving Mapunity: two causes:
  - **Dissonance** between co-founders' differing ideas and the stated objective (Pandian, his technical "bedrock", left first).
  - **Economic pressure** at home after taking a 50% pay cut, and a job offer from Yahoo after winning hackathons.
- Co-founders sense warning signs but **nobody wants to "bell the cat"**; they talk in roundabout ways.
  - The guilt: putting the startup ahead of oneself is the default feeling; voicing your own feelings feels like **putting yourself ahead of the startup**, though sometimes necessary.
- **Ambition mismatch** is dangerous: one wants a unicorn, one wants to shut down, one wants "one more fundraise".

#### 2.5.1.6 Founder vs. Employee Responsibilities
| Dimension | As founder |
|---|---|
| **Skin in the game** | Higher; raising money and responsible for the whole team |
| **Team dependence** | Team depends on you for paychecks (Obvious needed a massive pay cut; team stood by while an M&A deal was pending) |
| **Face of the company** | You make promises to investors |
| **Fiduciary duty** | Do right by investors: constant updates, ask and share feedback, keep them informed about pivots |

#### 2.5.1.7 Timeline of Startup Signals
- A **gut feel** whether it will work emerges within about **12 to 18 months**.
- Corrective actions possible: **pivot, raise more funds, bring in different people, change markets**.
- By the **18 to 24 month mark** you know for sure whether you are in it.
- **Biggest pitfall:** neither succeeding massively nor failing, stuck in between (suits only those building a lifestyle business).
- Needing "**one more fundraise**" repeatedly signals **too many eggs in one basket**.
- Rationality is hard because the startup is "your baby".

#### 2.5.1.8 Career Moves and the North Star
- **North star:** "be around **interesting people doing interesting things**"; **journey is the reward** (versus colleagues who aim at a destination such as VP or CEO). What is "interesting" changes by season (cloud, mobile, on-demand delivery, AI).
- He followed people into Yahoo (strong engineers), Flipkart, Ola, Grab; self-described **"irrational, impatient optimist"**.
- Recurring pattern: roughly **6-month stints before jumping** to the next big move, and about **2 years** when settled.
- Taking a **role cut** at Flipkart (joined as product manager though previously senior) paid off: promoted to senior product manager in about 7 months; Flipkart announced a **$1 billion round** a week after his offer and **honoured the dollar value** of his equity.
- Moves: individual contributor -> manager, and platform -> consumer roles (Ola).
- **Live the life of the consumer your product serves**: he drove for Ola (and Uber) to understand drivers, then built **Ola Suvidha**, cutting driver onboarding from half a day to **5 minutes** using digital APIs (PAN, licence, Aadhaar, bank account).
- **Niche but essential problems** have less competition, and doing them well makes you **part of the fabric** other companies operate on.
- Moving to Obvious: midlife questioning ("what am I doing with my life?"), wish to build from scratch, crypto as a "rebel" and open-ended space; quit Swiggy 6 months in, but this time **funding interest existed before resigning** (maturity vs. earlier jumps).

#### 2.5.1.9 Calculated Bets
- Obvious was a **calculated bet with layered fallbacks**:

| Level of bet | Fallback |
|---|---|
| **Domain: Fintech** | Join a Fintech firm (e.g., PhonePe, Paytm, Groww) |
| **Industry: crypto** | Work in jurisdictions favouring crypto (e.g., Dubai) or with US/Hong Kong companies |
| **Product: wallet** | Work with chains that fund |
| **Self** | Confidence in oneself |
| (Also **society** per his framing) | Overall societal acceptance |

- For failure, **all levels must fail**; so risk is lower than it appears.
- Outcome: wallet failed ("everyone needs one but nobody pays for it"), crypto failed in India, and an acquisition (multi-million dollar term sheet) collapsed when the acquirer was **hacked three days before closing**.
- Book **"Thinking in Bets"** (Annie Duke): suited to the "will this work for me?" mindset; covers **loss aversion** and overweighting things that seemed to work without knowing why (like a **winner's luck streak** when odds are still 50/50).
- Book **"Shoe Dog"** (Phil Knight): suited to "how can I make this work for me?"; **journey versus destination**: Nike's founder cared about running and giving good shoes, not building a billion-dollar business.
- Journey people are accommodated if it fails; destination people ("the next Ambani") face short-lived taunts, though dreaming big is respected.
- Book **"Quit"** and **escalation of commitment** are mentioned in the closing exchange (when to quit).

#### 2.5.1.10 Handling Failure and Uncertainty
- **Plan for the journey**: outcomes are not in your control, inputs are.
- **Types of failure:** economic (recoverable), status/social (recoverable), and **feeling left alone** (the real failure: not having built linkages).
- **Affordable loss:** his wife's standard formula: **"give me 18 months of money, then do whatever you want"**; an unused **education loan** acts as insurance for continuity.
- Adversity is a given; do not give it too much weight; **no regrets**.
- Society now says **"you have not failed, you have learned"**; investors told him to come back when he starts again.
- Success depends on **timing, not only effort**; avoid **survivorship bias** (millions of failed startups that did the same thing are invisible).
- Support systems: **network, co-founders, friends, family**.

#### 2.5.1.11 Network, Asking and Serendipity
- **"Your network is your net worth"**; most people **underestimate their social ability**; people want to help, so **ask**.
- Fundraising observation: asking for **feedback** often brought money; saying "I am raising money" brought only feedback.
- Archetypes for people from smaller towns:
  1. **Declare "I belong here"** regardless of others' views.
  2. **Ask for help constantly** from a small circle (introductions, advice); over-communicate.
  3. **Silent sufferers** who take time to come out of their shell, build a small circle, then let their work talk.
- **Initiative matters**: he flew to Singapore on a travel visa before interviews, got the offer in three days (large companies typically take 1 to 1.5 months); a Titan MD case where wanting the job openly helped.
- **Luck and serendipity** play a large role (chance meetings leading to funding).
- **Two insights and a proposal:** carry two genuine domain insights (e.g., a surprising industry statistic) to create **surface area for serendipity**, and a proposal ready (funding, co-founder, engineer, sales) if someone bites.

## Quick Revision Summary

### Key Definitions
- **Homophily:** tendency to bond with similar people (same college, city, job, language); optimizes comfort, not complementary skill.
- **Trust:** belief the other acts in good faith and will not cheat; feeling safe to be vulnerable.
- **Complementarity:** the other brings skills, networks and judgment you lack, so together you cover more ground.
- **Interpersonal attraction strategy:** pick a co-founder you like and trust; relationship first, business logic second.
- **Resource-seeking strategy:** pick a co-founder for a gap (capital, tech, distribution, domain credibility); capability first, relationship second.
- **Structural blind spot:** conflict that emerges by default from structure (no spelled-out roles or equity), not from bad intentions.
- **Decision orientation:** outward-facing (partnerships, fundraising, positioning) vs. inward-facing (product, architecture, operations) split between founders.
- **Affordable loss:** a pre-agreed amount of money or runway (e.g., 18 months) you can lose without disaster.

### Important Formulas / Numbers
- No formulas in this module.
- **20%**: verbal equity promised to Ansh Nanda (conditional, never received).
- **About 70%**: founding teams that split equity within the first month, often equally and with no vesting.
- **100+**: investors who rejected Razorpay's founders; first idea was a crowdfunding platform.
- **12 to 18 months**: when the gut feel of whether a startup works appears; **18 to 24 months**: when you know for sure.
- Practical test: **Would I choose this person for this problem if I did not already trust them?**

### Must-Remember Points
- Team-founded startups beat solo on survival and scale on average, but **a bad team is worse than a good solo founder**; the team premium appears only when the team functions.
- Teams of **friends have higher turnover than teams of strangers**; long familiarity should lower confidence unless roles and conflict decision rights are discussed.
- **High trust does not imply complementarity**; the two can exist independently.
- Zepto/KiranaKart: informal verbal equity promises are fragile; the third person brought in for a skill is exposed.
- Razorpay: same-background pair succeeded through **clean role split (strategy/external vs. product/tech)**, forced by shared adversity.
- Interpersonal teams risk **homophily trap**; resource-seeking teams risk **untested ability to work under pressure**.
- External signals: same origin story, generous and specific talk about each other, named role split, documented equity, open handling of disagreement ("we never fight" is a warning), respect for early third person, roles matching background.
- Signals guarantee nothing and must be read indirectly; accept the risk consciously, for example when the role builds lasting capability.
- Pradeep's lessons: align on motivation, exit horizon and wildest-success vision before starting; layered fallback bets; journey vs. destination; network and asking for help; two insights and a proposal.

## 3.2 Reading a Startup as a Joinee

### 3.2.1 Is the startup healthy from the inside?

#### Key Concept: Health of a Startup
- **Health** here means: is it a place worth joining, **not** venture performance or financial performance
- Assessed on three dimensions: **relationships, roles, rewards**
- These must be discussed with founders early, and founders must discuss their own positions with each other
- Employees must go and ask the founders about them

#### Case: Zepto (Two True Stories)

| Aspect | Story 1: Healthy on paper | Story 2: Culture concerns |
|--------|---------------------------|---------------------------|
| Source | Publicly documented | Publicly documented (Reddit post claiming to be from a former employee) |
| Equity | Earlier founder-equity dispute resolved by a **documented settlement** | - |
| Roles | One co-founder runs **strategy and the outward face**; the other runs **product** | - |
| Outcome | Role split held for years; clearer than before | Culture built around the **CEO's own schedule** |
| Work hours | - | Meetings starting at **2:00 AM** (CEO does not wake early); long working hours |
| Talent | - | Claim that **experienced professionals avoid** the company, so staff are mostly early-career |
| Founder response | - | Did not deny meeting times; admitted **manipulative dark patterns** in the app (said since removed) |

#### Key Concept: One-Dimension Diagnostic Trap
- A student evaluating from outside usually meets **only one** of the stories, depending on which article or video is seen first
- This happens whenever a diagnostic measures **only one dimension** and treats it as the whole answer
- **Healthy bonding team on paper does NOT equal healthy culture**

#### What an Outside Evaluation Must Cover
- How **dimensions actually get made** once the founder has real power
- Signals **nobody puts in a pitch deck**
- Whether the environment will **teach something** (even if hard)
- What the candidate is **offered** and what the candidate is **giving up**

### 3.2.2 Evaluating a startup from the outside

#### Key Concept: Joinee versus Co-founder

| Aspect | Co-founder | Joinee |
|--------|-----------|--------|
| Decision | Who to build a company with | Whether to hand **2-3 years of career** to people who have already made the decisions |
| Roles and equity | Negotiated **before day one** | Mostly **inherits** existing structure |
| Information | Inside access | Only a **handful of interviews** plus public information |
| Skill needed | Negotiation | Reading a healthy team **accurately from outside with less access** |

#### Actionable Checks for a Joinee
- Three actionable lessons, each involving search, verification, specific questions and negotiating specific terms:
  - **Reading attrition**
  - **Reading equity documentation**
  - **Reading the POSH committee**
- Investor profile (is it invested? broad composition? who are the investors?) is worth knowing
- A **first-time joinee has no levers** over investors, but should know what they are getting into

#### Key Concept: Founder Mode versus Manager Mode
- **Conventional management advice:** once the company scales, the founder's job is to **hire good people (managers) and get out of the way**
- This mirrors the shift from **effectual** (early stage) to **causal** (scaling stage)
- **Founder mode argument:** founders who **delegate too early** and trust an org chart they cannot see into watch the company **drift from its mission and vision**
- **Brian Chesky example:** took some decision-making rights back when the company was drifting; this made the company **healthier, not worse**
- **Critics' view:** founder mode is a fancier name for **micromanagement**
- Debate is heated: some read it as permission to control everything; others as a narrow point about one stage that gets over-applied; **both sides are partly right**

#### Why Outside Candidates Must Tell Them Apart
- Labels do not reveal behaviour
- **Nobody describes themselves as a micromanager**; many founders describe themselves as being in founder mode
- Must judge **actual behaviour**, not the self-label

## 3.3 Founder Mode and Disagreement

### 3.3.1 Founder mode: does disagreement survive?

#### Key Concept: Healthy versus Dysfunctional Founder Mode

| Feature | Healthy founder mode | Dysfunctional founder mode |
|---------|---------------------|----------------------------|
| Questioning | Asks questions and pushes back hard | Asks as many hard questions |
| **Target** | The **decision** | The **person** defending it |
| After a lost argument | People leave having **heard something** | Everyone learns to **stop bringing bad news** |
| Dissent | Can tell founder they are wrong and **still have a job next morning** | Dissenter unwelcome or gets a poor response |
| Attrition | Normal | **Goes up** |

#### Case: Ola versus Airbnb

| Aspect | Ola | Airbnb |
|--------|-----|--------|
| Founder involvement | Founder personally involved in decisions at **multiple levels** | Founder took ownership of many decisions on seeing the company drift |
| Reported signals | About **13 senior executive departures over 5 years**; sometimes **45% senior turnover in one year**; a hired CEO left in **4 months** | Company turned around |
| Pattern | **Revolving door** | Healthier company |

#### Key Concept: Diagnostic Question
- **Core question:** What happened the last time somebody told this founder they were making a mistake?
- Can be asked in an interview: "Do you allow people questioning you?"

| Founder's answer | What it signals |
|------------------|-----------------|
| **Specific story**, including a time they were wrong and changed course | Disagreement has survived confrontation |
| **General philosophy** about an open door, welcoming criticism, no specifics | Possibly **no specifics exist**; vague, undocumented claim shows how power is used day to day |

## 3.4 Attrition and Culture Signals

### 3.4.1 Using attrition to investigate a startup

#### Key Concept: Why Attrition Matters
- Dysfunctional founder behaviour **permeates the org culture**
- Someone shouted at by the founder who cannot shout back may shout at a junior
- A founder can have built a very large successful business and still run a difficult culture; attrition shows **what kind of company** is being joined

#### How to Read Attrition

| Step | What to check |
|------|---------------|
| 1. Source | **LinkedIn**: how many joined, how many left |
| 2. Window | Only **a couple of years** is enough |
| 3. Tenure | How many **stayed long** |
| 4. Destination | Where leavers went |
| 5. Follow-up | Talk to leavers at other companies |

#### Interpreting Destinations and Tenure

| Observation | Reading |
|-------------|---------|
| Young people left for **education** | **Natural progression** |
| Left to join another company | Check the person; consider talking to them |
| People leaving in **under a year** | **Red flag** |
| Founder comments on a past employee's post | Separation probably **not bad** |

#### Limits and Supplementary Checks
- Reading depends on **company size**: with very few people (e.g., 6 versus about 30) patterns are hard to read
- Check the founder's **social media activity**: what they say and support
- Social media is a make-believe world, but **patterns emerge if you dig deep**
- Compare with **other companies in the industry** using the large amount of available data

### 3.4.2 Evaluating startup culture as a woman

#### Key Concept: What Structure Reveals
- Company structure indicates how a **woman's complaint and career** would be handled once inside

#### POSH Committee

| Point | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| Law | The **POSH Act** requires every employer with **10 or more employees** to have an **internal complaints committee** |
| Beyond the law | Is there an **outside person** (mentor, advisor, company member) one can talk to? |
| What to check | Website listing of the committee; how the company responds when asked; **who the committee members are** |
| What it signals | **Seriousness**; the founder wants a healthy, safe environment for creating value |
| Caution | Having a committee does **NOT** prove a great company; not having one does **NOT** prove a bad company |

#### Culture Cues

| Cue | Reading |
|-----|---------|
| Toxic, masculine, geeky startup culture (**Uber** cited) | Women may tolerate and get used to it because the company is good, at the cost of mental equanimity |
| Meetings at **2:00 AM**, late-night parties | Possible indicators only; a company can have a partying culture and still be respectful and healthy |
| Burden when someone falls ill | Often **falls on women**; society expects differently of men and women |
| **Woman co-founder or leadership member** | Usually **much better** signal; can be asked how things are |
| **Low attrition but only women leaving** | Warning cue |
| Stress | Startup stress can be motivating (dopamine from hard problems), but **not equally comfortable for everyone** |

#### Due Diligence Action
- **Ask another woman** who is in or has worked at the company about the culture
- Do some due diligence and be cautious before stepping in

## 3.5 Learning Environment and Candidate Responsibility

### 3.5.1 Psychological safety and choosing where to learn

#### Definition: Psychological Safety
- **Psychological safety:** feeling comfortable to express views and raise problems; others feel the same; the discussion is about **views, not people**
- **Google's internal research:** psychological safety was the **strongest predictor of high-performing teams**, ahead of who was in those teams

#### Framework: Edmondson's Two-Axis Model
- Axis 1: **How safe people feel raising a problem** (psychological safety)
- Axis 2: **How much is actually expected of them** (expectations)

| | Low expectation | High expectation |
|---|----------------|------------------|
| **Low safety** | **Empathy zone** (as named in lecture): nobody is pushed, nobody cares | **Anxiety zone**: real pressure, people **hide mistakes**, nothing is learned |
| **High safety** | **Comfort zone**: pleasant, nothing happens | **Learning zone**: intensity converts into **skill** |

#### Key Concept: Same Intensity, Opposite Value
- A founder-driven, hard-charging startup may be:
  - **Anxiety zone:** all pressure, no safety; teaches only how to survive stress; blame when things go wrong (even outside control)
  - **Learning zone:** real accountability; mistakes are named and worked through, not buried
- Both look alike from outside (high activity)
- Learning-zone teams leave people **rejuvenated** at day end and snowball into startup progress
- Founder in **healthy founder mode** (involved in the decision, not the person) is more likely to run a learning zone because the involvement comes from **fast, honest feedback**
- Ola cited as having many teams in the **anxiety zone**

#### Cues to Identify the Learning Zone
- How does the founder talk about a **past failure** (interview or podcast)?
- Do **early employees who left** describe specific things they learned?

#### Key Concept: What to Weigh at Early Career Stage
- **Rate of learning matters more than pay or title**
- Pay (LPA) is a poor way to evaluate a company; at this stage, money matters least ("**time billionaire**")
- Starting small or large does not determine the outcome (a large firm start does not guarantee becoming CEO or partner; a small startup start does not limit a career)
- What decides outcomes: speed of learning, interaction and progress

| Factor to examine | Reason |
|-------------------|--------|
| **Scope of work** | Determines learning |
| **People you will work with** | Learning comes from them |
| **Your manager** | Great founder + poor manager = little learning; poor founder + great manager = still a lot of learning |
| **Manager's reputation and past employers** | Check what people say about the manager |

#### Comparison: Same Job Title, Different Learning

| Aspect | Large company (Product Analyst) | Five-person startup (Product Analyst) |
|--------|--------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| Ownership | Owns **one metric on one team** | Owns the **whole onboarding** |
| Decisions | Waits for decision through **three layers of approval** | **Makes the call** |
| Feedback | Slow | Learns within **a week** whether it worked |
| Process | Coordination and bureaucracy **standardised**; no one indispensable, replaceable by a similar person | High individual impact |

#### Key Concept: Delta Add
- Ask: what is the **delta** you add to a large firm versus to a small startup, what you can push the company to, own and eventually create
- Parents may favour large corporates for "experience"; a large company does **not** necessarily give more learning
- Long career ahead (about 40 years) in a changing world (AI): personality, ability to talk to people and ability to get things done matter more; **knowledge is on tap**

### 3.5.2 Your responsibility as a candidate

#### Key Concept: Evaluation Goes Both Ways
- Candidates can also be the ones **misleading** the startup (founders may be naive, first-time)
- Honesty about **intentions, commitments and plans** builds trust

#### Examples of Dishonesty

| Situation | Issue |
|-----------|-------|
| Saying "I intend to stay three years" while having applied for a master's programme abroad | Not fair |
| Joining while building something on your own with a friend | Not true; should say upfront (e.g., a different domain, done in spare time) |
| Leaving after four months when it was known from the start | Not right |
| Being lax with truth and commitment | Not acceptable |

#### Consequences
- **Disrepute** to the individual and to the programme
- Companies may stop hiring from the programme after a bad experience
- Repercussions follow when a promise is broken

#### Principles
- **Do not withhold information** you are sure about; do not lead founders on
- Be honest about: building something of your own, likely other offers, plans to go abroad
- Reciprocity: candidates ask founders for honest answers (Did you have a fight? How did it go? Why did people leave? How are disagreements dealt with?); questions asked **of candidates** must be answered honestly too
- Apply the **relationships, roles, rewards** diagnostic from outside; read **attrition, culture and learning environment** as distinct signals; ask whether you are being as truthful as the founder

## 3.6 Founders' Interview: MyCaptain

### 3.6.1 Interview with the founders of MyCaptain

#### Key Concept: Origin of the Idea
- Both founders grew up on the internet and watched startups scale; had an internal drive to build online
- **Trigger:** three friends in a hostel room the night before a fluid dynamics exam, each wanting a different career (astronomer, economist, journalist) yet all stuck in **mechanical engineering**
- **Problem identified:** something is wrong with how careers are chosen in India
- **Mission:** change how Indians choose careers
- Problem resonated personally with co-founder in another city who saw the same thing in his college

#### Journey: Problem-Solution Fit and Pivots

| Stage | What happened |
|-------|---------------|
| **First solution: magazine** | Print magazine for school students; virtual team of **50-60** people (writers, illustrators, designers, logistics) across cities coordinated on **WhatsApp groups**; funded by **a loan from a parent that had to be repaid** |
| Mistake | Printed **500 copies before speaking to any school or student**; did not check whether kids read magazines |
| Result | About **1.5 years**, only about **123-124 subscribers** |
| **Pivot 1: events** | Small events and workshops in schools and colleges to get audience; sponsorships; thousands of students across cities; message resonated even though the magazine did not |
| Scalability question | Led to incubation at an incubator |
| Business plan competition | Won as the **youngest winners**; Rs 10 lakh prize **declined** because it came with incubation requiring **5% equity** |
| **Pivot 2: MyCaptain** | Online cohort-based live courses; early traction (about Rs 4-5 lakh a month while in college) |
| Bootstrapping | Sustained a **bootstrapped DNA for 5-6 years** |

- Lean principle stated: **if the first solution is perfect, you waited too long**

#### Co-founder Commitment

| Point | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| Ask | "I cannot guarantee even a small salary after graduation. Are you in or out?" |
| Condition | **Must not sit for placements** (applied to all full-time joiners in final year) |
| Test of commitment | Willingness to sacrifice placements |
| Person who did not join | Offered only **one year** against founders' plan of **5-8 years**; contribution was significant but commitment lacking; remained friends |

#### Equity Framework

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Factors | **Whose idea**, **contribution**, **when they joined**, **commitment (years)**, **relevant skill set** |
| Method | **Points** per factor; **weighted average**; small negotiation |
| Negotiation style | Fair asks; no hardball |
| Decision-making | The founder whose idea it was set the **direction (CEO type)**; other responsibilities split by capacity |
| Early hires | **All equity-based** (little cash); 4 core co-founders initially |
| Common public norm | First two co-founders split **equally or within 10-20 points**; third, fourth, fifth co-founders usually **under 15%** (sub-10 to sub-15%) |
| Dilution | Diluted in proportion to the ratio |
| Risk | **Co-founder conflict**, including equity division, is a primary reason early startups fail; similar issues arose later with early employees |

#### Early Traction and Hiring

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| Early signs | Problem-solution fit and very early product-market fit (2015-16, while in final year) |
| MVP | Lessons delivered via **WhatsApp groups and Google Drive**; first about **2,000 paying customers** served without own tech |
| First hires | **Sales team** (10-15 hires after the 4-5 core group); **cash is king** |
| Funding logic | **5 sales hires can fund 1 ops hire** |
| Basis | A **proven, scalable sales model** (not blind hiring) |
| Recruitment | Campus **freshers**; first interview question: "What did you want to do? Why are you here?" |
| Why alignment | Could not match competitors' salaries (a rival offered about **5 times** more); aligned people to **vision, mission, purpose**; hired those who **faced the same problem** |

#### Bootstrapping Rationale

| Reason | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| Edtech not yet popular | Only two large players at that time |
| Founders from non-premier institutions | Fundraising difficult |
| Model not seen as scalable | Cohort-based live learning |
| Investor appetite | Money went to e-commerce, marketplaces; **ops-heavy** businesses unfashionable (2015-16) |
| Outcome | **Unit-economics profitable**; sustained 2-3 years without external capital; approached investors at about **USD 1 million revenue run rate** (about 3 years after graduation) |

#### Product and Pricing
- Courses priced about **Rs 1,000**; benchmark was offline one-day workshops (8-16 hours) at the same price
- Offered month-long learning with a professional, more hours and better engagement
- Courses in areas such as **psychology, photography, writing, entrepreneurship, astronomy**, not academic exam prep
- **Horizontal expansion** of scope

#### Culture Building

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| Core | **Purpose and vision**; vision statement visible from every corner of the office |
| Mission milestone | Each year about 1 million people write an engineering entrance exam; goal: **1 million people on the platform pursuing a career they love** (first target 50,000 to 1 lakh) |
| Peak (bootstrapped) | **2.5 lakh** learners |
| Peak team | About **375 people**, of which about **250 in sales** (two product lines) |
| Where culture challenges came | **Revenue functions** (target-driven, high pressure), not specialised groups |
| Managers | Young (22-25); absorbed culture from founders and built teams around it |
| Culture shift | When **early employees who believed in the culture left** and new hires joined; later venture funding also changed culture |
| Internal talk | Conversations were about the mission, not funding rounds (rounds came later) |

#### Venture Capital and Exit

| Stage | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| Why raise | **2021-22**: every edtech raised funds; COVID validated online cohort learning; competitors entering; positioning was critical |
| Boom funding | First pre-series A (about USD 3 million) took about **2 months**, many commitments on phone calls |
| Bust | Later bridge round (about USD 2 million) took **1.5-2 years**, in small tranches (Rs 2-3 crore) |
| Signal | No funding for foreseeable years |
| Options | Hold on up to 5 years to reach profitability and about Rs 100 crore revenue for an **SME IPO**, or **sell** |
| Decision | **Sold** (acquisition offers had come every 3-6 months; three term sheets); sector was consolidating; business seen as Rs 100-200 crore, not Rs 800-1000 crore |
| M&A experience | Equity conversations **"brutal"**; very **humbling** |
| Acquirer | **Inorganically built**; 50-60% of senior leadership were ex-founders; culture determined by function leader and stage of function |
| Post-exit | One founder had a year of **vesting** left; the other moved on to a next venture |

#### Founders' Changed Perspective

| Theme | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| Mindset | Shift from **missionary** (earlier about 90/10) towards more **mercenary** (one at 50/50; the other about 60/40 missionary) |
| Reason | Investors expect capital to be **returned**; "no matter how impact-oriented a VC is, it is still a VC" |
| Opportunity filter | **High leverage** and exciting ideas; TAM as second or third filter, not first |
| Outcome view | An outcome need not be an IPO; businesses can be built to a scale and exited |
| Equity/capital | Keep **capital as a driver of the mission**, not let funding-round talk dominate team conversations |
| Culture | Product team must come from **strong purpose and ownership** |
| Early program hires | Preferred people from **education non-profits** (e.g., fellowship programmes), not from edtech |

#### Advice on Joining a Startup

| Question | Advice |
|----------|--------|
| When to join | **As early as possible** |
| Which startup | One with **early traction and early product-market fit** (avoids early mess) |
| Extreme-ownership personalities | Can join even **before traction** and shape it |
| Joining as a founder | Missed the boat once the startup is established |
| Founder test | Are founders **missionaries or mercenaries**? |
| Missionaries | Stick through **1-3 years**; a venture takes about **1,000 days** to figure things out |
| Mercenaries | Main motive is money; leave if **no traction in 6-12 months** |
| How to find missionaries | Have conversations, spend time, **intern** with them |
| Mistakes | Expect to get it wrong; by the third time a gut feel develops; startups are about mistakes |
| Geography | Mix of missionaries and mercenaries differs across the country |
| Future founders | Work at a startup **in the target industry** first for nuance and head start |
| Fit | Not every startup is equally good; if learning stops, move to another |
| Starting | Do not overplan; **early traction matters more than pedigree** |

- Honesty is two-way: disclose outside plans, other offers, plans to go abroad
- MyCaptain: magazine failed (about 124 subscribers) then events then online courses; bootstrapped; sold after funding bust
- Typical co-founder equity: first two near-equal; later co-founders under 15%
- Join early-stage with traction; venture takes about 1,000 days

# Module 4: Making the Call on a Startup Offer

## 4.1.1 Welcome to the Module

### 4.1.1.1 Purpose of the Module

- Brings together all earlier learning to make an **informed decision about joining a startup**
- Evaluate four external things: **founder, founding team, product, stage of the company**
- Also evaluate three personal things: **own goals, needs, ability to handle risk**

#### Key Concept: The Core Question

- Not only: **"Is this a good startup?"**
- But: **"Is this the right opportunity for me?"**
- Outcome of the module: a **practical framework** for any startup offer

### 4.1.1.2 Typical Decision Scenario

- An offer from a startup that is liked; the founder is sharp and the product is interesting
- Salary is slightly **lower** than a friend's offer from a large firm
- **ESOPs** are offered; a reply deadline is fixed
- Conflicting advice: parents ask why a safe option is being turned down; friends say startups are the future
- Underlying question: **how to know whether this is a good decision**

---

## 4.2.1 Making the Call: The Five-Question Framework

### 4.2.1.1 Overview of the Joinee Framework

- A **5-question checklist** for any startup offer
- Also useful for a prospective founder: shows **how a good candidate will judge the founder**
- **Order of questions:** first the **people**, then the **product**, then the **company**, then **you**

| # | Question | Focus |
|---|----------|-------|
| 1 | Who is this founder and what are they optimizing for? | People |
| 2 | Is the founding team in equilibrium? | People |
| 3 | Is this startup building something real? | Product |
| 4 | What stage is this company at, and does that match what I need? | Company (timing) |
| 5 | Can I survive the worst case? | You |

- **Question 5 is the most important question**

### 4.2.1.2 Question 1: Who Is the Founder and What Are They Optimizing For?

- Reads the founder's motivation using the **rich versus king** lens, seen from outside

| Founder type | Likely behaviour |
|--------------|------------------|
| **Wealth-focused (rich)** | Hires experienced leaders; gives up control if it helps the company grow |
| **Control-focused (king)** | Keeps decisions close; may avoid hiring people stronger than themselves |

- Both types can build good companies, but they create **very different workplaces**
- The joinee should know **which type** they are joining

### 4.2.1.3 Question 2: Is the Founding Team in Equilibrium?

- Uses the **3 Rs (relationships, roles, rewards)** as a check
- Ask about **equity and roles**; ask **who decides what**

| Team health | Sign |
|-------------|------|
| **Healthy team** | Answers these questions easily |
| **Team with problems** | Avoids the questions; the **two founders give different answers** |

### 4.2.1.4 Nature of Questions 3, 4 and 5

| Question | Concerns |
|----------|----------|
| Question 3 | The **product** |
| Question 4 | **Timing** (stage) |
| Question 5 | What happens to **you** if things go wrong |

### 4.2.1.5 How to Use the Framework

#### Definition: Heuristic

- The framework is a **simple heuristic / thumb rule**, **NOT a scoring system**
- No points to maximize; offers are **not** to be rated and ranked by a total number
- It helps look at the decision **from every side**

#### Key Points

- A startup can **fail one question and still be right** for the candidate
- The **final decision maker is the candidate**
- Not every box needs to be ticked; if something about the opportunity "gels", it can be chosen
- The aim is to decide **on criteria, not "flying blind"**
- Know **which question the startup fails** and judge whether that is an **acceptable risk**

---

## 4.2.2 Is the Startup Building Something Real?

### 4.2.2.1 Question 3 in Context

- A startup can look impressive from outside, but the issue is whether **customers get meaningful value**
- Look beyond **growth numbers, discounts and marketing**
- Test: is the startup **solving a real problem** and creating something customers genuinely want

### 4.2.2.2 Delta 4 Theory

#### Definition: Delta 4

- Idea from **Kunal Shah** (studied philosophy; built Freecharge; studied consumer behaviour before founding CRED)
- A test of whether a product is real
- When a new way beats the old way by **4 points or more** (on a 0 to 10 efficiency score), people **stop going back**
- The change in behaviour becomes **irreversible**

#### Steps of the Test

1. Take any way of solving a problem
2. Give it an **efficiency score from 0 to 10**
3. Score the new solution on the same scale
4. If the new score exceeds the old by **at least 4 points**, the behaviour change is irreversible

#### Examples

| Example | Old way | New way / Observation |
|---------|---------|-----------------------|
| **UPI** | Cash, right change, "will pay tomorrow" | Scan and pay; now ubiquitous, street vendors and autorickshaw drivers adopted it rapidly |
| **IRCTC** | Go to the station and stand in a queue | Even a poorly functioning online site (payment failures, uncertainty about ticket) retained users because the alternative was worse |
| **RedBus** | Going to an agent to buy a ticket | Users told others to use it |
| **Flipkart** | Travelling through traffic to a book store | Order from home |

#### Second Marker: Brag-Worthy Proposition

- A Delta 4 product makes people **want to tell others** (word of mouth)
- Kunal Shah calls this a **unique brag-worthy proposition**
- Using the product makes people feel **smart**
- Sign: users say "I don't do this the old way anymore"

### 4.2.2.3 Why It Matters When Deciding to Join

- A startup with only a **small improvement** can still raise money, grow for a while and hire employees
- It can **buy growth with discounts and marketing**
- When money slows, customers **return to old habits**
- In that situation the **newest employees are often the first to be let go**

### 4.2.2.4 Applying Delta 4 from Outside: Three Questions

| # | Question to ask | What it reveals |
|---|-----------------|-----------------|
| 1 | What happens to users if the company **shuts down next month**? How much will it hurt? Would they simply switch to a competitor? | If they would switch, the product is **not yet reversible** |
| 2 | Will customers **stay without being paid to stay**? What happens when discounts go away and customers pay the actual cost? | Whether demand is genuine rather than bought |
| 3 | Does the founder know and share the numbers, or use only **vanity metrics**? | Founder's understanding of retention |

- A founder may admit "customers would go to a competitor now, but the next version will make it hard to leave"; such **frankness deserves weight**
- A good founder knows these numbers, or at least knows **why they do what they do**, and shares them easily

#### Definition: Vanity Metrics

- Numbers such as **downloads and sales volume**
- Show **growth** but say little about whether customers will **stay**

### 4.2.2.5 Checking Customer Sentiment

- Read **reviews**, visit **app stores**, look at comments
- Are people **praising** or **simply using**?
- Observe **how founders respond** to bad or late comments
- A founder who responds honestly ("this is how it is now, we are building a better product, here is what we will do") shows **responsibility** and **honesty**
- No product is without downsides; the **response** is the signal

### 4.2.2.6 Very Young Startups with No Proof Yet

- Ask the same questions anyway; founders still have a **stance and understanding**
- Check how well they know the **lay of the land**, their own **expertise and competence**, and how **open** they are
- Honest statements (e.g., "we are not good yet, we are copying, but it will change"; "that is why we are calling you in") signal **trustworthiness**
- Look for this **trustworthiness**

## 4.3.1 What Stage Is the Company At?

### 4.3.1.1 Two Parts of Question 4

| Part | Content |
|------|---------|
| **Part 1** | What stage is the company at? (most people ask only this) |
| **Part 2** | What do **I** need from the **next 2 years**? (few people ask this) |

### 4.3.1.2 Definition: Product Market Fit (PMF)

- A company reaches **PMF** when a **clear group of customers wants its product badly**
- Test: bring the **marketing budget to zero**; will people still want the product?
- Under PMF, **demand pulls the company**; before PMF, the company has to **push the product**
- Examples cited: RedBus had no marketing budget when it started; Apple is said not to have advertised the first few iPhones

### 4.3.1.3 The Three Stages

#### Pre-PMF

- Company is still **searching**; product changes every few weeks
- Team of a few people; founders take part in discussions
- Joinee does **many jobs** (customer calls, data study, pitch deck help)
- Very little organizational structure and **no formal mentoring**
- **Highest chance of failure**, but the **greatest crucible of learning**
- Equity promised pays off **only if the company succeeds**; success chance is low, but if it succeeds the upside can be very large

#### Post-PMF

- Company has found something that works and is **building a way to deliver it again and again** (repeat, "autopilot")
- Sets up **processes for sales** and a **playbook for operations**
- Moves from **effectual to causal** and from **founder mode to manager mode**
- Hires people likely to be **managers**; processes and bureaucracy begin
- Roles carry **KPIs**; **turfs** are drawn; roles and responsibilities become more distinct
- **Series A** is described as perhaps the best stage: it shows an investor is betting on the company, so chances of doing well are high
- **Series B** gives definitely good chances, with **higher salary but possibly less equity**
- Earlier stage: **more equity, lower salary**
- Choice depends on **how long you are playing and what you are playing for**
- Learning: transforming a company from effectual to causal and founder-driven to manager-driven; learning about customers and operations; understanding **coordination** and how organizations are built; this learning **stays for a long time**

#### Growth Stage

- Company has become larger; **no more experiments**, work is cranking up repeat orders so things do not fall
- Need to ensure **compliance** is good; company is **raising money**
- **Hockey-stick growth** (not every firm has it); example: Zepto grew from about 18 people to a valuation of about 5 billion in under 3 years
- Informal organization becomes formal
- Role can **grow very quickly**, but joinee must **manage people** quickly: the shift is from **doing things to getting things done**
- **Politics** appears: early "one for all, all for one" energy gives way to **turf wars** as people "see the pot of gold"; org culture may feel disoriented
- This still gives another valuable set of experience

### 4.3.1.4 Stage Comparison

| Feature | Pre-PMF | Post-PMF | Growth |
|---------|---------|----------|--------|
| Status | Searching | Repeating what works | Scaling, no experiments |
| Product | Changes every few weeks | Delivery made repeatable | Cranking up repeat orders |
| Structure | Very little; no formal mentoring | Processes, KPIs, turfs | Formal; politics and turf wars |
| Role of joinee | Many jobs | More distinct roles | Managing people quickly |
| Mode | Founder-driven, effectual | Founder mode to manager mode, causal | Managers get things done |
| Failure risk | Highest | Series A shows an investor is betting on it | Compliance and fundraising focus |
| Equity vs salary | More equity, lower salary (equity pays only on success) | Series B: higher salary, possibly less equity | Not specified |
| Learning | Greatest crucible, breadth | Coordination, building organizations | Managing people, org change |

### 4.3.1.5 Part 2: What Do You Need?

- Have a personal **North Star**: why am I doing this
- Be **truthful to oneself**, whether or not it is told to parents, friends or founders

| If you need | Look for |
|-------------|----------|
| **Breadth** (try many functions, unsure what you are good at) | A **pre-PMF** company, or one **just about** when it starts to know what it is doing; gives many options |
| **Depth in a known interest** (e.g., financial modeling, performance marketing, customer talks, B2B sales) | A **slightly larger** startup with **someone senior to teach you** |
| **Mentoring** to learn fast | A company with a mentor available; having a mentor while you learn is important |
| **Stable income** (support family, repay educational loans) | A **well-funded, scaling** startup (e.g., Series B or C) that pays a decent salary while offering learning |

- A **mismatch** (e.g., confused about wants but joining a tiny startup, then complaining of no learning) is the problem to avoid
- Options when unsure: ask to work a short **trial period** (10 days, 15 days, 1 or 2 months), possibly unpaid for the deciding time, to test fit and culture
- Practice: for every offer, **write down the company stage and own needs** and check whether they match

## 4.4.1 Can I Survive the Worst Case?

### 4.4.1.1 Why Question 5 Matters

- Most people skip it because it seems negative
- It **makes it safe to act on the other four questions**
- Taking a chance on a startup does not mean **ignoring what could go wrong**

### 4.4.1.2 Reid Hoffman's Risk View (The Start-Up of You)

- Co-founder of LinkedIn; book with Ben Casnocha
- The so-called **safe option still carries risk** in a fast-changing world
- A **well-managed risk is less dangerous than it feels**
- The real danger is a **risk you cannot recover from**

### 4.4.1.3 Plan A, Plan B and Plan Z

| Plan | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Plan A** | What you are doing right now |
| **Plan B** | Where you move if Plan A needs to change; usually builds on what you have learned |
| **Plan Z** | The **lifeboat**; keeps you safe if everything goes wrong |

- Examples of Plan Z: move back to parents' place for some months, take a stable job to rebuild savings, go back to study
- If a **Plan Z can be lived with**, startup failure is **survivable**
- Plan Z should be **written down**; "I will figure it out" is not a plan
  - Hoffman's image: a startup is like **jumping off a plane and building a parachute on the way down**
  - If the plan is "I'll figure it out", accept it as a **cost paid** and narrow down future possibilities

### 4.4.1.4 What the Worst Case Looks Like for a Young Joinee

- Startup fails; the person leaves with **2 or 3 years of compressed experience**
- Leaves with **colleagues who will go on to build things**
- Many **second-time Indian co-founders** met their co-founders while working as early employees elsewhere

### 4.4.1.5 Four Worst-Case Questions

| # | Question | Detail |
|---|----------|--------|
| 1 | Suppose the company shuts down in **9 months**: can you pay bills for **3 to 6 months** while looking for the next job? | If not, think about what would help |
| 2 | Will the **next employer understand what you did**? | Employee number 12 at a startup that closed can **show what was built** (strong story); **4 months with no clear output** is a weak story; choose a role producing something showable |
| 3 | What are you **giving up that you cannot get back**? | Most early-career chances come around again; some do not |
| 4 | What is your **Plan Z**? | Write it down |

#### Q3 Detail: The Train Analogy (Kanwal Rekhi)

- "Only one train out of the platform" is a **wrong assumption**; trains leave continuously
- Advice given to a third-year engineering student wanting to quit for a startup: **finish the degree**, get about **3 years of experience**, then join a startup
- Chances that may **not come back**: a **scholarship** (e.g., to study abroad), a rare **visa** opportunity
- People later say "what if I had said yes"

### 4.4.1.6 Affordable Loss and Life Stage

- Link to **effectuation**: decide the **affordable loss** (1, 2 or 3 years)
- Young and without encumbrances, one can **take chances with time**
- Career is a **marathon, not a sprint**; pick one thing and build capability; avoid doing too many things at once
- Worst case if everything fails: some experience, friends, knowing what a startup is; former colleagues may later invite you to join something new
- Venkataraman's remark on timing: best time to start is **when you have no encumbrances**; an even better time may be just after marriage if the spouse works and agrees
- Do **not** act when **life situation is different** (e.g., family needs the income, loans to repay) just to look bold; that is **irresponsible**
- Play **within limits of responsibility**; keep a small **sandbox for play**

### 4.4.1.7 Family Considerations (Indian Context)

- **Unfavorable personal circumstances** (term attributed to Vasant and Nam): e.g., Deepinder Goyal's family was unhappy when he left a stable job at Zomato's start
- Family opinion matters a lot; take parents' opinion and try to convince them
- Most important: tell parents your **affordable loss** and **what you will do if things do not go well**
- Parents have seen more and bring a different perspective; **see if they have a value proposition**
- **Find out what worries the family**; the underlying reason behind yes or no
- Some parents actually **want** their child to try because they had no safety net themselves; some alumni offer to fund students

| Situation | Guidance |
|-----------|----------|
| Family took **loans** for your education and expects repayment (especially first generation) | Quitting a job and leaving loans pending is **irresponsible**; pay loans off |
| Example of responsible approach | After 4 years in B2B sales: loan paid, simple house bought (about 55 to 60 lakhs), **2-year runway**, friends who would offer a job; then started up |

- Example: a PGP founder told his father that if he took the company offer the father would be happy but he would be miserable; if he joined a startup the father would be unhappy; he chose his startup; the first startup did not do well, a later one did very well
- Contrast: **responsible** (decisions within limits of responsibility) versus **macho** ("I will make it happen")

### 4.4.1.8 Research Before Deciding

| Research step | What to do |
|---------------|------------|
| **Company history** | Check Tracxn or Crunchbase; Ministry of Corporate Affairs data; funding raised; articles on whether the company is high or low on cash |
| **Founder's public statements** | Watch podcasts and interviews; look for **consistency** (e.g., a founder who talks about culture but keeps losing early employees) |
| **Founder online** | Look at LinkedIn to see how the founder responds to people |
| **Interview behaviour** | Ask questions; if the company dislikes questions, that is a warning; if it sees them as a sign of ability to think, it is a **good startup** |
| **Specific question** | "What does winning look like for the founders?" |
| **Former employees** | Talk to people who have left; ignore nasty remarks, look at the **logic**; **triangulate** (is it many people or one disgruntled employee?) |

### 4.4.1.9 Identity and Final Choice

- Large corporate companies give **identity** and that works
- The question is whether identity comes from **interesting work that can "put a dent in the universe"**; then consider a startup
- Case: a candidate with offers from JustBooks and HP; the JustBooks founder said that at HP she would be a **cog in the wheel** and would not change HP's destination, while at JustBooks she could change where the company lands; she joined HP under parental pressure
- Join a startup **only if truly believing** it is the space to learn from, **not** because someone else says so

## 4.5.1 Interview: Snehanjan Shome, Design Generalist at Great Learning

### 4.5.1.1 Background and Context

- Design and UI/UX background (mechanical engineering degree); earlier ran a small design and web services studio whose first venture failed with debt
- Mentored by a startup co-founder, then joined **Zoojoo.be** (an HR tech platform) incubated at **NSRCEL, IIM Bangalore** (late 2013 to mid-2015)
- Later: second startup **Aware** (meditation app), both acquired by **Roundglass**; then **Great Learning**
- Played a **design generalist** role; now a **cross-functional generalist**
- Chose not to join an MNC because of **self-image**: wanted to connect with people and understand needs (the textbook definition of design)

### 4.5.1.2 Choosing the Company and the Problem

- Great design happens **within constraints**; early-stage startups in an incubator have little cash and cannot throw money at problems (**jugaad**)
- Joined because of the **problem to solve**, not salary or equity; needs were low and the founder could barely pay
- Chasing **glory** of doing something few can do
- The **first company sets the tone** for what you eventually do; domains can be switched later
- Advice to a young joinee: **equity should not be the number one factor**; ask **what problem** you are solving and **how much of it you own**
- Not an NGO: a joinee **should still optimize for own benefit** and ask for money if creating business value; optimizing only for problem at the first job is "not the most optimal strategy"
- Once you are someone people want to work with, the rest comes easily

### 4.5.1.3 Taking Ownership and Building Breadth

- Founders each owned a domain: business development, operations, engineering; he **owned the entire user experience**
- Ownership gave a **crazy breadth of view** (how marketing interacts, even helping an external client with internal apps)
- Being known for one thing (the **USP**, "purple cow") makes it easier to be hired and to empathize with founders' vision
- Staying within the same domain but solving **different problems** keeps interest
- **Journey is the reward**: fix your baseline; ask whether the problem will keep you engaged for 6, 7 or 10 years; people with money but misery are common
- Job hopping can be fine until a fit is found; then **go deep**
- **Problem safari**: try many problems until finding a cluster you excel at
- **Ownership varies** by role; the problem can be interesting anywhere; it is also acceptable to **shadow someone** to learn how they solve problems
- Founder attribute to look for: **go-getter attitude and relentlessness** ("if I want it, I will get it"); in a small team everybody plays a hero in a different domain; setbacks are necessary to get from A to B
- Observation: execution is getting **cheaper** (AI tools); the ability to think and to know what to solve is rarer; experience matters (Dunning-Kruger effect); learn from how other humans use AI and decide

### 4.5.1.4 Salary and Equity

- Discuss equity **straight and clean** so expectations are clear
- At the first startup equity was not negotiated; a **harder, structured negotiation** happened at the second (shares versus ESOPs, numbers to look at)
- ESOPs to a fresh joinee can **seem meaningless** because it is too early; what matters is whether equity makes sense **long term**, i.e., the company has plans and executes consistently
- If a company does not treat equity as important as cash, something is wrong
- Many companies give equity cheaply and **never exit**; equity from several companies did not work out for one person, but **network of people** remained
- Keep a **reasonable, amicable cap table**; do not grab everything for yourself

### 4.5.1.5 Bootstrapping and Fundraising

| Aspect | Points |
|--------|--------|
| **Bootstrapped company** | Founders keep control and exit on own terms; customers pay to solve a problem; growth is **slow but sustainable**; peace of mind (no scrambling for salary money every 3 months) |
| **Funded company** | "All money comes with strings"; investor almost always gets **preferential exit**; money raised only because it could be is a poor reason |
| **Hyper-growth (10X to 100X)** | Needs money to burn |
| **Rule** | Look for companies **not raising money just for the sake of it**; raise if there is a plan that needs it |

- Raising money creates **opportunity cost** pressure: how to make the most of it
- Money sitting idle in a bank does not help the joinee
- A bootstrapped company may raise money **only when needed** (e.g., to pay salaries while a slow client pays)
- Joining a bootstrapped company is great, but it is **not** the only good option

### 4.5.1.6 Culture

#### Definition: Culture

- A **default way of doing something in a given situation**; includes beliefs, how people talk, prioritize and handle crises
- "If you don't take good decisions on a good day, you will not take them on a bad day"
- **No good or bad cultures**, only cultures that fit different people; choose one that **fits your style**
- Some sectors (e.g., **finance**, regulated) need hierarchy; others allow freedom
- Different people suit different cultures: executors who need clear instructions versus strategists who run with freedom
- **Subcultures** exist within teams; depends on leaders
- Culture should follow the **task**: **process control** (e.g., sales teams that must not over-promise) versus **culture control** (product, tech, design teams doing new things)
- Assessing culture beforehand is hard: talk to **former** employees (usually less positive) and **current** ones (usually positive); filter disgruntled versus self-promoting views
- **Hawthorne effect**: the culture changes when observed; hiring managers show best behaviour
- Set **expectations clearly and early, from both sides**

### 4.5.1.7 Career Pacing

- Career is a **marathon**; build **influence and trust** over time; proof across the board
- Leaders **lead by example**; leadership is getting people to rally, not doing everything yourself
- "If you want to run fast, run alone; if you want to run long, run together"
- Staying long builds understanding of dynamics, **credibility and networks**; hoppers may build none
- Newer generation described as clearer about what they want (more self-actualization focus), adaptable and responsible
- Small niche products (e.g., a very specific app) can reach about $100,000 (close to a crore); a $10 million company is harder; business difficulty is **not linear**: it rises sharply near the top, while overheads stay small at small scale

## Quick Revision Summary

### Key Definitions

- **Five-question framework:** founder (what optimizing for), team equilibrium, product real, stage match, survive worst case; order is people, product, company, you
- **Heuristic:** thumb rule, **not** a scorecard; a startup can fail one question and still suit you; the candidate decides
- **Rich vs king:** wealth-focused founder hires strong leaders and shares control; control-focused founder keeps decisions close
- **Team equilibrium (3 Rs):** healthy team answers equity, roles, decision questions easily; both founders give the same answer
- **Delta 4:** new way beats old way by **4+ points on a 0 to 10 scale**, so behaviour change is irreversible
- **Brag-worthy proposition:** users want to tell others about the product
- **Vanity metrics:** downloads, sales volume; show growth, not retention
- **Product market fit (PMF):** clear customer group wants the product badly; demand pulls even with **zero marketing**
- **Plan A / Plan B / Plan Z:** current path / pivot building on learning / lifeboat if everything fails
- **Affordable loss:** time and money you can lose (1, 2 or 3 years) without harm
- **Culture:** default way of doing things; process control versus culture control
- **Bootstrapping:** funding growth from customers, keeping control

### Stage Summary

| Stage | Key point |
|-------|-----------|
| **Pre-PMF** | Searching; many jobs; no mentoring; highest failure risk; greatest learning; equity only if success |
| **Post-PMF** | Repeat and processes; effectual to causal; founder to manager mode; Series A best; Series B higher salary, less equity |
| **Growth** | Hockey-stick; formal organization; managing people; politics and turf; compliance |

### Must-Remember Points

- Three Delta 4 questions: **shut down next month?**, **stay without being paid to stay?**, **vanity metrics or real numbers?**
- Newest employees are often **first to be let go** when money slows and customers return to old habits
- Look at **how founders respond to bad reviews**; honesty signals trustworthiness
- Question 4 has two parts: **company stage** and **what you need in the next 2 years**
- Needs mapping: breadth leads to pre-PMF; depth with a mentor leads to slightly larger startup; stable income leads to well-funded Series B or C
- Worst-case checks: **3 to 6 months of bills** after a 9-month shutdown; **showable output**; things **not recoverable** (scholarships, visas); **written Plan Z**
- Kanwal Rekhi: there is never only one train; finish degree, gain about 3 years of experience, then join a startup
- Do not take startup risk with pending **family loans** or dependants; stay within **limits of responsibility**
- Tell parents the **affordable loss** and the **fallback plan**; find what worries them
- Research: Tracxn/Crunchbase, Ministry of Corporate Affairs, founder podcasts (consistency), LinkedIn, former employees (triangulate)
- Interview insights: choose the **problem and ownership** over equity; first company sets the tone; culture has no good or bad, only fit; bootstrapped growth is slow but sustainable; funded money comes with strings
