## 1.1 Logic and Critical Thinking in Reasoning

### 1.1.1 Seeing Is Not Always Believing

#### Key Concept
- **Information may be genuine while the conclusion drawn from it is still incorrect**
- A dashboard may contain real numbers, a graph may be accurate, an analysis sophisticated — yet **assumptions or alternative explanations** may be overlooked
- **Data never speaks for itself** — people interpret data, ask questions, make assumptions, construct explanations; hence **errors in reasoning become inevitable**
- Revised question: not "Is seeing believing?" but "**Do we believe everything we see, hear and read?**"

#### Dashboard Example
- Website traffic up **30%** over a weekend → seems marketing campaign worked
- Alternative explanations: **bots**, **technical error** in analytics, **viral post** creating curiosity but not purchases
- **The numbers are real, the graph is real, yet the conclusion may be wrong**

#### Human Tendencies in Evaluating Information
- Notice only evidence that **supports** our opinions; ignore evidence that **challenges** them
- **Jump to conclusions** with little information
- Rely on **heuristics (mental shortcuts)** — often useful, sometimes misleading
- **The smarter we become, the better we are at defending existing beliefs** rather than questioning them
- Critical thinking is **not intelligence**; it is the **intellectual discipline to question our own beliefs**

#### Case: Target Pregnancy Prediction (2012)
- **Andrew Pole** and team at **Target** built a predictive model to identify pregnant customers
- Signals: shift from scented to **unscented lotion**, **calcium** and **magnesium** supplements → likely first/second trimester
- Assigned a **pregnancy prediction score**; coupons timed to each stage
- A father in Minneapolis complained his teenage daughter got baby mailers; she turned out to be pregnant — **the model knew before the father**
- Model was **technically correct, data real, analysis rigorous**
- **What went wrong was the thinking around the analytics**, not the analytics
- Technical question asked: "**Can we predict this?**"; human questions neglected: "**Should we?**", second-order effects, trust, relationship with customers
- Response: inserted irrelevant ads (tools, beer, lawnmowers) to hide targeting — **precision stayed, they just hid it**
- **Analytical thinking was sophisticated; critical thinking was absent**

#### What Critical Thinking Is (and Is Not)
| Misconception | Reality |
|---|---|
| Criticising everything | **Not** criticism |
| Being sceptical of every claim | **Not exactly** |
| Actual meaning | **A careful process of evaluating information before reaching a conclusion** |

- Core questions: **What exactly is the claim? What evidence supports it? Is there another explanation? What assumptions am I making? Could I be wrong?**
- Asked by managers, judges, consultants, scientists, journalists

### 1.1.2 Logic and Critical Thinking as Two Checks

#### Definition
- **Critical thinking (Scriven & Paul):** "**Self-guided, self-disciplined thinking which attempts to reason at the highest level of quality in a fair-minded way**"
- **Reasoning:** the process of **moving from what you already know or believe to a new conclusion**
- **The information is not what makes reasoning good or bad — the process is**; two people with the same information can reach opposite conclusions

#### Founder Example
- Signups doubled after a price cut
- Founder 1: lower prices drove signups → cut prices further
- Founder 2: check whether it was the **price cut or a marketing push** in the same week
- **Same fact, different process** — only the second checks whether the conclusion is earned

#### Two Checks
| Check | Question | Tests |
|---|---|---|
| **Logic** | **Does the conclusion follow from the premises?** | **Structure** of the move |
| **Critical thinking** | **Are the premises true, complete and relevant?** | **Material** going into the move |

#### Two Points of Failure
| Failure | Description |
|---|---|
| **Structural** | Conclusion **does not follow** from premises, even if premises are true |
| **At the source** | Structure is fine, but premises are **incomplete, biased or false** — "argument standing on nothing" |

- Reasoning runs **constantly in the background** (reading a metric, sizing up a competitor, trusting a claim) — fast and unchecked
- Skill: **slow down** and run both checks before accepting a conclusion
- Logic and critical thinking = **two expressions of one discipline**
- Critical thinking is **not a checklist, not a personality trait — it is a learnable skill**

### 1.1.3 When Logical Reasoning Goes Wrong

#### Case: Challenger Space Shuttle (28 January 1986)
- Unusually cold morning at Kennedy Space Center; forecast **29°F**, colder than any prior launch
- **Morton-Thiokol** engineers concerned about the **O-ring** — rubber seal between booster segments preventing hot gas escape
- Data: earlier launch at **53°F** showed O-ring **erosion and blackening**
- **Roger Boisjoly** argued to delay: cold linked to damage; tomorrow colder → higher risk
- NASA management asked for **proof that cold caused failure** — reasonable and logically defensible on its face
- Engineers' chart plotted **only flights with O-ring damage** vs temperature; **excluded flights with no damage** (incl. warm launches)
- Without that comparison, pattern looked weak; with it, **the colder the launch, the more likely the damage**
- Chart **understated the risk**; launch went ahead; **73 seconds** after liftoff O-ring failed; **all 7 crew killed**

#### Key Lesson
- **It was not the logic — the evidence itself was incomplete**
- **Logic says nothing about whether premises are the right or complete ones**
- **A completely valid argument can reach a wrong answer if its starting premises are flawed**
- Better question than "management ignored engineers": **What evidence and questions were left out of the thinking process?**

### 1.1.4 Logic and Critical Thinking in Practice

#### Definitions
- **Logic:** the **structure of an argument** — formal rules that determine whether a conclusion flows from its premises
- Example of valid structure: "All recorded O-ring failures happened below 40°. Tomorrow will be 29°. Therefore, there is a meaningful risk of O-ring failure."
- **Critical thinking:** examines **where premises come from, what is missing**, and **whether you are asking the right question**

#### One Without the Other
| Combination | Result |
|---|---|
| **Logic without critical thinking** | Argument holds internally but rests on a **shaky foundation** — faulty assumptions, selective evidence, vested interests |
| **Critical thinking without logic** | **Vague sense something is off**; cannot explain where reasoning breaks; easy to be **talked out of a correct instinct** by someone more confident |

- Scriven & Paul: reasoning at highest quality = valid argument (**logic half**) + being **self-disciplined** to question foundations and **fair-minded** to follow answers even to undesired conclusions
- **Good reasoning = ability to construct an argument that holds together + discipline to keep testing whether it should**

#### Organisational Cases
| Case | What Happened | What Was Missing |
|---|---|---|
| **IL&FS (2018)** | Defaulted on ~**₹91,000 crore**; 3 rating agencies gave **highest grade**; debt grew **44%** in 3 years | Information was public; no one with authority asked why a **line of credit for overdue payments was not used** for that purpose |
| **BYJU'S (2022–23)** | Valued at **$22 bn** (India's most valuable startup); growth genuine; written down **86%** to under **$3 bn** | No ongoing check on whether **pandemic-era growth and losses could continue** once conditions shifted |
| **Challenger** | Valid reasoning on incomplete chart | Excluded no-damage flights |

- Common thread: **reasoning was defensible on its own terms**, but nobody tested whether **starting assumptions deserved the confidence** placed in them
- Ask: "The data clearly shows" — **which data? What data got left out?**
- **Calling a decision data-driven does not mean it was reasoned carefully**
- Shift from "**Does this argument make sense?**" to "**What would I need to see or hear to know whether it is right?**" — the second needs critical thinking

## 1.2 Obstacles to Critical Thinking

### 1.2.1 Common Obstacles to Critical Thinking

#### Key Concept
- Failures occur in **capable, well-informed people** — not from carelessness or lack of tools
- Cause: **default settings of the mind** that run automatically

#### Confirmation Bias
- **Definition:** tendency to **favour information that supports what you already believe**

| Part | Description |
|---|---|
| **Selective search** | Look for evidence only where you expect agreement |
| **Selective interpretation** | Read **ambiguous** evidence to fit existing belief |
| **Selective memory** | Recall supporting instances more easily |

- Example: founder believes product advantage is **speed** — reads only load-time reviews; files "fast but a bit expensive" as a compliment; later remembers "fast", forgets "expensive"
- Remedy: **before reviewing evidence, write down what would change your mind**, then check whether you looked for it

#### Motivated Reasoning
- **Definition:** reasoning **shaped by what you want to be true** rather than what evidence supports

| Part | Description |
|---|---|
| **Asymmetric scrutiny** | Supporting evidence accepted quickly; opposing evidence picked apart, held to a higher standard |
| **Rationalisation** | A story built **afterwards** that makes the process look neutral, even to the person |

- Example: 8-month feature — week-1 engagement accepted as proof; week-3 drop challenged (tracking off, seasonal dip, need more data) — **same dashboard, only one cross-examined**
- Remedy: ask **who benefits if the conclusion is true — is it you?** Would you apply **equal scrutiny** to opposing evidence?

#### Anchoring
- **Definition:** tendency for the **first number or claim** to shape every judgement that follows, **even with no real basis**

| Part | Description |
|---|---|
| **The anchor** | Initial figure entering the conversation, often with little justification (e.g. pen at Rs 9.99) |
| **Insufficient adjustment** | Later estimates move away from the anchor, **but not far enough** |

- **Wheel experiment:** random wheel number shifted estimates of % of UN member countries that are African — higher spin → higher estimate
- **Investor valuation:** opening valuation becomes the reference point; later talks adjust from it rather than rebuilding from actual numbers
- Remedy: **notice the first number and ask whether it was ever justified**

#### How the Three Combine
| Obstacle | Role |
|---|---|
| **Anchoring** | Supplies the **starting number or belief** |
| **Confirmation bias** | Directs **search** toward supporting evidence |
| **Motivated reasoning** | Supplies the **reason to want it right** and screens out contrary evidence |

- Together they produce a conclusion that **feels fully earned from real data but is wrong** because the process **was never neutral**
- Not every conclusion deserves suspicion; scrutinise conclusions that **come fast, feel obvious and look convenient**

### 1.2.2 Critical Thinking in Professional and Civic Life

#### Key Principle
- **Convincing and true are not the same thing**
- **A confident tone and a correct claim often travel together, which is why people stop checking**

#### Professional Life
| Case | Obstacle | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **AI hallucination** — 2023 New York lawyer cited **non-existent cases** from an AI tool | Skipped premise check because output looked **fluent and polished** | **Sanctions**, loss of licence |
| **Hiring panels** — strong impression in **first 5 minutes** (confidence, shared background), then seek confirming evidence | **Anchoring + confirmation bias** | Bad hire, long-term team impact |
| **Startup metrics reporting** — improving metric highlighted, deteriorating one footnoted | **Motivated reasoning at organisational scale** (not outright fraud) | Board decides on incomplete picture |

- Board check: "**What number are we not looking at right now?**"
- Professional critical thinking = **risk management** for decisions involving others' money, time and careers

#### Civic Life
| Context | Obstacle |
|---|---|
| Out-of-context / AI-generated videos in disasters, news, elections — **first version sets the frame**; corrections spread less | **Anchoring at civic scale** |
| **COVID-19** claims — belief tracked prior views on institutions, vaccines; opposing sides saw overlapping studies, trusted different subsets | **Confirmation bias** (life-and-death stakes) |
| **Elections** — statistics and projections from sources with competing incentives | Same tools as boardroom |

- Ask: **Where did this number come from? What might be missing? Am I accepting it because it is well-supported or because I wanted to hear it?**
- Volume of claims has grown; fabricating a convincing fake is now near-instant with AI; **checking habits have not kept pace**
- Critical thinking is **not just a personal virtue but a civic responsibility**; a citizenry that anchors on first claims can be moved by **whoever is fastest and most emotionally compelling**

### 1.2.3 Consolidating Logic and Critical Thinking

#### Summary of the Pillar
| Element | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Reasoning** | Moving from what you know to a new conclusion |
| **Logic** | Test of **structure** — does the conclusion follow? |
| **Critical thinking** | Test of **material** — do premises deserve trust? |
| **Both needed** | **Each catches a failure the other cannot see** |
| **Confirmation bias** | Shapes what evidence you **search for and notice** |
| **Motivated reasoning** | Shapes which conclusions you **want** and how sceptically you examine evidence |
| **Anchoring** | First number/claim **sets the frame** |

- The three **reinforce each other**, producing fluent, confident conclusions on a non-neutral process
- Critical thinking protects **good decisions in a boardroom and good judgement in a democracy** with the same tools — it is **civic infrastructure**

## 1.3 Evidence and Interpretation

### 1.3.1 Evidence, Interpretation, and Framing

#### Key Concept
- In all the cases, **the structure was not the problem — the premises were**
- Premises **do not arrive labelled** trustworthy or not; they come as **evidence** — a number, report, claim, chart or dataset
- Evidence is **not neutral**: it must be **interpreted**, and it is **framed before it reaches you**
- **Framing:** word choice, word order, what is included or left out can change conclusions **without altering a single fact**
- **Patrick Hurley — cognitive vs emotive meaning:** "headcount reduction" vs "gutted workforce"
- Framing = a whole **architecture of presentation** (chart, headline, slide, policy summary)
- Goal is **not cynicism**; ask "**What if this was presented differently?**" and whether that would change your belief

### 1.3.2 Evidence versus Assertion

#### Definitions
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Assertion** | A statement offered with **no support attached**; accepted on the speaker's **authority, confidence or repetition** |
| **Evidence** | Something offered in support of a claim that can be **checked, traced and verified** by someone other than the claimant |

- **The difference is not phrasing — both can sound equally confident; it is what stands behind the sentence**

#### Example
| Statement | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Our product is the fastest on the market." | **Assertion** | Nothing to check |
| "Independent benchmarking by a third-party testing firm found our product loads pages 40% faster than our 3 closest competitors." | **Evidence** | **Source** (third-party firm), **method** (independent benchmarking), **specific comparison** (40% vs 3 competitors) |

- Unsupported claims can be **more** persuasive — real studies carry **caveats and margins of error**; bare assertions do not
- Test: **what would it take to check it yourself?** Specific source/method/dataset → evidence; none → assertion

#### Numbers as Assertions
- **A number is evidence only if it has a traceable source and method**
- "**9 out of 10 professionals recommend**" = assertion unless you know who, source, sample size, who conducted the survey
- **Specificity does the persuasive work that verification should be doing**
- Market sizing slide "**$50 billion opportunity**" = assertion unless source and measure cited
- Civic: "**many people say**" — confidence of delivery tells nothing about support

#### Evidence That Fails the Test
- Citation, chart or screenshot may still fail: citation **does not say what is claimed**, **misleading axes**, **altered or out-of-context** screenshot
- Second step people skip: **has someone credible confirmed the source says what it is used to say?**

#### Well-Informed Assertions
- Not every assertion should be distrusted — e.g. an experienced **doctor's clinical judgement** from history, symptoms and decades of experience need not come with CT/MRI/blood tests
- Basic habit: **Is this evidence, or an assertion wearing evidence's clothing?** — asked **before** examining meaning, framing or omissions

### 1.3.3 Facts, Inferences, and Interpretations

#### Definitions
| Layer | Definition | Answers | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fact** | **Verifiable state of affairs**, true regardless of who looks; checkable by anyone | **What happened?** | Quarterly revenue was $2.3 million; unemployment 4.1% last month; video recorded on March 3rd |
| **Inference** | **Conclusion drawn from facts using reasoning** — a step beyond what was observed | **What does it likely lead to?** | Revenue rose in each of the last 3 quarters, so it will likely rise next quarter |
| **Interpretation** | **Assigns meaning or significance** to facts; shaped by **perspective, values, framework** | **What does it mean?** | "Growth shows new sales strategy works" vs "Growth reflects market-wide seasonal upswing" |

- Facts **do not argue for anything** by themselves
- A **good inference** is disciplined, follows a **visible path** that can be checked for **validity or strength**
- Two people can produce **different, equally reasonable interpretations** of the same facts; choosing requires **additional evidence, judgement and context**

#### Civic Example: Crime Statistics
| Layer | Statement |
|---|---|
| **Fact** | Reported incidence of a crime rose **15%** last year (if methodology sound) |
| **Inference** | Reflects an **actual increase** in crime rather than change in **reporting behaviour or policing focus** — valid only after **ruling out alternative explanations** |
| **Interpretation** | A policy failed / an approach to public safety should be abandoned — **shaped by values** |

- All three are often delivered **in the same breath with the same certainty**; listeners treat value-laden interpretation **as if on the same footing as fact**
- **Interpretation is necessary** — facts rarely say what to do; refusing to interpret lets **someone else's interpretation fill the space**
- Aim: **know which layer you are in** and stop **certainty of fact** leaking into inference and interpretation

#### Practical Habit
- **Fact:** Is it checkable? Is it in dispute?
- **Inference:** Evaluate for **validity or strength** like any argument
- **Interpretation:** Judgement about significance — deserves scrutiny, **open to reasonable disagreement**

## 1.4 Framing and Selection

### 1.4.1 How Framing Influences Interpretation

#### Definition
- **Framing does not change what is true; it changes what the reader or listener takes away as true**
- Medical study: "**90% survival rate**" vs "**10% mortality rate**" — identical numbers; survival framing made doctors and patients **more willing to choose the procedure**

#### Mechanisms of Framing
| Mechanism | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Word choice** | **Cognitive vs emotive meaning** (Hurley) — same fact, different emotional charge | "Reduced headcount by 15%" vs "gutted its workforce" |
| **2. Comparison point** | What the number is **measured against** | Sales +5%: modest; "best in company history" = triumph; "below industry 12% average" = problem |
| **3. Visual framing** | Chart scale/window; charts look **objective by default** | Y-axis starting at 90 makes 2-point shift dramatic; 3-month spike vs flat 3-year trend |
| **4. Order and emphasis** | What comes first, headlines, what is buried | Revenue +8% headline; costs +14% in paragraph 6 — technically complete |
| **5. Anchoring** | A number's **mere presence** shifts judgement | See below |

#### Anchoring (Kahneman)
| Mode | Description |
|---|---|
| **Deliberate but insufficient adjustment** | Consciously move from a starting number but **not far enough** |
| **Simple suggestion** | Number shifts judgement even when **never treated as an estimate** and known to be **arbitrary** |

- **Real estate agents:** different listing prices for the same house → valuations biased toward listing price, though agents **denied** it played a role
- **Multiplication:** $1\times2\times\dots\times8$ estimated **lower** than $8\times7\times\dots\times1$ — first numbers set the anchor
- **The anchor is often just the first number a presenter chooses to put in front of you**

#### Applications
- **Pitch deck:** same unit economics — "capital efficient vs category" or "margins below investor expectations"
- **News:** "Unemployment falls to lowest level in a decade" vs "Millions still without work despite reported job gains" — neither fabricated
- **Politics:** "tax relief for working families" vs "tax cut mostly benefiting high earners" — both defensible

#### Key Points
- **No neutral, frame-free presentation exists** — choosing words, order, comparison point is unavoidable
- Framing is **not inherently manipulative or dishonest**
- Goal: **notice the frame** and ask what an **equally accurate alternative frame** would look like
- **Substitution test:** reframe — failure rate instead of success rate, smaller time window, harsher comparison — **same conclusion?**
- **Anchoring check:** has your estimate moved far from the first number? If not, **the conclusion came from the frame, not the fact**

### 1.4.2 Selection Bias and the Objective Record

#### Selection Bias
- **Definition:** concerns **what never reaches you at all** — the **sharpest form of framing**, not a separate topic
- You can notice a loaded word, but **not something never shown**
- **WYSIATI — "What You See Is All There Is" (Kahneman):** the mind builds a **coherent story from available information** without asking what is missing; **near-automatic, affects everybody**
- A conclusion from a **small, selective set feels as solid** as one from a complete picture
- **More powerful than other framing mechanisms** — a misleading axis can be caught, a **missing data point cannot**
- Prior question: **"What wasn't I shown that might have changed my mind?"**

#### Survivorship Bias — WWII Bombers
- Researchers mapped damage on **returning bombers**: heavy on **wings and tail** → natural conclusion: armour there
- **Abraham Wald:** data only included planes that **made it back**; damage showed where a plane **could be hit and survive**
- Planes hit in **engines and cockpit** never returned → **armour belonged where data showed no damage**
- **The absence of data was the most important signal**, invisible until someone asked what was missing
- Business: **startup accelerator** showcasing successful alumni excludes failed ones → sample **not representative of real odds**

#### Availability and Risk Perception (Civic)
- People **overestimate vivid causes of death** (plane crashes, shark attacks, terrorism) vs common ones (**heart disease, diabetes**)
- Cause: vivid events get **disproportionate media coverage** → more **available in memory**
- **Availability gets mistaken for frequency**
- Public concern and policy shaped by what is **dramatic and reportable**, not what is common and serious
- **No one has to suppress data** — one category is simply more visible, and **visibility is mistaken for the complete picture**
- Habit (distinct from substitution test): **What population, dataset or cases were excluded before I saw the evidence? If included, would I draw the same conclusion?**

#### The Objective Record
- Idea challenged: the **raw record** (video, dataset, transcript) is **neutral, untouched**, free of framing and selection
- **Even the raw record is not neutral** — it reflects **choices about what to capture and leave out**, made **at collection, before any framing**
- **Video:** camera has a **field of view**; everything outside does not exist in the record; "the tape doesn't lie" — **real but not automatically complete**
- **Datasets:** exist only because someone **decided what counted as a data point** — a choice, not a neutral fact
- Kahneman: mind builds a **smooth coherent narrative** from incomplete data and mistakes coherence for completeness
- Clean, legible records feel authoritative; **clarity ≠ guarantee nothing was left out**
- **A record can be entirely genuine but still partial** — **evidence may be genuine and still be incomplete**

# Module 2: Critical Reading and Interpretation

## 2.0 Learning Objectives

- Separate what a text **explicitly states** from what it **implicitly assumes or wants the reader to conclude**
- Apply **close-reading techniques** to literary texts and business documents
- Analyse **narratives** using the **5Cs** and the **SCR** structure
- Build and evaluate **counter-readings** using the **evidentiary, consistency and predictive tests**

## 2.1 Texts and Close Reading

### 2.1.1 Texts and Close Reading

#### Key Concept: The Two Levels of a Text

- Every text (novel, business report, speech, email, article) makes claims on **two levels at the same time**
- **Explicit level:** what the text **directly states**
- **Implicit level:** what the text **assumes, values, emphasises or wants the reader to conclude** without saying it directly
- **Critical reading** requires attention to **both** layers
- **Close reading** = the disciplined practice of identifying both layers and **pointing to where each appears** in the text
- Aim is **not** to be suspicious of every statement; it is to separate what is **supported by the text** from what the reader is **encouraged to infer**

#### Definition: Explicit Claim vs Implicit Position

| Aspect | Explicit claim | Implicit position |
|---|---|---|
| **Meaning** | What the text directly states | What the text assumes, values or wants you to conclude |
| **Evidence** | Can be **quoted** or pointed to as an exact sentence | Must be **inferred** from a pattern of choices (what is chosen, omitted, emphasised) |
| **Example** | "Revenue grew 12% this quarter" | Repeated emphasis on speed implies speed is valued above quality |
| **Fact-checking** | **Easier** to fact-check | **Harder** to fact-check and challenge |
| **Critical question** | What exactly does the text say? | What is the text quietly asking me to believe? |

- **Most persuasion happens at the implicit layer** - it is hidden, does more work, and is harder to challenge; hence close reading matters
- Example: a report citing only metrics favourable to leadership's prior decision **implies the decision was correct** without saying so

#### Key Concept: Illustrations of the Two Levels

| Text | Explicit claim | Implicit position |
|---|---|---|
| Earnings call | "We grew active users 8% quarter over quarter" | Opening with user growth and mentioning falling revenue per user only in a later, unprompted analyst question implies **growth matters more than monetisation** |
| Op-ed | "The new policy reduced permitting delays by 30%" | Discussing only permitting speed, never cost or who absorbed it, implies the policy is a **clear success** - a position argued **by omission** |

#### Key Concept: A Horse and Two Goats (R.K. Narayan, 1960)

- Setting: tiny South Indian village **Kirittam**; **Muni**, an elderly, very poor goatherd reduced to **two goats**; an American tourist arrives in a yellow station wagon near an ancient **clay horse statue** (with a warrior statue)
- Language barrier: Muni speaks only **Tamil**, the American only **English**

| Character | Unchecked assumption | What he "heard" |
|---|---|---|
| **Muni** | The khaki-clad foreigner is a **government official investigating a murder**; later, that money must be for his goats | A deal to sell his **two goats** |
| **American** | Muni **owns the clay horse** and is willing to sell it | An **agreement** to sell the statue |

- Outcome: the American leaves with the statue; Muni leaves with **120 rupees** (100-rupee note + 20 more); the goats follow Muni home
- **Nobody lied**, yet both left with a wrong idea of what happened
- Source of error: each built the transaction on an **assumption he never checked**, reading what he **wanted/expected to hear** and filling in the rest
- This is exactly what an **implicit position** is - each read the other's implicit position wrongly, and it felt obvious
- Themes: **poverty and wealth, class and cultural differences**, different values attached to the same object, different ways of seeing the world
- Lesson: close reading helps catch others reading their assumptions into your words and calling it agreement - and **catch yourself** doing it

### 2.1.2 Close Reading Techniques

#### Key Concept: The Five Techniques

| # | Technique | Question to ask | What it signals | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Repetition and emphasis** | What word, phrase or idea keeps recurring? | Importance the text has not stated directly | Memo mentioning **speed 6 times, quality once** implicitly **ranks speed over quality** |
| 2 | **Absence and omission** | Whose perspective would complicate the claim, and is it present? | Links to **selection bias** - missing risk, counterargument, stakeholder, region, metric or outcome | Hiring announcement covering only the role being hired, not the role eliminated, implies the loss does not matter |
| 3 | **Word choice** | What do the words carry beyond facts? | Separates **cognitive meaning** (states a fact) from **emotive meaning** (carries an attitude) - Patrick Hurley | **"Restructured" vs "gutted"** describe the same event; choosing one is an implicit position on whether it was justified |
| 4 | **Structure and order** | What comes first; what is held to the last paragraph or appendix? | **Order is a framing choice**; hierarchy matters | A risk **buried in paragraph 9** is told to matter less than the opportunity leading paragraph 1 |
| 5 | **Assumed audience** | Who does the text assume is reading; what does it assume they believe? | A text written as if its conclusion is obvious implies any reasonable reader already agrees | That assumption is itself a position, often unexamined |

### 2.1.3 Annotating a Real Paragraph

#### Key Concept: Worked Annotation

- Paragraph: *"Our platform helped thousands of small businesses grow this year. As always, we remain focused on the merchants who make a community what it is."*

| Layer | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Explicit claim** | Helped **thousands of small businesses grow** |
| **Implicit position 1** | **"As always"** implies **consistency** the paragraph never demonstrates with evidence |
| **Implicit position 2** | **No mention of businesses that left** the platform - an **absence, not a denial** |

#### Key Concept: Two Common Traps

| Trap | Description | Check |
|---|---|---|
| **Mistaking a position for a fact** | An implicit position repeated often enough starts to **feel like a fact** | Could a **reasonable person read the same text and hold the opposite position**? If yes, it is a position, not a fact |
| **Overreading** (the opposite error) | Finding an implicit position supported only by **your own assumptions**, not the text | Interpretation must be **anchored in the text** |

#### Key Concept: The Discipline of Evidence

- Close reading is **not speculation** for its own sake
- Every implicit reading needs a **specific textual hook**: a **word**, an **omission** or a **structural choice**
- Method: **state the implicit position**, then **name the textual feature** that supports it
- If you cannot point to the hook, you have a **hypothesis, not a close reading**
- Guiding question: **"Where exactly in the text did I get this idea?"**

### 2.1.4 Two Layers, One Discipline

#### Key Concept: Summary Principle

- **Explicit claims** tell you what a text **actually says**; **implicit positions** tell you what it **wants you to believe**
- Close reading = catching both and pointing to **exactly where each lives** in the text

#### Key Concept: The Close-Reading Checklist

| # | Question |
|---|---|
| 1 | What does the text **explicitly claim**? What sentence could you quote? |
| 2 | What **repeats** and what gets **emphasised**? |
| 3 | What is **absent** that a fuller account would include? |
| 4 | What do the specific **words carry** beyond their factual content? |
| 5 | What **comes first** and what is **buried**? |
| 6 | **Who** is the text assuming **already agrees** with it? |

- Applies to novels, films, business reports and data sets

#### Key Concept: Checklist Applied to A Horse and Two Goats

| Question | Application |
|---|---|
| **Explicit claim** | The two men never understand each other's language, yet each believes communication is happening; Muni is extremely poor, the American wealthy (station wagon, crisp khaki, English) |
| **Repeated / emphasised** | Muni's poverty (no food, debts, lost livestock); the communication gap; contrast between poor rural village and affluent modern America; the clay horse - shows the misunderstanding is **cultural and economic, not just linguistic** |
| **Absent** | What happens after the statue is removed; villagers' reactions; the American never learns Muni did not own it; whether Muni keeps the money or faces consequences; neither gains the other's perspective - omissions keep focus on **irony**, not resolution |
| **Word choice / symbolism** | **Two goats** = reduced fortunes, fragile livelihood; **the horse** = cultural history and mythology for Muni, mere **art object** for the American; car/clothing words = wealth, mobility, modernity; Kirittam words = simplicity, poverty, tradition |
| **First vs buried** | Opens with Muni's daily life and poverty, so readers see through **Muni's perspective**; the real misunderstanding is revealed gradually; final irony (goats return home) comes only after both leave satisfied |
| **Assumed audience** | Readers recognise that language barriers cause genuine misunderstanding, wealth/poverty shape interpretation, cultural objects carry different meanings, and will appreciate the irony; invites **empathy** for both rather than judging either as foolish |

- Deeper lesson: **two people, two unchecked assumptions** - both readings felt obvious, both were wrong

## 2.2 Close Reading Business Documents

### 2.2.1 Applying Close Reading to Business Documents

#### Key Concept: Techniques by Document Type

| Document | Technique | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| **Annual reports** | Structure | What leads the **shareholder letter** vs what appears only in the regulator-required, rarely read **risk factors** section |
| | Word choice | "**Challenging year**" vs "**difficult year with significant losses**" - same numbers, different imagined scene |
| | Omission | Which **segment, region or metric** from last year quietly **disappeared** this year |
| **Press releases** | Emphasis | The **headline** states the number the company wants remembered - usually not the whole story |
| | Structure | An **executive quote near the top** frames how the following facts are interpreted |
| | Assumption check | What would need to be **omitted or reframed** for the headline number to still sound impressive? |
| **Pitch decks** | Omission | Which **competitor risk** or **failed channel** never appears |
| | Word choice | "**We are capital efficient**" vs "**Our margins are below what investors expect at this stage**" - same unit economics |
| | Assumption check | The **growth projection slide** almost always assumes **past growth conditions will hold** - is it named or only implied? |

- **Structure, word choice, omission and emphasis** reveal a text's real position; **identifying assumptions** reveals what it is quietly standing on
- Aim: read critically **without assuming every communication is misleading**

### 2.2.2 Close Reading in Practice

#### Key Concept: Fast Read vs Slow Read

- A quick read leaves an impression (solid quarter, some challenges, confident management)
- A slow, sentence-by-sentence read (like an analyst, journalist or auditor) asks **what each phrase actually says**
- **Confident-sounding language or numbers with no verifiable content is suspicious**

#### Key Concept: Line-by-Line Findings (Northgate report)

| Phrase | Issue | Close-reading point |
|---|---|---|
| "**Broadly in line with expectations**" | Whose expectations, set by whom and when? | Sounds precise but **no baseline** given; cannot be checked |
| "**Footfall declined 8% YoY**" + "**online engagement metrics trending positively**" | **Precision vs vagueness** | Hard, checkable number paired with a vague phrase is rarely accidental; the vague one is **probably not good news** either - if strong, a figure would be given |
| "**Explore opportunities to optimise the store portfolio**" | **Corporate euphemism** | Usually means **closing stores**; reader supplies the meaning without the report committing in writing |
| "**Operating costs increased 6% and these investments position Northgate well for future growth**" | **Unsupported causal claim** | **Asserted, not demonstrated** - no metric, comparison or timeline; stated with the same confidence as the hard figure |
| "**The team has worked tirelessly**" | **Emotional / reputational language** | Says nothing about performance; placed right after rising costs - a warm sentence after the least comfortable numbers **softens the transition** |
| "**Q4 will present similar dynamics... committed to delivering value for stakeholders**" | **Vague closing statement** | Commits to nothing; a claim almost no company would contradict, so carries **little information**; a strong close gives a **number, target or timeline** |

#### Key Concept: The Side-by-Side Rewrite

- Same information rewritten so every vague claim is **backed by a number** or **flagged as an unproven assumption**
- The rewrite is **shorter** - much of the original's length did **persuasive, not informational** work
- The rewrite sounds **far less confident**
- Conclusion: the original is **not dishonest**, but written to **feel more informative than it was**

#### Key Concept: Four Rewrite Questions

| # | Question |
|---|---|
| 1 | What is the **comparison point** for this number? |
| 2 | What is this **adjective doing**? |
| 3 | Who is the **hidden actor** in this sentence? |
| 4 | What **question** is this sentence really answering? |

- Goal is not to catch a lie but to build the habit of noticing how **confident language differs from real evidence**

## 2.3 Narratives, Power, and Counter-Readings

### 2.3.1 Narratives and Interpretation (Part A)

#### Key Concept: The 5Cs of a Narrative

| C | Question | Tortoise and Hare |
|---|---|---|
| **Character** | Who is placed at the centre? | Hare and tortoise |
| **Context** | What circumstances are treated as relevant? | A public race with a referee (fox) |
| **Conflict** | What problem, tension or disruption is presented? | Natural speed vs persistence and discipline |
| **Choices** | Which decisions receive attention? | Hare naps; tortoise keeps going |
| **Consequences** | What outcomes become the ending or moral? | Tortoise wins; hare loses |

- The 5Cs are the underlying structure of almost every story - about businesses, leaders or organisations

#### Key Concept: Same Story, Three Readings

| Reading | Focus | Moral |
|---|---|---|
| **Conventional** | Tortoise's persistence | **Slow and steady wins the race** |
| **Hare-centred** | Hare's choices | **Exceptional ability can be undone by arrogance, overconfidence and complacency** - he assumed talent equalled success |
| **Ebner-Eschenbach** (Marie von Ebner-Eschenbach) | After the race | The tortoise begins to think of himself as a **fast runner** - we credit our own ability for success when the real reason may be luck or others' mistakes |

- **None changes any event**; only the emphasis and conclusion change

#### Definition: Counter-Reading (introduced)

- A counter-reading **does not invent new events or ignore old ones**; it interprets the **same story from a different angle**

#### Key Concept: Blockbuster vs Netflix

| Element | Dominant narrative | Counter-reading |
|---|---|---|
| Story | Netflix (small DVD-by-mail underdog) saw where technology was heading; Blockbuster too complacent, attached to **late fees**, too slow; **better technology won** | **Insiders had proposed streaming and subscription** years earlier |
| Cause | External technological disruption | **Senior leadership chose** to protect short-term late-fee revenue and the physical store network |
| Question asked | Who innovated? | **Who inside recognised what was coming, and what happened to their proposal?** |

- Facts: 9,000+ stores at peak in late 1990s; hundreds of millions per year from late fees; **bankruptcy in 2010**
- The story given is **never simply a transcript** - someone decides the important characters, context, conflict, choices and consequences

#### Key Concept: Guiding Questions for Narratives

- How do narratives persuade? How is storytelling connected to power? Whose perspectives are included or left out? How is a counter-reading built?
- Big question: **which interpretation is stronger on logical grounds?**

#### Key Concept: Narratives as Persuasive Structures

- A narrative is a sequence of events **deliberately arranged** to lead towards a particular conclusion, often before we realise it
- Persuasive power comes from the **order** in which facts are presented, not only the facts

#### Formula: SCR Model

$$\text{Narrative} = \text{Situation} \rightarrow \text{Complication/Conflict} \rightarrow \text{Resolution}$$

| Stage | Also called | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Situation** | Setup | Introduces a **problem or opportunity** |
| **Complication / Conflict** | Disruption | Something **changes, goes wrong or creates tension** |
| **Resolution** | Resolution | The organisation **responds, adapts or succeeds** |

- **WeWork:** sold a story of **community and workplace revolution**, positioned as a **technology** rather than real estate company; **mission first, financial second** justified its valuation; the **IPO prospectus** exposed a mismatch between story and balance sheet, and the valuation collapsed
- Ask: What is the setup? What is the disruption? What counts as the resolution? **What assumptions about the future are encouraged by how the past is told?**

### 2.3.2 Power, Omission and Counter-Readings (Part B)

#### Key Concept: Politics of Storytelling

- Stories shape what is seen as **legitimate, normal, innovative, acceptable or unacceptable**
- Whoever establishes the **dominant narrative** gets to define these
- **Not simple propaganda** - the everyday process through which some ways of understanding seem reasonable and others do not
- The same facts can produce very different public judgments depending on **framing**

| Company | Narrative | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Uber** | Disruption, consumer freedom, cheaper rides, innovation vs outdated regulation | Drivers as independent contractors; working conditions framed as price of innovation - **a labour issue reframed as a technology issue**; shaped public opinion and policy |
| **IBM** | Professionalism, rigour, long-term thinking | Received **more benefit of the doubt** during controversy than a younger firm doing similar things |

#### Definition: Narrative Omission

- **Every story leaves something out**; ask **"Who isn't in this story?"** and how they would tell it
- Shows **who is telling the story** and **which perspective is privileged**
- **Silence can itself become part of the narrative**

| Company | Story told | Left out |
|---|---|---|
| **Nike** (1990s) | Elite athletes, personal achievement, performance | **Supplier-factory workers** in Indonesia and Vietnam - wages and working conditions |
| **Facebook** | Connecting the world, empowering users | **Third-party data buyers** and the commercial ecosystem around user data |

- Exercise idea: in a sustainability report, list three missing stakeholder groups; would they write the same story?

#### Definition: Counter-Reading

- **Not simply disagreeing** - a **different way of constructing the same story**
- **Events stay the same; perspective changes**: change the **narrator**, **start the story elsewhere**, or use a **different definition of success**
- Rebuilds the 5Cs from a different viewpoint: change the central **character** and the **conflict** often changes, different **choices** matter, **consequences** look different

#### Key Concept: How to Construct a Counter-Reading

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | Identify the dominant narrative's **main character** |
| 2 | Identify its **measure of success** (stated or implied) |
| 3 | Identify **missing stakeholder groups** whose experiences/outcomes are ignored |
| 4 | Using **available evidence**, rebuild the **conflict and key choices** from their perspective |
| 5 | **Compare the two versions using the 5Cs** |
| 6 | Ask whether the stories **contradict** each other or **highlight different aspects** of the same events |

#### Key Concept: Evaluating Rival Interpretations

- A counter-reading is **still an argument** and must be evaluated - not by which feels more satisfying, but by **evidence and reasoning**

$$\text{Test the story: Evidence} \rightarrow \text{Consistency} \rightarrow \text{Prediction}$$

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| **Evidentiary test** | Where is the **verifiable evidence**? Reliable evidence or mainly speculation? (Noticing a missing group is only the beginning) |
| **Consistency test** | Does it explain the evidence **at least as well** as the dominant narrative? Does it **account for the facts that made the original persuasive**, or ignore what does not fit? |
| **Predictive test** | Which interpretation better explains **what happened next**? A strong explanation helps understand what may happen under similar conditions |

| Fable reading | Test performance |
|---|---|
| Slow and steady | Fits the single race, **weaker on predictive** test (says nothing about a rematch or a hare that does not stop) |
| Arrogance undoes talent | **Stronger predictive** - identifies a repeatable behaviour pattern |
| Ebner-Eschenbach | **Strong on consistency** - explains what happens after the finish line; survives closer scrutiny, but is **not the one true moral** |

### 2.3.3 Boeing 737 MAX Case (Part C)

#### Key Concept: The Case

- Oct 2018 - Mar 2019: **Lion Air Flight 610** (Indonesia) and **Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302** crashed; nearly **346** killed
- **Dominant narrative (Boeing):** **pilot error and training**; aircraft fundamentally safe; implied differences in pilot training, culture and aviation standards
- **Counter-reading** (journalists, engineers, accident investigators, government inquiries): centred on the aircraft - **MCAS (Manoeuvring Characteristics Augmentation System)**, designed to push the nose down; **faulty sensor data** activated it repeatedly though no stall danger; pilots fought it but it kept forcing the nose down
- Pilots had **little or no MCAS training** because Boeing wanted the aircraft marketed as requiring **minimal additional pilot certification**
- **Internal communications** showed employee concerns about design and certification before the second crash

#### Key Concept: 5Cs Shift

| C | Dominant narrative | Counter-reading |
|---|---|---|
| **Character** | Pilots | Aircraft **design and certification process**; Boeing management |
| **Conflict** | Pilot competence | **Engineering decisions and regulatory oversight** |
| **Choices** | Pilots' cockpit actions in final minutes | **Management decisions** on design, testing, certification years earlier |
| **Consequences** | Better pilot training | **Aircraft redesign, stronger regulatory oversight, examination of corporate decision-making** |

- Events and timeline unchanged; **interpretation changed**

#### Key Concept: Why the Counter-Reading Was Stronger

| Test | How it passed |
|---|---|
| **Evidentiary** | Accident investigation reports, **flight data recorders**, internal Boeing communications, **FAA documentation** - not speculation or hindsight |
| **Consistency** | Explained **both crashes by the same mechanism** and why experienced pilots struggled to recover in identical circumstances |
| **Predictive** | If design was the problem, similar accidents would recur unless changed - followed by **global grounding**, **MCAS redesign**, revised pilot-training requirements, added regulatory scrutiny |

#### Key Concept: Final Lessons

- Narratives being constructed does **not** mean every corporate story is dishonest, **nor** that every counter-reading deserves equal credibility
- Ask: **who is telling the story**, whose perspective is **prioritised** or **missing**, what **definition of success** is assumed
- Most important: does the counter-reading meet the **same standards of evidence and reasoning** as the original?
- Ebner-Eschenbach warning for companies: one success mistaken for proof of brilliance, forgetting **timing, circumstances or others' mistakes**
- Critical reading separates **a good story** from **a good explanation**; a persuasive story is **not necessarily the strongest explanation**

## Quick Revision Summary

### Key Definitions

- **Explicit claim:** directly stated, quotable, easier to fact-check
- **Implicit position:** assumed/valued/intended conclusion, inferred from patterns of choice; where most persuasion happens
- **Close reading:** identifying both layers and pointing to where each lives in the text
- **Textual hook:** a word, omission or structural choice supporting an implicit reading; without it, only a hypothesis
- **Narrative:** events deliberately arranged towards a conclusion
- **Narrative omission:** who is left out and whose perspective is privileged; silence is part of the narrative
- **Counter-reading:** same events, different perspective (narrator, starting point, definition of success)

### Important Frameworks

$$\text{SCR} = \text{Situation} \rightarrow \text{Complication/Conflict} \rightarrow \text{Resolution}$$

$$\text{5Cs} = \text{Character} + \text{Context} + \text{Conflict} + \text{Choices} + \text{Consequences}$$

$$\text{Evaluation} = \text{Evidentiary} \rightarrow \text{Consistency} \rightarrow \text{Predictive}$$

### Must-Remember Points

- Five techniques: **repetition/emphasis, absence/omission, word choice, structure/order, assumed audience**
- Traps: **position mistaken for fact** (opposite-reading check) and **overreading**
- Speed 6x vs quality 1x = implicit ranking; "restructured" vs "gutted" = cognitive vs emotive meaning; risk in paragraph 9 = matters less
- "As always" = unsupported consistency; missing departed merchants = absence, not denial
- Business red flags: no baseline, precise vs vague pairing, euphemism ("optimise store portfolio"), unsupported causal claim, emotional filler, vague close
- Rewrite is shorter and less confident: original felt more informative than it was
- Blockbuster: leadership rejected internal streaming proposals; WeWork: story vs balance sheet; Uber: labour reframed as technology
- Boeing 737 MAX: pilot error narrative vs MCAS/design counter-reading - passed all three tests

# Module 3: Constructing and Evaluating Arguments

## 3.1 Arguments and Rhetorical Appeals

### 3.1.1 Arguments in Business Decisions

#### Key Concept: From Story to Argument
- Business narratives are **not just a collection of facts**; they are a carefully constructed account guiding the audience to a particular view of a problem and a particular solution.
- The **resolution** stage (of SCR: Situation, Complication, Resolution) is where description turns into **recommendation**: this is where **storytelling becomes an argument**.
- **Stories capture attention; arguments justify decisions.** Business almost always needs both.

| Role | Does not simply... | Actually argues... |
|------|-------------------|--------------------|
| **Startup founder** | Say a market exists | The market is **worth entering** |
| **Consultant** | Describe challenges | For **one strategy over another** |
| **Product manager** | Present customer feedback | A feature **deserves funding** |
| **Employee** | Write an email | For approval of a proposal |

#### Key Concept: Arguments in the Age of AI
- AI tools (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Copilot) produce **polished, organised** reports, memos, marketing copy and recommendations in seconds.
- Technology changed the **speed** of production, **not the standards** by which arguments are judged.
- Questions to ask beneath polished writing:
  - What is the **claim**?
  - What **evidence** supports it?
  - What **reasoning** connects evidence to conclusion?
  - What **assumptions** lie underneath?

#### Key Concept: Netflix 2006 Case
- In 2006, **DVD-by-mail** was profitable, growing and well understood; **streaming** was expensive, unreliable and broadband was not widespread.
- "Streaming is the future" is **neither evidence nor an argument**.

| Element | Netflix 2006 |
|---------|--------------|
| **Claim** | Netflix should invest aggressively in streaming |
| **Evidence** | Rising broadband adoption, falling data transmission costs, changing media consumption, projections of consumer behaviour |
| **Reasoning** | Why these trends justify a costly investment **now** rather than waiting 5 years |

- **Evidence alone is not enough**; the **reasoning (logical bridge)** persuaded the board.
- **The argument came first, the story came later**: successful decisions are retold as stories with clear heroes, villains, turning points and neat endings (hindsight).
- Managers must **justify** resolutions, conclusions and recommendations, not just communicate clearly.

### 3.1.2 Understanding Persuasive Communication

#### Key Concept: Five Ideas of the Module
1. **Rhetorical appeals**
2. **Building blocks** of an argument (claim, evidence, reasoning)
3. **Deductive vs inductive** reasoning
4. **Toulmin model**
5. Arguments across **professional contexts**

- Some arguments feel convincing even when weak; others fail despite good evidence.
- **Data rarely speaks for itself**: the audience decides whether data is trustworthy, whether it matters and whether it justifies the recommendation.
- **Persuasion begins before the logic is fully evaluated.**

### 3.1.3 Evaluating Arguments Through Rhetorical Appeals

#### Definition: Aristotle's Three Modes of Persuasion
- **Aristotle** (ancient Athens, 2,000+ years ago) observed persuasion in courts and the assembly; in his treatise on **rhetoric** named three modes.
- People listen because they **trust the speaker**, **care about the issue**, and the **reasoning makes sense**.

| Appeal | Basis | Key question | Essence |
|--------|-------|--------------|---------|
| **Ethos** | Credibility | **Why should I trust you?** | Experience, expertise, reputation, transparency |
| **Pathos** | Emotion | **Why does this matter?** | Helps audience see what is at stake |
| **Logos** | Logic | **Why should I believe the conclusion follows from the evidence?** | Connects evidence logically to a recommendation |

#### Key Concept: Ethos
- Audiences make a **quick trust judgment** before evaluating evidence.
- Example: same investment advice from a friend with no financial background vs an analyst with 20 years' experience: **same evidence, different source**; the analyst is trusted.
- Credibility can be **earned through behaviour**; **admitting uncertainty** or **acknowledging mistakes** can increase trust.
- **Patagonia "Don't Buy This Jacket"** (Black Friday, full-page newspaper ad):
  - Explained hidden environmental costs of production (water, energy, raw materials).
  - Contained **very little technical/statistical evidence**.
  - Persuasive because it **strengthened ethos**: acting **consistently with its environmental values**, even at the risk of lower sales.

#### Key Concept: Pathos
- **Emotion is almost always part of decision-making** (even choosing coffee or chai).
- Employees rarely support organisational change because a spreadsheet says so; they support it because they **understand what is at stake**.
- Pathos is **not manipulation**; it helps the audience see **why an issue deserves attention**.

#### Key Concept: Logos
- Too many charts, graphics or statistics **without a convincing argument** can overwhelm an audience.
- Good logos **connects data logically** to a recommendation and **explains why the facts support** the conclusion.
- Logic is **only one part** of persuasion; strong evidence struggles without trust (ethos) or relevance (pathos).

#### Key Concept: Amazon Prime (All Three Appeals)
- Customers were asked to pay an **annual fee upfront** for benefits not yet experienced.

| Appeal | How Amazon Prime used it |
|--------|--------------------------|
| **Ethos** | Mid-2000s reputation for reliability, on-time delivery, fair returns |
| **Logos** | Simple economic calculation: frequent orders make the subscription pay for itself; pay once, save repeatedly |
| **Pathos** | Selling **convenience**: no hesitation over delivery charges, no waiting, a smoother, less frustrating experience |

- Success was **not inevitable**; **no single appeal would have been enough alone**: the right **combination and balance** was needed.

#### Key Concept: Generative AI and Rhetorical Appeals
- **Ethos:** Do you trust it because it **sounds confident**, or have you **independently verified sources**?
- **Pathos:** AI generates emotionally engaging language well, but that does **not make it more accurate**.
- **Logos:** Does the reasoning **genuinely connect** evidence to conclusion, or merely place plausible facts beside a plausible recommendation?
- **Fluency can create an illusion of expertise.**
- **A well-written argument is not automatically a good argument.**
- The three appeals are **interconnected**, not separate ingredients.
- The three questions reveal **how** an argument persuades, not whether it is correct: **persuasion and proof are not the same**.

## 3.2 Structure and Reasoning of Arguments

### 3.2.1 Building Blocks of an Argument

#### Key Concept: Persuaded vs Convinced
- **Being persuaded is not the same as being convinced**: you can trust the speaker and care about the issue yet face a weak argument.
- Every argument has three parts; missing any one makes it **much weaker**.

| Part | Definition | Key point |
|------|-----------|-----------|
| **Claim** | Conclusion the speaker wants the audience to believe or do | May be **stated** or **implied** |
| **Evidence** | Information offered in support of the claim | Must be **relevant**; can be quantitative or qualitative |
| **Reasoning** | Explanation of **why** the evidence supports the claim | The **logical bridge**; most often skipped |

#### Key Concept: Claim
- Explicit examples: "We should launch this product", "Enter the Indonesian market", "This campaign was successful".
- **Implied claims:** advertisements rarely say "buy this"; the conclusion is understood.
- If the audience must guess the claim, their job becomes harder.
- Test: **"What exactly is the author asking me to believe or do?"** An unclear answer signals an unclear argument.

#### Key Concept: Evidence
- Includes financial data, customer research, market reports, experiments, surveys.
- **Not limited to numbers**: interviews and observations are evidence (a cancellation interview can reveal more than sales figures).
- **Relevance:** Bangalore employee satisfaction scores do **not** support opening a Hyderabad office, even if accurate.
- Evidence is **information that helps answer the question under discussion**.

#### Key Concept: Reasoning
- "Customer satisfaction increased by 15%" is **evidence**; alone it could support many claims (service improved, redesign worked, lower prices).
- Without reasoning, evidence is **facts sitting beside a conclusion**; the audience may draw a **different connection**.
- **AI customer support example:**

| Element | Content |
|---------|---------|
| **Claim** | Use AI to handle first-level customer inquiries |
| **Evidence** | 3-month pilot: response time 12 min to 1 min; support costs down 35%; satisfaction unchanged |
| **Reasoning** | If routine inquiries are handled faster at lower cost without reducing satisfaction, automation improves efficiency while maintaining quality |

- Reasoning **adds no new evidence**; it explains **why** evidence leads to the conclusion.

#### Key Concept: Evidence-Claim Mismatch
- "Customers love our app" supported by "1 million downloads" is a mismatch.
- Downloads show people **were willing to try** the app, not that they **loved** it (may have deleted it, left poor reviews, downloaded for a discount).
- **AI Vietnam example:** GDP growth, internet penetration, middle-class incomes, digital infrastructure lead to "enter Vietnam": missing reasoning (product fit, regulation, competitor dominance).
- **Confidence is not reasoning.** Ask not only "Are the facts correct?" but **"How exactly do these facts support this conclusion?"**

### 3.2.2 Types of Reasoning in Business

#### Definition: Deductive vs Inductive Reasoning

| Feature | **Deductive** | **Inductive** |
|---------|---------------|---------------|
| Direction | **General rule to specific case** | **Observations to general conclusion** |
| Output | **Certainty** (if premises true and applied correctly) | **Probability** only |
| Interpretation | Very little; conclusion built into the rule | Open to revision with new evidence |
| Key question | Does it **necessarily follow**? | How **likely** is it? |
| Evaluate by | **Validity** of the logic | **Strength** of evidence and alternative explanations |
| Business use | Compliance, financial reporting, tax, employment law, internal policies | Strategy, market research, surveys, forecasts, trends |

#### Key Concept: Deductive Examples
- All employees must complete cybersecurity training to access systems; Priya has not; **therefore Priya cannot access the systems**.
- Every expense above Rs 50,000 needs board approval; proposal is Rs 75,000; **therefore it needs board approval**.
- **AI can operate deductively:** fraud detection rule, e.g., transactions above a value from an unfamiliar location require verification.

#### Key Concept: Inductive Examples
- Electric scooter sales rose every year for 5 years; demand is **likely** to keep growing (breakthroughs, policy change, recession could alter the trend).
- **Most business decisions are inductive**: managers use patterns to predict what is likely.
- New store decision: study similar locations, population growth, income, traffic, purchasing behaviour: success becomes **more or less probable, never certain**.
- **Plant-based foods:** six quarters of rising sales, surveys, social media awareness lead to more shelf space; could fail due to fad, inflation or shifting preferences. **Reasonable, not certain.**
- An inductive conclusion being overturned **does not mean the reasoning was poor**.

### 3.2.3 Reasoning in Business and AI

#### Key Concept: AI Works Inductively
- Generative AI predicts the next word through **probabilistic predictions** based on **patterns** in vast text, not rigid rules.
- Identifies **statistical regularities**, explaining both:
  - **Strength:** fluent, quick responses
  - **Weakness:** convincing but **incorrect or hallucinated** statements
- The model **is not lying**; it **extends a pattern beyond where evidence supports it**, a risk of induction itself.

#### Key Concept: UPI and Digital Payments
- Decade of growth in UPI transactions, smartphone adoption, merchant acceptance, QR code comfort.
- Retail CEO in 2021: design future stores around digital payments rather than cash: **inductive** (pattern-based prediction); turned out largely correct, yet uncertain.

#### Key Concept: Certain vs Probable
- People often expect the **wrong level of certainty**.
- "The data **proves** customers will buy" should prompt a pause; business data rarely proves anything.

| Deductive-sounding language (overclaims) | Inductive language (appropriate) |
|------------------------------------------|----------------------------------|
| proves, clearly demonstrates, guarantees | suggests, supports, indicates, makes likely |

- Using deductive language for inductive conclusions makes the argument **sound stronger than it is**; confidence comes from **language, not logic**.
- Key test: **"Is this actually certain or simply probable?"**
- Recognising uncertainty makes business thinking **more honest**, not weaker.

### 3.2.4 The Toulmin Model in Legal Arguments

#### Definition: Toulmin Model
- Developed by philosopher **Stephen E. Toulmin**; a structured method for **analysing and building** arguments based on how people argue in everyday life (boardrooms, courtrooms, newspapers, science).
- Most effective when there are **no clear answers**.
- Does not assume arguments are perfect; identifies pieces **whether stated or not**; business arguments often leave 1 or 2 unstated.
- Shifts the question from **"Do I agree?"** to **"Which part is weak?"**
- **Six parts:** three **fundamental** (claim, grounds, warrant) and three **optional** (backing, qualifier, rebuttal).

| Element | Type | Definition | Key point |
|---------|------|-----------|-----------|
| **Claim** | Fundamental | Conclusion / main argument the author wants to prove | Everything else supports it |
| **Grounds** | Fundamental | Evidence: facts, statistics, examples | Observations; do not automatically prove anything |
| **Warrant** | Fundamental | Reasoning linking grounds to claim | **Most important, most forgotten**; may be explicit or implied |
| **Backing** | Optional | Support for the **warrant** itself | Studies, industry reports, longitudinal evidence; goes beyond claim-evidence-reasoning |
| **Qualifier** | Optional | Indicates **strength/limits** of the claim | "probably", "likely", "some", "many", "presumably", "in most cases" |
| **Rebuttal** | Optional | Conditions under which the argument **no longer holds** | Addresses counter-arguments; builds credibility |

#### Key Concept: Four-Day Workweek Example

| Element | Content |
|---------|---------|
| **Claim** | Company should introduce a 4-day workweek |
| **Grounds** | Productivity up 12% in a 6-month trial; sick leave down; retention improved |
| **Warrant** | If productivity rises while well-being improves, the schedule benefits both organisation and staff |
| **Backing** | Answers "Was the gain temporary?": peer-reviewed studies, industry reports, longitudinal evidence from other firms |

- Without the warrant, evidence and conclusion merely **sit next to each other**.

#### Key Concept: Microsoft Japan Example

| Element | Content |
|---------|---------|
| **Claim** | Microsoft should continue exploring flexible work arrangements |
| **Grounds** | Higher productivity, shorter meetings, lower electricity/operational costs, greater satisfaction (4-day week, full salary) |
| **Warrant** | Improving productivity, cutting costs and raising well-being means the schedule change benefits the business |
| **Backing** | Organisational psychology research linking well-being, motivation and performance |

#### Key Concept: Qualifier
- Business recommendations often **sound more certain than they are**.
- Qualifiers **do not weaken** arguments; they make them **more honest**.
- Doctor saying "definitely works" is suspicious; "based on available evidence, a very high success rate" is trustworthy.
- **Avoid "always" and "never"**: absolute claims are hard to prove.
- **Confidence is valuable; overconfidence is not.**

#### Key Concept: Rebuttal
- Asks **"When would I be wrong?"**: requires being critical of one's own thinking.
- AI customer service rebuttal: assumes routine inquiries stay simple; complex questions or regulation may need human oversight; savings may not materialise.
- Identifying limitations first makes the recommendation **more credible**, showing **intellectual honesty**.

#### Key Concept: Toulmin Handout Example (Ticket Scalping)
- **Claim:** scalping may be efficient in selling tickets but not effective for customer satisfaction or firm profit.
- **Grounds:** Depken's MLB/NFL study; scalper buys at $145 and may resell for over $1000.
- **Warrant (explicit):** definitions of **efficiency** (resources allocated to those best served, minimising waste) and **effectiveness** (comparison of a firm's costs and profit).
- **Backing:** firm could be stuck with surplus tickets without scalping (Swofford).
- **Rebuttal:** resale companies help fans who missed the on-sale; but they exist because scalpers and bots made buying hard.

#### Key Concept: Arguments Across Professions
- Each profession has its own standards for **convincing evidence**, **expected reasoning** and **acceptable uncertainty**.
- **The skeleton stays similar; the cladding changes.**

| Context | Feature |
|---------|---------|
| **Legal** | Built by advocates to persuade judges/juries; rarely disagree on **facts**, disagree on **what facts mean**; emphasis on **warrants** grounded in **established legal principles/precedent** |
| **Scientific** | Almost never claims absolute certainty; cautious phrases ("evidence suggests", "results indicate", "findings are consistent with"); **intellectually honest and fundamentally inductive** |

- Example: two lawyers, same accident facts: one argues **negligence**, the other **reasonable conduct**: the disagreement is in **interpretation (warrant)**.

## 3.3 Evaluating Arguments

### 3.3.1 Validity vs Soundness

#### Definition: Validity
- An argument is **valid** when, **if the premises are true, the conclusion must be true**.
- About the **relationship between premises and conclusion**: **form, not content**.
- Says nothing about whether premises are true in the real world.
- **Test:** Is there any possible way for premises to be true and conclusion false? **No = valid; Yes = invalid.**

#### Definition: Soundness
- **Sound = valid + all premises actually true.** The **deductive gold standard**.

$$\text{Sound} = \text{Valid structure} + \text{True premises}$$

#### Key Concept: Dolphin Example
- "All fish live in water. A dolphin lives in water. Therefore, a dolphin is a fish."
- **Invalid**: many non-fish live in water (otters), so the conclusion does not follow; hence **cannot be sound**.
- **Every sound argument is valid, but not every valid argument is sound.**
- Valid-but-unsound arguments are common in **marketing, casual arguments and political rhetoric**.
- Checking only whether the conclusion follows, without checking premises, is **half the job**.

### 3.3.2 Strength vs Cogency

#### Definition: Strength
- An inductive argument is **strong** when, if premises are true, the conclusion is **probably** true.
- **Strength comes in degrees.**
- Like validity, it concerns the **premise-conclusion relationship**; a strong argument can rest on **false premises**.

| Argument | Strength |
|----------|----------|
| Every crow I have ever seen is black, so all crows are black | **Moderately strong** |
| I saw one black crow yesterday, so all crows are black | **Very weak** (thin evidence base) |
| All lottery winners announce within 24 hours; nobody announced; probably nobody has claimed | **Strong structure**, even if premise is false |

#### Definition: Cogency
- **Cogent = strong + premises true or well supported.** The **inductive equivalent of soundness** and the **inductive gold standard**.

$$\text{Cogent} = \text{Strong structure} + \text{True / well-supported premises}$$

| Family | Claims | Structure test | Structure + true premises |
|--------|--------|----------------|---------------------------|
| **Deductive** | Certainty | **Validity** | **Soundness** |
| **Inductive** | Probability | **Strength** | **Cogency** |

- Example of inductive argument: it rained every day this week in Bangalore during monsoon, so it will probably rain tomorrow.

### 3.3.3 Evaluating Evidence

#### Key Concept: Five Evidence Tests
- Evaluating evidence is **messier and judgment-based** compared with checking validity.

| Test | Question | Example / red flag |
|------|----------|--------------------|
| **Relevance** | Does it bear on the claim? | "Great camera" is not evidence the phone is a **good investment**; weak arguments are often **true but beside the point** |
| **Sufficiency** | Is there enough? | One black crow cannot support a claim about all crows; **size and diversity** matter; "most people say" is a **cherry-picked** sample |
| **Credibility** | Is the source credible and unbiased? | Check **expertise, track record, bias/conflict of interest**; company-funded health study is **not automatically wrong** but deserves **more scrutiny** |
| **Currency** | Is it current? | 15-year-old internet statistics or later-revised medical studies may now **mislead** |
| **Counter-evidence** | Does it hold up against contrary evidence? | Ignoring contrary evidence is **cherry picking**: a **huge red flag** |

### 3.3.4 Integrated Evaluation Procedure

| Step | Action |
|------|--------|
| 1 | Identify the type: claims **certainty** (deductive) or **probability** (inductive)? |
| 2a | Certainty: check **validity**, then **soundness** |
| 2b | Probability: check **strength**, then **cogency** |
| 3 | Apply the **five evidence tests** to every premise doing real work |

- The aim is **not cynicism**; it is telling apart an argument that **earns agreement** from one that only **sounds** like it does.
- Core distinction: **feeling persuaded vs being justifiably persuaded**.

## Quick Revision Summary

### Key Definitions
- **Argument:** claim + evidence + reasoning; justifies decisions (stories capture attention).
- **Ethos / Pathos / Logos:** credibility (trust you?) / emotion (why it matters?) / logic (does it follow?).
- **Claim:** conclusion to believe or act on; may be implied.
- **Evidence (Grounds):** information supporting the claim; must be relevant.
- **Reasoning (Warrant):** logical bridge explaining why evidence supports claim.
- **Backing:** support for the warrant. **Qualifier:** strength of the claim. **Rebuttal:** when the argument fails.
- **Deductive:** general to specific, certain. **Inductive:** observations to general, probable.
- **Valid / Sound / Strong / Cogent:** see formulas below.

### Important Formulas
$$\text{Argument} = \text{Claim} + \text{Evidence} + \text{Reasoning}$$
$$\text{Sound} = \text{Valid} + \text{True premises}$$
$$\text{Cogent} = \text{Strong} + \text{True / well-supported premises}$$

### Must-Remember Points
- Netflix 2006: **evidence alone was not enough**; reasoning persuaded; the story came after the argument.
- Patagonia = **ethos** via consistency with values; Amazon Prime = **all three appeals combined**.
- **Persuasion is not proof**; fluency can create an **illusion of expertise**.
- **Most business decisions and AI text generation are inductive**; AI hallucination = extending a pattern beyond the evidence.
- "Proves/guarantees" on inductive evidence **overclaims**.
- Toulmin: 3 fundamental (claim, grounds, warrant) + 3 optional (backing, qualifier, rebuttal); warrant is the most forgotten.
- Legal arguments dispute **warrants (interpretation)**, not facts; scientific arguments are cautious and inductive.
- Every sound argument is valid; not every valid argument is sound.
- Evidence tests: **relevant, sufficient, credible, current, survives counter-evidence**.

# Module 4: Fallacies and Cognitive Biases

## 4.0 Learning Objectives

### 4.0.1 Module Objectives

#### Key Concept
- **Identify** common fallacies and cognitive biases affecting reasoning and business judgement
- **Apply** critical thinking techniques to detect flawed reasoning and cognitive shortcuts
- **Evaluate** evidence, assumptions and persuasive messages before accepting conclusions or decisions
- **Compare** fast and slow thinking across different decision-making contexts in business

## 4.1 Warrant Failures, Not Just Bad Arguments

### 4.1.1 Parts of an Argument

#### Definition
| Part | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Claim** | What someone wants you to believe |
| **Evidence** | The data or facts given |
| **Warrant** | The reasoning that **connects the evidence to the claim** |

- **Deductive** arguments are judged as **valid / sound**; **inductive** arguments as **strong / cogent**

### 4.1.2 Fallacy as a Broken Warrant

#### Definition
- **Fallacy:** occurs when the **link (warrant) between evidence and conclusion is not strong enough**
- Most bad business decisions happen **not** from missing evidence or false claims, but from a **break in the warrant**
- Business decisions are made quickly, driven by **gut instinct, confidence and persuasive language**
- Fallacies **sound reasonable** because they tap into natural, intuitive ways of thinking

| Fallacy | How it sounds |
|---------|---------------|
| **Hasty generalisation** | Sounds like common sense |
| **Appeal to authority** | Sounds like humility ("Who am I to question the CEO?") |
| **Slippery slope** | Sounds like sensible risk management ("If we allow this, where does it stop?") |

#### Key Concept: Hybrid Work Example
- Evidence: team productivity **fell 10%** after moving to hybrid work (evidence is **real**)
- Claim: "Remote work is hurting productivity; everyone back to office"
- Alternative explanations: two key employees left, a difficult project, changed performance metric, delays elsewhere
- The **problem is the warrant** - the assumption that "fell after hybrid" means "caused by hybrid"
- A conclusion **being right does not make the reasoning sound**
- **Core question for every fallacy:** not "Is the evidence true or false?" but **"Where exactly does the warrant break?"**

## 4.2 Attack and Distortion Fallacies

### 4.2.1 Common Feature

#### Key Concept
- Fallacies in the **claim** itself; they **shift focus away from the argument**
- Attack the person, twist the argument, or reduce options to two
- In all three, the **evidence is pushed to the background**
- Rule: evaluate the **argument, not the person**, and the argument **as actually made** (not distorted, exaggerated or artificially narrowed)

### 4.2.2 Ad Hominem

#### Definition
- **Ad hominem:** attacking the **person making the argument** instead of dealing with the argument
- Common where there are strong personalities or internal politics; hierarchy and experience create the **illusion that status is enough**

#### Key Concept: Examples
- **Kodak (late 1990s-2000s):** advocates of digital photography dismissed as inexperienced / not understanding the film-and-chemicals business; Kodak had invented the **first digital camera in 1975** - it had evidence and capability; failure lay in how arguments were received and **who** made them
- **Churn analyst:** 6-month analyst shows flagship product has highest churn in newest segment; senior manager says "You've only been here 5 minutes" - challenges the **person**, not the numbers
- An argument does **not** become weaker because we dislike, distrust or disrespect the person

#### Key Concept: Fallacy-free Response
- Test the data: How was churn calculated? Is the sample large enough? How was the segment defined? Any other explanation?
- Question is **"Is it true and does the evidence support the claim?"**, not **"Who said it?"**

### 4.2.3 Straw Man

#### Definition
- **Straw man:** taking someone's actual argument, changing it into a **weaker or more extreme version**, and arguing against that instead

#### Key Concept: Examples
| Actual proposal | Straw man response |
|-----------------|--------------------|
| Hybrid: office 2-3 days a week | "So you want everyone to work from home all the time?" |
| Remove 3 underused features to reduce technical debt | "So you want to strip down the product to nothing and drive away power users?" |

- Check: **put the original argument next to the response - are they talking about the same thing?** If no, it is a straw man
- You cannot make a good decision arguing against a position **nobody proposed**
- **Fallacy-free response:** restate accurately, identify potential problem, ask for evidence (e.g., "Who uses those features? Are they high-value customers? Cost to maintain vs revenue lost?")

### 4.2.4 False Dichotomy

#### Definition
- **False dichotomy:** presenting a situation as having **only two choices** when several others exist
- Convincing because it **creates urgency**; common under pressure, uncertainty and limited time

#### Key Concept: Examples
| Dichotomy | Missed alternatives |
|-----------|---------------------|
| "Cut prices or lose the market" | Bundle differently, loyalty programme, compete on service |
| "All-in on AI features this quarter or become irrelevant" | Smaller AI pilot, AI in one high-value part, targeted feature |
| Move fast or get left behind; cut costs or lose money | - |

- **Fallacy-free response:** "I'm not sure those are the only 2 options" - study whether AI affects customer choice, where it creates value, what competitors do; then consider several responses
- Habit: when given two choices, first ask **"Are these the only real choices?"**

### 4.2.5 Comparison

#### Key Concept
| Fallacy | What it does |
|---------|--------------|
| **Ad hominem** | Shifts attention from argument **to the person** |
| **Straw man** | Replaces argument with a **distorted version** |
| **False dichotomy** | **Narrows choices** before options are considered |

## 4.3 Causal Reasoning Fallacies

### 4.3.1 Nature of Causal Fallacies

#### Key Concept
- Fallacies about **cause and effect**; linked to inductive reasoning and causal claims
- Minds spot patterns and jump to conclusions: two things together = one caused the other; confident expert = truth; one decision = inevitable chain
- **Seeing a connection is not the same as establishing a cause**
- They look like good reasoning (data, trend, expert, sequence) yet the conclusion is a leap

### 4.3.2 Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc

#### Definition
- **Post hoc ergo propter hoc** (Latin): **"After this, therefore because of this"** - Y happened after X, so X caused Y
- Example: startup launches Instagram campaign, website traffic goes up, campaign assumed to be the cause
- Corrective: when data tells a neat story, ask **"What else changed at the same time?"**

### 4.3.3 Appeal to Authority

#### Definition
- **Appeal to authority:** accepting a claim **mainly because someone is important, successful or highly qualified**; a shortcut, tempting in startups

#### Key Concept: Examples
- Founder: "I've built 3 companies. Trust me. Customers won't pay more than Rs 499" - experience is **not** evidence that Rs 499 is the right price
- Needed: customer data, willingness-to-pay research, competitor pricing, conversion rates at price points, pricing experiment
- "Our senior data scientist says the model is accurate, so no need to question it" - experts make mistakes; **expertise in one area is not expertise in another**
- Right question: **Is this person an expert in this particular question, and what evidence supports the claim?**
- **Fallacy-free response:** do not dismiss experience; ask "What makes you think that?" / "Can we test it against customer data?"
- **Expertise tells us where to look**, but should not stop us examining evidence

### 4.3.4 Slippery Slope

#### Definition
- **Slippery slope:** assuming one relatively **small step will inevitably lead** to a chain of increasingly serious consequences
- Problem is **not** that consequences could happen, but assuming they **must** happen without showing why each step leads to the next

#### Key Concept: Example
- "2 remote days -> fully remote demand -> productivity falls -> culture collapses"
- Each link is **possible, but possible is not inevitable**
- Connects to **necessary and sufficient conditions** when linking cause and effect

## 4.4 Sampling Fallacies

### 4.4.1 Focus on Evidence

#### Key Concept
- Shift from argument construction to the **evidence itself** - how much and what kind
- Inductive reasoning gives **no certainty**; strength depends on how confidently we can generalise
- Reasonable logic + tiny or **unrepresentative sample** = misleading conclusion

### 4.4.2 Hasty Generalisation

#### Definition
- **Hasty generalisation:** taking a **small or unusual sample** and making a much bigger claim than the evidence supports

#### Key Concept: New Coke
- Popular myth: tested on few people; actually **about 2 lakh people** in blind taste tests; sweeter New Coke performed well, yet failed
- Test measured **taste preference**, not **brand attachment, nostalgia, identity** or reaction to a familiar product disappearing
- Two different questions: "Which taste do you prefer?" vs "Will you replace the Coke you know and love?"
- Coca-Cola returned to the old formula
- Lesson: **make sure the evidence actually represents the behaviour you are trying to predict** (not merely "don't trust small samples")

### 4.4.3 Anecdotal Evidence

#### Definition
- **Anecdotal evidence:** treating **one person's experience (or a handful)** as evidence of a broader pattern
- Entrepreneurs vulnerable: "I had this problem, so others must too" - a fine starting point, but experience is a **sample size of one**, not proof of a market

#### Key Concept: Rule for Both Sampling Fallacies
- **A story can give you a question, a sample can give you evidence, but neither automatically gives a conclusion**
- Ask **"How much can this evidence actually tell me?"** and "Is it enough and representative enough?" - not merely "Is it true?"

## 4.5 Reasoning Shortcuts About Change

### 4.5.1 Appeal to Nature

#### Definition
- **Appeal to nature:** something is better or more correct **simply because it is natural, organic** or original
- Example: "Organic growth is **always** healthier than acquisitions" - "always" does a lot of work; **Facebook's acquisitions of Instagram and WhatsApp** were key to its growth
- Right question: does this strategy make sense **for this business, in this market, now?**

### 4.5.2 Appeal to Tradition

#### Definition
- **Appeal to tradition:** **"We've always done it this way"**, so there must be a good reason to continue
- Example: **annual performance reviews**; **Adobe and Microsoft** changed their approaches during the **2010s**
- Tradition tells **what has been done**, not **whether it works**
- Follow-up question: **"Is this still working?"**

### 4.5.3 Appeal to Novelty

#### Definition
- **Appeal to novelty:** mirror image of tradition - **new is better simply because it is new**
- Example: "Newest AI-powered platform, so it must outperform our system" - need **benchmarks**, compared results, and whether it solves the problem better than alternatives

### 4.5.4 Common Corrective

#### Key Concept
| Shortcut | Label used | Question to ask |
|----------|-----------|-----------------|
| Nature | Natural / organic | Does it fit this context? |
| Tradition | Old / established | Is it still working? |
| Novelty | New | What do benchmarks show? |

- **Do not judge the idea by the label**; ask what the evidence shows

## 4.6 Cognitive Biases and the Psychology of Bad Reasoning

### 4.6.1 Turning the Lens Inward

#### Key Concept
- Spotting flawed reasoning in others is easy; in ourselves it is hard - brains take **shortcuts without our awareness**
- Being smart or well-informed **does not make us immune**
- Smart people's bad decisions are **not a character flaw or lack of intelligence** but **human cognitive architecture**
- Professionals fail because they trust a **gut feeling that felt like certainty** but was a bias

### 4.6.2 Five Ideas Covered

#### Key Concept
| No. | Idea | Source |
|-----|------|--------|
| 1 | **System 1 and System 2** | Daniel Kahneman |
| 2 | **Confirmation bias** | Kahneman |
| 3 | **Availability heuristic** | Kahneman |
| 4 | **Anchoring** | Kahneman |
| 5 | **Propaganda and manufactured consent** (with in-group bias, social proof) | Biases engineered at scale |

## 4.7 Thinking Fast and Slow: The Two Systems

### 4.7.1 System 1 and System 2

#### Definition
- From Kahneman's **Thinking, Fast and Slow**: two modes, like two characters in one head

| Feature | **System 1** | **System 2** |
|---------|-------------|-------------|
| Speed | **Fast, automatic** | **Slow, deliberate** |
| Effort | Almost **no effort**; never feels like a choice | **Effortful**, gets tired, **lazy by default** |
| Examples | Angry face perceived instantly; 2+2=4; driving on empty road; reading tension on a client's face; "bread and ... butter" | 17 x 24 in head; comparing two job offers; DCF model; parking in a tight spot |
| Role | **Default author of most judgments** | Switches on only when System 1 cannot cope or senses error; often **rubber-stamps** System 1 |

### 4.7.2 Why It Matters in Business

#### Key Concept
- Business runs on **uncertainty** (ambiguous signals, incomplete data) - exactly where System 1 takes over
- System 1 **fills gaps** and creates confident answers - constructs the **best possible story from limited evidence**, then fully believes it
- Example: manager declares "We've cracked this account" after **one client meeting**; System 1 wants a story **now**, not statistical significance

### 4.7.3 Intuition: When to Trust It

#### Key Concept
- **Intuition is not inherently wrong** - experienced professionals (seasoned investor, ops head with 15 years) have **well-calibrated System 1** via **pattern recognition**
- Problem: **not knowing when to trust it and when to override it**
- **Pricing, hiring, walking away from a deal** need System 2, yet fatigue / time pressure tempt us to let System 1 decide
- Every bias = **System 1 taking a shortcut + System 2 too tired, rushed or willing to go along**

## 4.8 Confirmation Bias

### 4.8.1 Definition and Mechanism

#### Definition
- **Confirmation bias:** tendency to **search for, notice, interpret and remember** information that confirms existing beliefs, and **overlook, dismiss or explain away** contradicting information
- After System 1's first impression, System 2 looks for reasons the **first impression is right**
- Built-in bias toward **believing and confirming** rather than doubting and disconfirming; default = accept a story first
- Not laziness but **cognitive efficiency** - confirming is easier than disconfirming (which needs imagining how you might be wrong)

### 4.8.2 Business Examples

#### Key Concept
- **Market research:** leading questions ("Wouldn't you love a product that does X, Y, Z?") instead of neutral ones
- **Hiring:** snap judgment in **first 90 seconds**, rest of interview collects supporting evidence
- Emotional investment in an idea makes us seek reasons it will work

### 4.8.3 Antidote

#### Key Concept
- **Not willpower** - bias operates before awareness
- Antidote is **structural**: a habit/process of **actively seeking disconfirming evidence**
- Ask: **"What evidence would prove me wrong, and have I looked for it?"**
- Formally assign someone to play **devil's advocate** on every major recommendation

## 4.9 Availability Heuristic

### 4.9.1 Definition and Mechanism

#### Definition
- **Availability heuristic:** System 1 **substitutes** "How likely/frequent is it?" with **"How easily can I recall an example?"** and treats **ease of recall** as the answer
- Ease of recall depends on **recency, vividness, media coverage, memorability** - not real-world probability

### 4.9.2 Examples

#### Key Concept
- **Plane crash vs car accident:** plane crashes feel riskier (news coverage, vivid images); data shows **more people die in car accidents** (common, dispersed, rarely national news)
- **Zerodha / Nithin Kamath:** vivid success story stays available; quiet failures never made headlines - **distorted sense of odds**

### 4.9.3 Antidote

#### Key Concept
- Ask: **"Am I basing this on actual data or the example that comes to mind fastest?"**
- **Replace vivid anecdotes with actual base rates** - what the broader data says

## 4.10 Anchoring Bias

### 4.10.1 Definition

#### Definition
- **Anchoring:** an **initial number**, even **arbitrary and logically unconnected**, influences a **subsequent numerical estimate**; the mind clings to it
- One of the **most well-replicated findings** in behavioural science

#### Key Concept: Spinning Wheel Experiment
- Wheel **rigged** to stop on low or high number; then asked to estimate **percentage of African countries** in a region
- High-number viewers gave **higher** estimates; low-number viewers gave **lower** estimates

### 4.10.2 Business Anchors

#### Key Concept
- **First number** in salary negotiation, funding round or pricing discussion carries outsized weight; **whoever states the first figure sets the anchor**, and counteroffers are pulled toward it
- Client's early **low budget**; **competitor's pricing page**; **first-draft revenue projection** anchoring 3-5 year expectations
- **Awareness reduces but does not eliminate** anchoring - even warned **judges and real-estate agents** showed measurable pull

### 4.10.3 Antidotes

#### Key Concept
| No. | Antidote |
|-----|----------|
| 1 | **Set the anchor yourself** - thoughtfully, with justification, not to manipulate |
| 2 | **Privately write your own independent estimate** before hearing the other side's opening number (pre-commitment) |
| 3 | Generate **multiple independent estimates** from many people and starting points before converging |

## 4.11 Propaganda and Manufactured Consent

### 4.11.1 In-group Bias and Social Proof

#### Definition
| Bias | Definition | Link to earlier bias |
|------|-----------|----------------------|
| **In-group bias** | Favouring, trusting, giving benefit of doubt to people in **our group** (same school, college, function, industry, background); sceptical of outsiders | **Confirmation bias at scale** |
| **Social proof** | Treating what others (similar / respected people) do or believe as **evidence of what is correct**, instead of evaluating evidence | **Availability heuristic applied to consensus** |

#### Key Concept: Corporate Examples
- Manager weighs feedback from a colleague with the **same educational background**; outsider's accurate critique dismissed
- Leadership team all from the **same business school** - **unconscious echo chamber**; confidence comes from peers reinforcing, not **independently earned**
- Industry-level: "**recurring revenue is always superior to one-time sales**" becomes unquestioned wisdom through **repetition and social reinforcement**, not testing
- Lesson: notice whether confidence comes from **independent evidence** or the comfort of agreement

### 4.11.2 Propaganda

#### Definition
- **Propaganda:** a **deliberate, systematic attempt to shape perceptions, opinions and behaviour** toward a conclusion, **not through open argument and evidence** but by **engineering cognitive shortcuts**
- Earlier biases happen **by accident**; propaganda uses them **deliberately at scale**
- **Edward Herman and Noam Chomsky**, **propaganda model**, **Manufacturing Consent (1988)**: institutions typically do **not lie outright**; they decide what counts as reasonable debate, which voices are credible experts, which facts are repeated and which get **buried**

### 4.11.3 Biases Behind Propaganda Techniques

#### Key Concept
| Technique | Works because of |
|-----------|------------------|
| **Repetition** | **Availability heuristic** - repeated = easier to recall = feels truer |
| **Selective framing** | **Confirmation bias** |
| **Trusted spokespeople** | **In-group bias** |
| **Opening frame / narrative** | **Anchoring** |

### 4.11.4 In Business

#### Key Concept
- **As audience:** industry reports funded by companies they favour; thought leadership just before a fundraising round; analyst notes repeating a company's talking points as independent findings
- **As producer:** layoffs framed as **"right sizing for the future"**; scripted town hall making employees feel they made decisions; **board deck leading with the one metric trending up** (anchoring device) while hiding declining ones

### 4.11.5 Legitimate Persuasion vs Manufactured Consent

#### Key Concept
| Aspect | **Legitimate persuasion** | **Manufactured consent** |
|--------|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| Basis | **Genuine evidence and reasoning** | **Selective framing, repetition, borrowed authority** |
| Scrutiny | **Stands up to scrutiny**; holds even when challenged | Depends on audience **not seeing the full picture** |
| Outcome | Informs a decision | Engineers agreement, bypasses judgment |

- Techniques themselves are **neither good nor bad**; what matters is the **intent** and **what evidence is left out**
- **Receiver takeaway:** ask **"What would this narrative need to leave out to remain this convincing?"**
- **Producer takeaway:** know which lever (bias/fallacy) you are pulling; are you **informing or manufacturing** a decision?

## 4.13 Summary

### 4.13.1 Connecting the Five Ideas

#### Key Concept
| Element | Role |
|---------|------|
| **System 1** | Wants a fast, coherent, confident answer |
| **Confirmation bias** | Protects / reinforces that answer |
| **Availability heuristic** | Supplies vivid examples that build the answer |
| **Anchoring** | Sets the starting point |
| **Social proof** | People around us reinforce it; shaky answer feels solid |
| **Propaganda** | Someone else pulls all these levers **deliberately and repeatedly** |

- Expert intuition built over years is **real and valuable**
- **Higher stakes, uncertainty or persuasive pressure = more need for deliberate pauses** (devil's advocate, pre-written independent estimate, search for what is missing)
- Professional edge = **knowing when not to trust instincts**, and spotting stories **too familiar or too convenient** to be true

### 4.13.2 Module Coverage

#### Key Concept
- **Warrant failures** - weak links between evidence and claims create misleading conclusions
- **Attack and causal fallacies** - distorted arguments vs unsupported cause-and-effect
- **Sampling and change fallacies** - assess evidence; question tradition, novelty, nature
- **Cognitive biases and persuasion** - shortcuts, anchoring, confirmation, availability, manufactured consent

## Quick Revision Summary

### Key Definitions
- **Warrant:** reasoning linking evidence to claim; **fallacy** = broken warrant
- **Ad hominem:** attack the person; **Straw man:** distort the argument; **False dichotomy:** only two options
- **Post hoc:** after this, therefore because of this; **Appeal to authority:** believe because of status; **Slippery slope:** small step = inevitable chain
- **Hasty generalisation:** small/unrepresentative sample; **Anecdotal evidence:** sample of one
- **Appeal to nature / tradition / novelty:** natural / old / new = better
- **System 1:** fast, automatic; **System 2:** slow, effortful, lazy
- **Confirmation bias, availability heuristic, anchoring, in-group bias, social proof, propaganda / manufactured consent**

### Important Formulas
- No numerical formulas; key relation: **Argument = Claim + Evidence + Warrant**

### Must-Remember Points
- Ask **where the warrant breaks**, not whether evidence is true
- New Coke: 2 lakh tested; measured taste, not attachment
- Kodak invented digital camera in 1975
- Antidotes: devil's advocate (confirmation), base rates (availability), private independent estimate (anchoring)
- Awareness **reduces but does not eliminate** anchoring
- Herman and Chomsky, Manufacturing Consent, 1988
- Persuasion survives scrutiny; manufactured consent needs hidden evidence

# Module 5: Expertise, Authority, and Value

## 5.1 The Credibility Checklist

### 5.1.1 Core Question and Central Claim

#### Key Concept
- Guiding question: **When someone says "The experts say...", what should make us believe them?**
- **Credentials alone are not enough** to establish expertise.
- **Central claim:** You **cannot understand expertise just by looking at experts**; you must look at the **networks of trust** around us, because they decide **whose expertise we even want to hear** in the first place.
- Session covers four things:
  - What expertise actually is
  - Different kinds of expertise
  - How trust and credibility work
  - How to question expertise without becoming closed to evidence

### 5.1.2 Maya and Manan: Two Ways of Distrusting the News

| Person | Situation | Response to Evidence |
|--------|-----------|----------------------|
| **Maya** | Barely encountered news; feeds, friends, habits never brought it up; nobody told her to ignore it | **May change her mind** when shown missing news |
| **Manan** | Has seen a lot of news, but was taught by family/friends that **outsiders are not to be trusted** | **Digs in deeper**; refuses to trust the evidence |

- Both distrust mainstream media, but for **different structural reasons**.

### 5.1.3 What Is Expertise: Four Common Signals

#### Definition
- Most people say a person is an expert because of **knowledge**, **track record**, **credentials**, or **peer recognition**.

| Signal | What It Shows | Limitation | Example |
|--------|---------------|------------|---------|
| **Knowledge** | Knowing a topic | Expertise is **not just having facts, but knowing which facts matter** | Memorising every chess rule but losing to a 10-year-old who plays all the time |
| **Track record** | Repeated good results that are **hard to fake**; **strongest evidence** | Works **only when reality gives clear and quick feedback** | Chess player knows of a blunder within moves; political pundit waits months |
| **Credentials** | Degrees, licences; a **shortcut** | Show **what someone has learnt**, not whether they are good **that particular day** | Surgeon's board certification vs actual surgical skill |
| **Peer recognition** | Awards, citations, reputation; **only insiders can judge insider work** | Fields can **fall in love with fashionable ideas** and reward **fitting in rather than being right** | - |

#### Key Facts
- **Chess master studies:** experts **do not think through more options** than beginners; they **see options differently** by **recognising patterns** others cannot perceive.
- **Philip Tetlock:** tracked thousands of expert predictions; **well-credentialed experts did barely better than chance**.
- **20 years of experience** can mean **20 years of skill** or **one year of experience repeated 20 times**.
- Expertise is **not any one of these four**; each is a **signal**, and **each can mislead**.
- **Nguyen:** all four signals share one feature: **most of us cannot check them on our own**; look at what underlies them.

### 5.1.4 Types of Expertise

#### Contributory vs Interactional Expertise
- Source: sociologists **Harry Collins and Robert Evans**.

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|------|---------|---------|
| **Contributory expertise** | **Actually does the work** | Surgeon; physicist running an experiment |
| **Interactional expertise** | Does not/cannot do the work, but has **soaked up the language of the field** well enough to hold an **expert conversation** | A great **science journalist** |

#### Explicit vs Tacit Expertise

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|------|---------|---------|
| **Explicit** | Knowledge that **can be written down** | A recipe |
| **Tacit** | Knowledge that **cannot be written down**; travels through **practice and apprenticeship** | Experienced **nurse** who just knows a patient is worsening; **carpenter** sensing something wrong in wood |

- **Nobody becomes an expert by reading a manual alone.**
- Tacit experts may **lack credentials** yet still be experts.

#### Experiential Expertise
- **Informal knowledge from lived experience**, e.g., a **chronic illness / 20-year migraine sufferer** knows things a textbook may not.
- **Does not replace formal knowledge**, but supports **good decision-making**.

#### Meta-Expertise
- **The skill of judging experts when you are not an expert yourself**; what we need **most of the time**.
- Per Nguyen, it **depends on surroundings**: good source-evaluation skills fail if **someone else chose your sources** or **taught you to distrust good evidence** (Manan).

### 5.1.5 The Three Checklist Questions

#### Key Concept
When told **"The experts say..."**, ask:
1. **Which experts?**
2. **Expert in what exactly?**
3. **How did you come to trust them?**

### 5.1.6 Admitting Uncertainty and Expert Habits

- **Admitting you don't know something makes you more believable, not less.**
- Saying "confident about this part, less about that" or "this is outside my area" earns **more trust** than "I know everything" (which leads to making things up).
- **Overconfidence** works, but **not all the time**.

| Habit of a Credible Expert | Meaning |
|----------------------------|---------|
| **Show your reasoning** | Make reasoning visible |
| **Stay in your lane** | Do not claim expertise in everything |
| **Disclose your incentives** | State what you get in return |
| **Make mistakes clear** | Openly acknowledge errors |

### 5.1.7 Epistemic Bubble vs Echo Chamber

| Feature | **Epistemic Bubble** (Maya) | **Echo Chamber** (Manan) |
|---------|-----------------------------|--------------------------|
| Cause | Relevant voices **left out / missing** | Outside voices **discredited in advance** |
| How formed | **Accident**, algorithm, friend group, habit | **Constructed**; trust only in own group |
| Stability | **Fragile**; can **pop** | **Not open to change** |
| Fix | Bring in missing voices / change algorithm | Exposure alone fails; person digs in |

### 5.1.8 Critiquing Experts

- Questioning experts is **absolutely okay and necessary**.

| Reason Experts Can Go Wrong |
|-----------------------------|
| **Overconfidence** |
| Framing problems through their **own narrow tools** |
| **Vested interests** and **funding pressures** |
| **Groupthink** (influenced by their own group) |

- Critical thinker's job: **find these blind spots**.

## 5.2 The Social Construction of Value

### 5.2.1 The Joshua Bell Story

- **January 2007**, Washington D.C. metro station: man in jeans and baseball cap played violin for **about 45 minutes**.
- He was **Joshua Bell**, one of the world's best violinists, on an instrument **worth millions**.
- Days earlier he **sold out a concert hall** with good seats **well over $100**.
- In the station almost nobody stopped; he collected **a little over $30**.
- **Same man, same music, same violin**: the difference was **not in the sound but in the setting** (hall, ticket, reputation, crowd).

#### Key Concept
- Value is **not just inherent/intrinsic**; much of it is **built by institutions, markets, brands, and reputations**.
- Not all, but **most** of value is given/built by these outside elements.
- Five areas: **intrinsic vs extrinsic value**, **how institutions create value**, **what price measures**, **brand equity**, **reputation and legitimacy**.

### 5.2.2 Intrinsic vs Extrinsic Value

| Type | Definition | Example |
|------|------------|---------|
| **Extrinsic value** | Value **as a means to something else** | A **hammer** becomes valuable when put to a nail |
| **Intrinsic value** | Value **for its own sake** | Happiness, friendship, a person's life |

#### Examples of Constructed Value
- **De Beers diamond:** in the **1940s**, De Beers and its ad agency ran **"A diamond is forever"**, reshaping the engagement-ring market. The **carbon was always there; the meaning and much of the price were added**.
- **Mona Lisa vs skilled copy:** side by side they may look identical; the original's value comes from **history, authenticity, and its place in a story**, which are **relational**.
- A thing's value is largely **a fact about its relationship with the viewer/listener/receiver**.

#### Key Concept
- **Socially constructed does not mean fake or arbitrary.**
- **Money** is socially constructed but still **very real**.
- Key questions: **Who builds value? How is it built? Do we notice it is built?**

### 5.2.3 How Institutions Create Value

| Mechanism | How It Creates Value |
|-----------|----------------------|
| **Museums** | Do not only display value; they **confer** it (glass case, light, label = "this matters") |
| **Symbolic / cultural capital** (**Pierre Bourdieu**) | Cultural fields run on a **currency that isn't money**; critics and curators decide **what counts as good taste** |
| **Rankings** | **Do not just measure** institutions; they **change the institutions** and how people work within them |
| **Certifications** (organic labels, **Michelin stars**, fair trade marks, academic accreditation) | Useful because **we often cannot judge quality ourselves**; the label **stands in for knowledge we don't have** |

- Risk of labels: they **create a line**; we may **trust the label more than our own judgement**.

### 5.2.4 Limits of Constructed Value

- Constructed value is **still valid and authentic**.
- A story and endorsement can build a lot, but **not forever**.
- If the **claim the value rests on is false**, the **value tends to fall apart**; exposing this is the critical thinker's job.

### 5.2.5 Reputation and Legitimacy

- **Reputation and legitimacy** are the **social forms of credibility** (same idea as credibility of experts).
- They **let strangers rely on each other without checking everything**.
- Example: a trusted **organic** label previously consumed can be trusted again.

#### Key Concept
- Joshua Bell's music did not change; **the ticket price, venue, reputation, and crowd** changed: **the machinery that tells us what to value**.
- **Lesson:** value is **not fake; it is made** (socially constructed) by people, experts, and institutions, **with vested interests**.
- Museums, critics, experts, rankings, prizes, brands, reputations are **useful**, but each is **a lens that shapes what and how we see**.

## 5.3 Critical Thinking in Professional Research

### 5.3.1 Role of a UX Researcher

#### Key Concept
- Tech organisations stand on **three pillars**:

| Pillar | Role |
|--------|------|
| **Engineering** | Backbone/skeleton of the product |
| **Product** | Strategy; market place, need, niche space |
| **Design** | Look, feel, and interaction |

- **Research** is **"the blood that flows across"** all three; each needs **customer feedback**.
- Senior researcher's role: join **product strategy** and **vision** conversations, and above all be **an unbiased, neutral voice in the room**.
- Each pillar **owns** something and becomes **territorial**; research brings the **customer's perspective** at launch.
- This gives research **credibility** but also **tension** (trashing ideas others are proud of).
- Day-to-day tasks: **recruiting respondents**, choosing **methodology**, selecting markets, logistics, **moderation**, then **analysis and synthesis** tied back to business needs.
- Balance: provide **accurate data even if it does not match** what business wants, while remembering you are **part of the organisation and its growth**.

### 5.3.2 Territoriality, Bias, and Research Rigour

- **Researchers can also be territorial.**
- Other pillars are **open** because their shared agenda is **business growth**; territoriality arises when **feedback is not structured or not on strong legs**.
- Parallel: a **medical research paper** is trusted because of **inherent trust in scientific rigour** (control and test groups), though people still check for **industry lobbies**.
- Researcher's **strongest leg = research rigour**: "You're not trusting me; this is what 100 people said."
  - A **statistically significant, representative sample**.
  - About **100 respondents** gives roughly a **95% confidence level**; **margin of error reduces**, moving toward the **centre of the bell curve**.
- Research is **not always right**:
  - **Henry Ford:** "If you ask people what they want, they'll say a flying horse."
  - **Steve Jobs** never researched the iPhone with consumers.
- For unfamiliar territory (e.g., new **AI features**) with **no benchmarks**, study the **closest immediate behaviour** (e.g., how HR/IT/admin manually summarise tickets; pain points, need gaps).
- Navigating disagreement: be **partners**, the **voice of the customer**; rely on data, numbers, **consumer verbatims**; if overruled, act as a **voice of warning** with potential risks and actions.

### 5.3.3 Structure and Communicating Insights

- Researchers are taught: **don't complicate**; "**What's the story?**"
- **"Don't want death by data; inspire me with insight."**
- Explain as you would **to a child** (lion example): break it down logically, without treating stakeholders as dummies.
- Stakeholders want **simple, clear points**: "What are the three things I need to do?"

#### Insight Simplification Sequence

| Step | Question |
|------|----------|
| 1. **Data** | What did we capture? |
| 2. **Pattern** | What patterns emerge; which are **dominant**? |
| 3. **Human behaviour** | Why is the pattern happening? |
| 4. **Business implication** | What does it mean for the business? |
| 5. **Action / Recommendation** | What should the client do? |

- **Organisational conversations are like interpersonal ones**, but with **formality and decorum**; hence boards and markers to **visually break down thought**.
- **Three languages starting with C:** Engineering speaks **code**, Product speaks **company**, Design and Research speak **customer**; each may not understand the others.
- Frameworks must be applied **fluidly** on the ground, considering the humans involved.

### 5.3.4 Credentials, Pedigree, and Earned Credibility

- Early-career experience: an MBA from a **non-pedigree institute**, treated by a Big Four consultant as **"just a data vendor"**.
- Lesson: either be **the loudest voice** or **build that voice over years** by **consistently doing good work** and earning a name.
- Later, **16 years of experience** and work with major brands brings **credibility**; pedigree-based conversations stop.
- Still, a **half-baked point of view** can be challenged by anyone; **rigour and groundwork** are essential.
- **Elevator pitch:** grab attention **in three lines**; tailor content to engineer, product manager, and designer; end with **three key takeaways**.
- **Empathy** (knowing what the other person wants) ties together structure, **awareness of biases**, and the ability to **think beyond your credentials**.
- **Crux of research = observation**: a good researcher **observes and listens**, which is **empathy**.

### 5.3.5 AI, Trust, and Value

- Focus has moved from **AI capabilities** to **value in AI**: metrics such as **man-hours saved, labour cost saved, more tasks per employee, dollars saved, new ventures**.
- Key issue is **trust**.
- **Transactional analysis model (Parent-Adult-Child):**

| Interaction | Result |
|-------------|--------|
| **Ideal: Adult-Adult** | Trust (and understanding) |
| **Common: Parent (human)-Child (AI)** | AI lacks information, causing frustration, **trust issues**, broken workflows |

- People assume AI is intelligent, but it is **only as intelligent as the data you feed it**.
- BCG research (as cited): **only 5% of organisations** investing in AI see **five times returns**, because they **cleaned their data** and **rebuilt workflows**.
- Data in many firms is **scattered** (email, Slack, Teams, phone); **provenance not established**.
- **AI = procedural intelligence** (algorithm, process); **humans = jugaad** (making things work).
- **Empathy for AI is not desirable** (it is a tool, like a laptop or calculator); **trust is**.
- **Forward deployed engineers** (OpenAI, Anthropic) help companies **clean data** for AI.

### 5.3.6 Never Trust a Finding at Face Value

#### The Hindu Case
- Times of India ad mocked The Hindu as **boring**.
- Finding: The Hindu seen as **intellectual** (read for exams, GRE/TOEFL/IELTS), with a **small loyal** audience; the larger spending population did not see it that way.
- Instinctive response: change everything (colour, entertainment).
- Actual approach: **twist it** – "**Stay ahead of times**" (with Ogilvy & Mather): the problem is a **perception issue**; differentiation is **uniqueness**, delivered with humour and wit, using its **credibility as a thought leader**.

#### The Rapido Case (2018)
- Initial hypothesis: find a positioning space **vs Uber and Ola**.
- Insight: Uber/Ola users would **never consider** bike taxis; real target is the person **struggling in the public (RTC) bus**.
- Campaign with Allu Arjun and Ranveer Singh, **starting at 19 rupees**; worked very well.
- **Commute currencies:** **pride, convenience, comfort** (e.g., hiring a tempo traveller for a wedding out of pride).

#### The Fragrance Case
- About **200 interviews**; award-winning qualitative study for a large conglomerate entering fragrance.
- Client wanted only: "Tell me which notes on the **fragrance wheel** to focus on" (copying Gucci, Calvin Klein, Yardley) due to a **launch date** and **existing supply chains**.
- Researchers warned against becoming a **"me too" / copy brand**.
- Resolution: a **short-term, mid-term, long-term plan**: use existing supply chain for a premium option now, change **packaging codes**, and build new supply chains for the mid/long term.
- Research had to think about **supply chain** because that was **the client's context**.

### 5.3.7 Data vs Narrative

#### Key Concept
- **Numbers are only about 30% of the story**; data is a strong leg, but **no one sees your legs**.
- **Data provides accuracy; narrative influences decision-making.**
- A data-backed recommendation **does not speak for itself**; it needs **sensitivity to human behaviour** and **framing for the audience**.
- **Establish context first** (who we met, what we observed, why they do it), then recommend; the reader then already knows **why**.

### 5.3.8 Closing Lessons

- With good critical thinking, process, and tools, **the question can change**, but **the answers must always be truthful**; be **open to the question changing**.
- Habit of mind: **"Stay frustrated, stay intrigued"**; without frustration, intrigue, and inquisitiveness, **you are not growing**.
