## MODULE 1: Foundations of Critical Thinking

---

### Seeing Is Not Always Believing

- **Data never speaks for itself:** people interpret, assume and construct explanations, so reasoning errors are inevitable
- **Genuine information ≠ correct conclusion:** dashboard traffic +30% may be bots, analytics error or a viral post
- **Critical thinking:** a **careful process of evaluating information before reaching a conclusion**, **NOT** criticising everything or doubting every claim
- **Core questions:** What is the claim? What evidence? Another explanation? What assumptions? Could I be wrong?
- **Intelligence ≠ critical thinking:** smarter people get better at defending beliefs; what is needed is **intellectual discipline**
- **Target (2012):** pregnancy model was accurate, but the **thinking around the analytics** was missing ("Can we?" was asked, **NOT** "Should we?")

> **CLA:** A marketing report credits a redesign for a 40% traffic jump, ignoring that the launch coincided with a major holiday sale. What analytical error is present?
> - **Failing to control for a confounding event that could independently explain the increase** ✓

---

### Logic vs Critical Thinking

| Check | Question | Tests |
|---|---|---|
| **Logic** | Does the conclusion follow from the premises? | **Structure** |
| **Critical thinking** | Are the premises true, complete, relevant? | **Material / premises** |

- **Critical thinking (Scriven & Paul):** "Self-guided, self-disciplined thinking which attempts to reason at the highest level of quality in a fair-minded way"
- **Reasoning:** moving from what you know to a new conclusion; **the process, NOT the information**, makes it good or bad
- **Two failure points:** **structural** (conclusion does not follow) or **at the source** (premises incomplete, biased or false)
- **Logic without CT:** coherent argument on a shaky foundation; **CT without logic:** a vague sense that something is off, and you can't explain it
- **A learnable skill**, **NOT** a checklist or personality trait

> **CLA:** "All top-performing employees arrive early. Priya arrives early. Therefore, Priya is a top performer." Primary flaw?
> - **Affirming the consequent: a shared trait treated as proof of the conclusion** ✓
>
> **CLA:** "Ignore Raj's cost-cutting proposal; he was recently divorced and isn't thinking straight." Reasoning error?
> - **Ad hominem: dismisses the proposal by attacking Raj personally** ✓
>
> **CLA:** "Either we cut costs immediately or the company goes bankrupt within a year." Why is it flawed?
> - **It ignores other options between the two extremes (false dichotomy)** ✓

### When Logic Goes Wrong: Challenger and Business Cases

| Case | Key Fact | Missing Check |
|---|---|---|
| **Challenger (1986)** | Forecast 29°F; O-ring damage at 53°F earlier; Boisjoly urged delay | Chart showed **only damaged flights** and left out no-damage warm launches |
| **IL&FS (2018)** | ~₹91,000 cr default; top ratings; debt +44% in 3 yrs | Why was the credit line for overdue payments never used? |
| **BYJU'S** | $22 bn → written down 86% to < $3 bn | Could pandemic-era growth continue? |

- **Valid logic + incomplete evidence = wrong answer**
- **Data-driven ≠ well reasoned:** ask **which data? what was left out?**
- **Key question:** "What would I need to see or hear to know whether it is right?"

> **CLA:** A retailer doubles ad spend, assuming more exposure proportionally raises sales. What hidden assumption does this depend on?
> - **Customers' purchase decisions are significantly influenced by advertising exposure** ✓
>
> **CLA:** A CEO enters a market solely because a competitor succeeded there, without analysing the competitor's advantages. Weakness?
> - **Assuming success will transfer without evaluating differences in resources and context** ✓
>
> **CLA:** "Our competitor failed because it raised too much funding too early, so we should avoid funding until we have significant revenue." Critical flaw?
> - **It assumes a single cause for the failure without ruling out other factors** ✓

### Obstacles: Confirmation Bias, Motivated Reasoning, Anchoring

| Obstacle | Definition | Working Parts | Counter |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Confirmation bias** | Favouring information that supports existing belief | **Selective search, interpretation, memory** | Write down what would change your mind first |
| **Motivated reasoning** | Reasoning shaped by **what you want to be true** | **Asymmetric scrutiny, rationalisation** | Who benefits? Same scrutiny both ways? |
| **Anchoring** | First number/claim shapes later judgements | **Anchor + insufficient adjustment** | Was the first number ever justified? |

- **Combined:** anchoring sets the start, confirmation bias steers the search, motivated reasoning screens out contrary evidence, so the result **feels earned but the process was not neutral**
- **Scrutinise conclusions** that come **fast, feel obvious, look convenient**
- **Examples:** UN-Africa wheel spin (anchoring); week-1 vs week-3 engagement (motivated reasoning); "fast but expensive" review (confirmation bias)

### Professional and Civic Life

| Setting | Example | Obstacle |
|---|---|---|
| Professional | NY lawyer cites AI-hallucinated cases | Fluent output, premise check skipped |
| Professional | Hiring panel's first-5-minute impression | **Anchoring + confirmation bias** |
| Professional | Improving metric spotlighted, falling one footnoted | **Motivated reasoning (organisational)** |
| Civic | First viral version of events sets the frame | **Anchoring (civic scale)** |
| Civic | COVID-19 claims believed according to prior views | **Confirmation bias** |

- **Convincing ≠ true**
- **CT is risk management** at work and a **civic responsibility**

### Evidence vs Assertion

| Term | Definition | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Assertion** | Statement with **no support attached**; rests on authority, confidence, repetition | No source, method or dataset |
| **Evidence** | Support that can be **checked, traced, verified** independently | Named **source + method + specific comparison** |

- **Difference = what stands behind the sentence**, **NOT** how confident it sounds
- **A number is evidence only with a traceable source and method**; specificity is **NOT** verification
- **A cited source can still fail:** citation misread, misleading axes, altered screenshot. Confirm the source says what it is used to say
- **Well-informed assertion** (an experienced doctor's clinical judgement) is acceptable
- **Ask:** is it an "assertion wearing evidence's clothing"?

> **CLA:** "Nine out of ten dentists recommend our toothpaste," with no sample size or selection method disclosed. How should a critical thinker evaluate it?
> - **Question the sample size and selection method before accepting the statistic as representative** ✓

---

### Facts, Inferences, Interpretations

| Layer | Definition | Answers | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fact** | Verifiable state of affairs | What happened? | Revenue was $2.3 m |
| **Inference** | Conclusion drawn from facts by reasoning | What does it likely lead to? | Rose 3 quarters, so will likely rise again |
| **Interpretation** | Meaning/significance shaped by values | What does it mean? | Growth shows our strategy works / reflects a seasonal upswing |

- **Inference** is checked for **validity or strength** and must **rule out alternative explanations** (crime +15% may reflect reporting or policing focus)
- **Interpretation is necessary**; just don't treat it as fact

> **CLA:** Cities with more ice cream shops have higher crime; a report concludes ice cream shops cause crime. Most likely explanation for the flaw?
> - **Both are influenced by a third factor such as warmer weather or population density** ✓
>
> **CLA:** An inspector checks 50 of 10,000 units, finds no defects, and concludes the whole batch is defect-free. Reasoning type and limitation?
> - **Inductive reasoning: probable but not certain conclusions** ✓

---

### Framing

- **Framing changes what is taken as true, NOT what is true**; there is **no frame-free presentation**

| Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|
| **Word choice** (cognitive vs emotive, Hurley) | "Reduced headcount 15%" vs "gutted workforce" |
| **Comparison point** | Sales +5%: "best ever" vs "below 12% industry average" |
| **Visual framing** | Y-axis from 90; 3-month vs 3-year window |
| **Order & emphasis** | Revenue +8% headline, costs +14% in paragraph 6 |
| **Anchoring** (Kahneman) | Insufficient adjustment / simple suggestion: real-estate listing prices, 1×…×8 vs 8×…×1 |

- **Substitution test:** reframe the claim (failure rate, other window, harsher comparison). Same conclusion?

> **CLA:** A hospital says "90% survival rate" rather than "10% mortality rate". Why do patients respond more positively?
> - **Framing effect: identical information presented differently changes perception** ✓
>
> **CLA:** Auto-enrolment with opt-out gives far higher retirement-plan participation than opt-in. Principle?
> - **The default option frames the decision and reduces the effort needed to comply** ✓
>
> **CLA:** An employee frames a raise as "protecting the company from losing me to a competitor" rather than "wanting more money". Effect on the employer?
> - **It shifts the employer's focus from a personal cost to a loss the company could avoid** ✓

---

### Selection Bias, Survivorship Bias, Objective Record

| Concept | Key Point |
|---|---|
| **Selection bias** | What **never enters the frame**; the sharpest form of framing and harder to catch than a bad axis |
| **WYSIATI** (Kahneman) | "What You See Is All There Is": the mind builds a coherent story from the data it has |
| **Survivorship bias** | Wald's bombers: armour goes **where returning planes show NO damage**; accelerator alumni showcases |
| **Availability** | Vivid deaths (sharks, crashes) overestimated vs heart disease and diabetes; **availability mistaken for frequency** |
| **Objective record** | Videos and datasets reflect **capture choices**; **genuine but partial** |

- **Test:** what cases were excluded before I saw the data? If included, same conclusion?

> **CLA:** A company launches a product nationwide because three focus-group customers loved it. Weakness?
> - **Generalising from a small, unrepresentative sample to the entire market** ✓
>
> **CLA:** A satisfaction survey sent only to town-hall attendees shows 95% satisfaction. Why might it misrepresent sentiment?
> - **Attendees are likely more engaged and positive than the average employee, biasing the sample** ✓

## Quick Reference: Which Check to Apply

| Situation | Action/Answer |
|---|---|
| Conclusion doesn't follow from premises | **Logic** failure (affirming the consequent, false dichotomy) |
| Premises incomplete or biased | **Critical thinking** failure; ask what data was left out |
| Attack on the person, not the claim | **Ad hominem** |
| Two variables move together | Look for a **third factor / confounder** |
| Sample to population | **Inductive**: probable, not certain |
| Small or self-selected sample | **Unrepresentative / selection bias** |
| Only successes visible | **Survivorship bias** |
| Same fact, different wording or default | **Framing** |
| First number dominates | **Anchoring** |
| Claim with no source or method | **Assertion**, not evidence |
| Polished AI output | Verify premises: **convincing ≠ true** |

## Key Decision Rules Summary

| Rule | Detail |
|---|---|
| Two checks | Logic = structure; CT = premises |
| Valid ≠ true | A valid argument on flawed premises gives a wrong answer |
| Evidence test | Source + method + comparison you can check |
| Three layers | Fact (what happened), inference (likely next), interpretation (meaning) |
| Substitution test | Reframe it; if the conclusion changes, the frame was driving it |
| Missing-cases test | Ask what was excluded before you saw the data |
| Anchor check | Was the first number ever justified? |
| Scrutiny trigger | Fast, obvious, convenient conclusions |

## MODULE 2: Critical Reading and Interpretation

---

### Explicit Claims vs Implicit Positions

| Aspect | Explicit Claim | Implicit Position |
|---------|---------|---------|
| **Meaning** | Directly stated | What the text assumes, values or wants you to conclude |
| **Evidence** | **Quotable** / exact sentence | **Inferred** from pattern of choices (chosen, omitted, emphasised) |
| **Example** | "Revenue grew 12%" | Speed stressed repeatedly = speed valued over quality |
| **Fact-check** | Easier | Harder - **most persuasion happens here** |
| **Question** | What does the text say? | What is it quietly asking me to believe? |

- **Close reading:** identify both layers and point to where each lives in the text
- **A Horse and Two Goats (R.K. Narayan):** Muni (Tamil) thinks the American is an official, then that he is buying the goats; American (English) thinks Muni owns the clay horse - **nobody lied**, each built on an **unchecked assumption**
- **Earnings call:** opening with 8% user growth, revenue-per-user decline only later = growth valued over monetisation
- **Op-ed:** 30% faster permits, no cost mentioned = success argued **by omission**

> **CLA:** What distinguishes an explicit claim from an implicit position in a text?
> - **Explicit claim is directly stated and quotable; implicit position must be inferred** ✓

---

### Close Reading Techniques

| Technique | Question | Example |
|---------|---------|---------|
| **Repetition & emphasis** | What keeps recurring? | Speed 6x, quality 1x = **implicit ranking** |
| **Absence & omission** | Whose perspective would complicate it? (**selection bias**) | Hiring note silent on eliminated role |
| **Word choice** | **Cognitive** (fact) vs **emotive** (attitude) meaning (Hurley) | "Restructured" vs "gutted" |
| **Structure & order** | What is first; what is buried? (order = framing) | Risk in paragraph 9 / appendix matters less |
| **Assumed audience** | Who is assumed to already agree? | Conclusion written as "obvious" |

> **CLA:** A memo mentions 'speed' six times and 'quality' only once. What does this signal?
> - **An implicit ranking where speed is prioritised over quality** ✓

> **CLA:** An acquisition report repeats 'growth' nine times, calls factory closures 'asset optimisation', and puts safety risks in an appendix. Evaluating the placement of safety risks applies which technique, conveying what?
> - **Analysing structure and order - signals the risk is of lesser importance** ✓

---

### Annotation, Traps and Textual Hooks

| Item | Point |
|---------|---------|
| **Explicit** | "Helped thousands of small businesses grow" |
| **"As always"** | Implies **consistency never shown with evidence** |
| **No departed merchants** | **Absence, not a denial** |
| **Trap 1: Position as fact** | Check - could a reasonable reader hold the **opposite** position? |
| **Trap 2: Overreading** | Reading supported only by **your own assumptions** |
| **Textual hook** | Word, omission or structural choice; none = **hypothesis, NOT a close reading** |

> **CLA:** In "Our platform helped thousands of small businesses grow this year. As always, we remain focussed on the merchants...", what does 'as always' reveal?
> - **An implicit position of consistency that the text fails to support with evidence** ✓

---

### Close-Reading Checklist

| # | Question |
|---------|---------|
| 1 | What does it **explicitly claim** (quotable)? |
| 2 | What **repeats** / is emphasised? |
| 3 | What is **absent**? |
| 4 | What do words carry **beyond facts**? |
| 5 | What comes **first** / is **buried**? |
| 6 | Who is assumed to **already agree**? |

- **Horse and Two Goats applied:** repeated poverty, communication gap, two worlds, clay horse; **two goats** = fragile livelihood; **horse** = mythology for Muni, art object for American; omissions keep focus on **irony**

### Business Documents

| Document | What to check |
|---------|---------|
| **Annual report** | Shareholder letter vs risk factors; "challenging year" vs "significant losses"; **disappeared segment/region/metric** |
| **Press release** | Headline number; **executive quote at top frames** the rest; what must be omitted for headline to impress? |
| **Pitch deck** | Missing competitor risk / failed channel; "capital efficient" vs "margins below expectations"; growth slide assumes **past conditions will hold** |

> **CLA:** For a pitch-deck growth projection extending past trends indefinitely, which assumption check best exposes a hidden weakness?
> - **Ask whether the slide explicitly names the assumption that past growth conditions will hold** ✓

---

### Close Reading a Report: Red Flags

| Phrase | Flag |
|---------|---------|
| "Broadly in line with expectations" | **No baseline** - whose expectations? |
| "Footfall -8%" + "online engagement trending positively" | **Precision vs vagueness** - vague one likely not good news |
| "Optimise the store portfolio" | **Euphemism** for store closures, uncommitted |
| "Costs +6%... position us well for growth" | **Unsupported causal claim** - no metric, comparison, timeline |
| "Team worked tirelessly" | **Emotional/reputational** filler, placed after bad numbers |
| "Committed to delivering value" | **Vague close** - no one would contradict it |

- **Rewrite:** shorter, less confident - original **felt more informative than it was** (**NOT** dishonest)
- **Rewrite questions:** comparison point? adjective doing what? **hidden actor**? what question is it answering?

> **CLA:** "Operating costs increased 6% and these investments position the company well for future growth." What concern does this raise?
> - **The growth claim is asserted as fact without any supporting metric or timeline** ✓

> **CLA:** Why treat "explore opportunities to optimise the store portfolio" with suspicion?
> - **It is a euphemism that avoids openly committing to closing stores** ✓

> **CLA:** A report pairs "footfall declined 8% year over year" with "online engagement metrics trending positively." What does this suggest?
> - **The vague metric is probably hiding results that are not actually favourable** ✓

### Narratives: 5Cs and SCR

| 5C | Question |
|---------|---------|
| **Character** | Who is at the centre? |
| **Context** | What circumstances are relevant? |
| **Conflict** | What tension/disruption? |
| **Choices** | Which decisions get attention? |
| **Consequences** | What becomes the ending/moral? |

$$\text{SCR} = \text{Situation (problem/opportunity)} \rightarrow \text{Complication (tension)} \rightarrow \text{Resolution (adapt/succeed)}$$

- Persuasion comes from the **order** of facts
- **WeWork:** "tech company, community, mission first" story vs balance sheet exposed by IPO prospectus

> **CLA:** A green-energy startup: (1) gap in residential solar storage, (2) critical lithium battery shortage threatening survival, (3) novel silicon alternative doubling capacity at half cost. Under SCR, what is the function of (2)?
> - **The Complication - creates narrative tension that justifies the eventual innovative solution** ✓

### Same Story, Different Readings

| Reading | Moral |
|---------|---------|
| Tortoise-centred | **Slow and steady wins** |
| Hare-centred | **Talent undone by arrogance/complacency** |
| **Ebner-Eschenbach** | Tortoise mistakes one win for being a sprinter - over-crediting own ability |

- **Blockbuster:** dominant = Netflix's better tech won; counter = **leadership rejected internal streaming proposals** to protect late fees and stores

> **CLA:** The popular narrative credits Netflix's innovation for the retail giant's bankruptcy. What does a counter-reading bring to light?
> - **Internal proposals for streaming services were rejected by senior leadership** ✓

> **CLA:** Traditional narrative: slow legacy firm vs digital startup. Counter-reading: the firm's own engineers proposed a digital transition that executives rejected to protect retail margins. How does this shift the 5Cs?
> - **From external technological conflict to internal leadership choices** ✓

### Power and Narrative Omission

| Company | Narrative | Left out / reframed |
|---------|---------|---------|
| **Uber** | Disruption, consumer freedom | **Labour issue reframed as technology issue** |
| **IBM** | Professionalism, rigour | Gets more **benefit of the doubt** |
| **Nike** | Elite athletes | **Supplier-factory workers** |
| **Facebook** | Connecting the world | **Third-party data buyers** |

- **Politics of storytelling:** NOT propaganda - everyday process making some views seem reasonable
- **Narrative omission:** "Who isn't in this story?"; **silence is part of the narrative**

> **CLA:** A solar firm's campaign shows cheap rural electricity but ignores displacement of indigenous families. Which action applies 'narrative omission'?
> - **Identifying how the campaign excludes displaced families whose experiences complicate the empowerment story** ✓

### Counter-Readings and the Three Tests

- **Counter-reading:** same events, **different perspective** (narrator, start point, definition of success) - **NOT** mere disagreement
- **Construct:** main character -> measure of success -> missing stakeholders -> rebuild conflict/choices with evidence -> compare via 5Cs -> contradict or different aspects?

| Test | Question |
|---------|---------|
| **Evidentiary** | Verifiable evidence or speculation? |
| **Consistency** | Explains evidence **at least as well**; accounts for facts that made original persuasive? |
| **Predictive** | Better explains **what happened next**? |

> **CLA:** Which combination of tests evaluates whether a counter-reading is more robust than the dominant version?
> - **Evidentiary, consistency and predictive tests** ✓

> **CLA:** Food delivery platform: dominant = 'algorithmic route optimisation'; counter = 'systematic wage underpayment'. What must the consistency test determine?
> - **Whether the wage theory explains the high driver retention and rapid delivery times that made the original narrative persuasive** ✓

> **CLA:** ApexGo delivery robots hit pedestrians twice; company blames pedestrians/infrastructure; audit finds a hidden navigation script overriding operator emergency commands. Which finding passes the consistency test?
> - **The algorithm defect explains both collisions and why certified operators could not regain manual control** ✓

## MODULE 3: Constructing and Evaluating Arguments

---

### Arguments in Business Decisions

- **Stories capture attention; arguments justify decisions.** Business needs both.
- **Resolution** stage (SCR) is where storytelling becomes an **argument** (recommending what should happen next).
- **Netflix 2006:** Claim = invest aggressively in streaming; Evidence = broadband adoption, falling data costs, changing consumption; **Reasoning** = why act now and not in 5 years. **Evidence alone is NOT enough.**
- **The argument came first, the story came later** (hindsight creates heroes and villains).
- AI changed the **speed** of producing arguments, **NOT** the standards for judging them.

> **CLA:** Netflix (2006) asked the board to shift resources from profitable DVD-by-mail to unproven streaming; broadband trends alone would not persuade. Which element was most critical?
> - **Reasoning connecting the evidence to the claim** ✓

---

### Rhetorical Appeals (Aristotle)

| Appeal | Basis | Key Question | Example |
|--------|-------|--------------|---------|
| **Ethos** | Credibility (experience, expertise, reputation, transparency) | Why should I trust you? | 20-year analyst vs friend; **Patagonia "Don't Buy This Jacket"** |
| **Pathos** | Emotion / what is at stake (**NOT** manipulation) | Why does this matter? | Employees back change because they see what is at stake |
| **Logos** | Logic connecting data to recommendation | Why does the conclusion follow from the evidence? | Prime "pays for itself" calculation |

- **Patagonia:** very little statistical evidence; persuasive because it acted **consistently with its environmental values** (ethos), even at the risk of lower sales.
- **Admitting uncertainty / mistakes** can build credibility.
- **Amazon Prime:** ethos (reliability reputation) + logos (cost-saving calculation) + pathos (convenience, frustration-free shopping); **no single appeal alone was enough**.
- **AI writing:** fluency creates an **illusion of expertise**; a well-written argument is **NOT** automatically a good argument.
- **Persuasion ≠ proof**; being persuaded ≠ being convinced.

> **CLA:** Founder highlights fifteen years of industry experience before sharing any data. Which appeal?
> - **Ethos** ✓
>
> **CLA:** During restructuring, leadership explains how the change protects employees' long-term job security instead of only showing cost-saving spreadsheets. Which appeal?
> - **Pathos** ✓
>
> **CLA:** A clothing company's Black Friday ad urging customers NOT to buy its jacket (little statistical detail) strengthened trust. Why?
> - **It reinforced credibility by acting consistently with company values** ✓
>
> **CLA:** Amazon combined its reliable-delivery reputation, a cost-saving shipping calculation and a frustration-free shopping promise to launch Prime. Which combination?
> - **Ethos, logos, and pathos working together** ✓

---

### Building Blocks: Claim, Evidence, Reasoning

| Part | Definition | Key Detail |
|------|-----------|------------|
| **Claim** | Conclusion the audience should believe or act on | Can be **stated or implied** (ads rarely say "buy this") |
| **Evidence** | Information supporting the claim | Numbers **and** qualitative data (interviews, observation); must be **relevant** |
| **Reasoning** | **Logical bridge**: why evidence supports the claim | Most often skipped; adds **no new evidence** |

- **Claim test:** "What exactly is the author asking me to believe or do?"
- **Relevance:** Bangalore satisfaction scores do **NOT** support a new Hyderabad office.
- **Mismatch:** 1 million downloads shows people **tried** the app, **NOT** that they **love** it.
- **AI Vietnam report:** accurate GDP/internet facts + "enter Vietnam" = **missing reasoning** (product fit, regulation, competitors).
- Ask not only "Are the facts correct?" but **"How exactly do these facts support the conclusion?"**

> **CLA:** A luxury car ad with "Live without limits" never says viewers should buy the car. What does this illustrate?
> - **Claims can be implied rather than explicitly stated** ✓
>
> **CLA:** A manager proposing a Chennai office presents Mumbai office satisfaction scores. Evidentiary flaw?
> - **The evidence lacks relevance to the claim** ✓
>
> **CLA:** An AI market-entry report has accurate GDP and internet-penetration statistics and recommends entry. What gap should a reader check?
> - **Whether the reasoning connects the evidence to the conclusion** ✓
>
> **Q:** Factually correct evidence does **NOT** automatically provide relevant support for a claim; relevance must be established separately.
> - **True** ✓

### Deductive vs Inductive Reasoning

| Feature | **Deductive** | **Inductive** |
|---------|---------------|---------------|
| Direction | General rule to specific case | Observations/patterns to general conclusion |
| Conclusion | **Certain** (if premises true) | **Probable**, open to revision |
| Evaluate by | **Validity**, then soundness | **Strength**, then cogency |
| Business use | Compliance, tax, policies, fraud-detection rules | Strategy, forecasts, market research (**most business decisions**) |
| Example | Expense > Rs 50,000 needs board approval; Rs 75,000 proposal needs approval | Scooter sales up 5 years, so demand **likely** grows; plant-based shelf space |

- **AI (generative) is inductive:** predicts next word from statistical patterns; fluent but can **hallucinate** by extending a pattern beyond the evidence (**NOT** lying).
- **UPI case (CEO 2021):** design stores around digital payments = inductive; risk = **pattern may not hold if conditions change**.
- **Overclaiming:** "proves / guarantees / clearly demonstrates" on inductive evidence; use "suggests / supports / indicates".
- Key test: **"Is this certain or simply probable?"**

> **CLA:** Footfall has risen every festive season for eight years; analyst predicts a rise again, admitting it is not guaranteed. Type of reasoning?
> - **Inductive reasoning** ✓
>
> **CLA:** Retail CEO (2021): digital payments growing, smartphone ownership rising, customers expect cashless, so redesign stores around digital payments. Main risk?
> - **The pattern may not hold if future conditions change unexpectedly** ✓
>
> **Q:** Inductive arguments make conclusions only **probable**, never guaranteed, even when evidence is strong.
> - **True** ✓

### Toulmin Model

| Element | Type | Role | Signal / Example |
|---------|------|------|------------------|
| **Claim** | Fundamental | Conclusion to accept | "Introduce a 4-day workweek" |
| **Grounds** | Fundamental | Evidence / facts | Productivity +12% in 6-month trial |
| **Warrant** | Fundamental | Reasoning linking grounds to claim (explicit or implied) | "If productivity and well-being rise, schedule benefits all" |
| **Backing** | Optional | Supports the **warrant** | Peer-reviewed / longitudinal studies; org-psychology research (Microsoft Japan) |
| **Qualifier** | Optional | Strength of the claim; makes it **more honest** | probably, likely, some, many; avoid "always/never" |
| **Rebuttal** | Optional | When would I be wrong? | "Assumes inquiries stay simple"; builds credibility |

- Shifts question from "Do I agree?" to **"Which part is weak?"**
- **Warrant** = most important and most often forgotten.
- **Legal arguments:** rarely dispute facts; dispute **what facts mean** (warrant grounded in legal principles/precedent).
- **Scientific arguments:** cautious ("evidence suggests"), intellectually honest, **inductive**.
- Skeleton stays similar across professions; the **cladding** (standards of evidence, reasoning, uncertainty) changes.

> **CLA:** "Our six-month pilot showed productivity rose 12%. Therefore, permanently adopt a four-day workweek." Which Toulmin element is missing?
> - **Warrant** ✓
>
> **CLA:** Two lawyers accept identical facts about a factory accident; one argues negligence, the other reasonable conduct. What does this illustrate?
> - **Disagreement centres on the warrant linking facts to legal conclusions** ✓
>
> **Q:** Match the reasoning terms:
> - Explanation connecting evidence to conclusion = **Warrant** ✓
> - Degree to which premises make an inductive conclusion likely = **Strength** ✓
> - Conclusion from patterns/observations, probable not certain = **Inductive Reasoning** ✓
> - Reasoning from a general rule to a specific conclusion = **Deductive Reasoning** ✓
> - If premises are true, conclusion must be true = **Validity** ✓

### Validity, Soundness, Strength, Cogency

$$\text{Sound} = \text{Valid} + \text{True premises}$$
$$\text{Cogent} = \text{Strong} + \text{True / well-supported premises}$$

| Term | Family | Meaning |
|------|--------|---------|
| **Validity** | Deductive | If premises true, conclusion **must** be true; **form, not content** |
| **Soundness** | Deductive | Valid + all premises actually true (deductive gold standard) |
| **Strength** | Inductive | If premises true, conclusion **probably** true; comes in **degrees** |
| **Cogency** | Inductive | Strong + premises true/well supported (inductive gold standard) |

- **Validity test:** can premises be true and conclusion false? No = valid.
- **Dolphin is a fish** (all fish live in water; dolphin lives in water): **invalid**, so cannot be sound.
- **Every sound argument is valid; NOT every valid argument is sound.**
- **Crows:** "every crow I've seen is black" = moderately strong; "one black crow" = very weak.
- **Lottery example:** strong structure even with a false premise, but **NOT** cogent.

> **CLA:** "All employees who miss the deadline will be penalised. Priya missed it. Therefore, Priya will be penalised." Structure holds regardless of whether premises are true. Which property?
> - **Validity** ✓
>
> **CLA:** An inductive argument has a reasonable structure but one premise later turns out false. Classification?
> - **Strong but not cogent** ✓
>
> **Q:** An argument can be logically valid even when its premises are false.
> - **True** ✓
>
> **Q:** A strong argument is cogent only when its premises are true or well supported.
> - **True** ✓

### Evaluating Evidence (Five Tests)

| Test | Question | Red Flag / Example |
|------|----------|--------------------|
| **Relevance** | Does it bear on the claim? | "Great camera" is **NOT** evidence of a good investment |
| **Sufficiency** | Is there enough, and diverse? | One anecdote; "most people say" (cherry-picked) |
| **Credibility** | Expertise, track record, **bias / conflict of interest**? | Company-funded study: **NOT** automatically wrong, needs **more scrutiny** |
| **Currency** | Is it current? | 15-year-old internet statistics; revised medical studies |
| **Counter-evidence** | Does it survive contrary evidence? | Ignoring contrary data = **cherry picking** |

- Goal: tell **feeling persuaded** from **being justifiably persuaded** (not cynicism).

> **CLA:** A beverage company funds its own study showing its drink improves long-term health and uses it for a public-health claim. Best response?
> - **Treat the findings with greater scrutiny due to conflict of interest** ✓
>
> **Q:** A strong argument considers evidence that contradicts its conclusion rather than relying only on supporting evidence.
> - **True** ✓

## MODULE 4: Fallacies and Cognitive Biases

---

### Warrant Failures

| Part | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Claim** | What someone wants you to believe |
| **Evidence** | Data / facts given |
| **Warrant** | Reasoning **connecting evidence to claim** |

- **Fallacy:** a **broken / weak warrant** - evidence may be true, but does not support the conclusion
- **Key question:** "Where does the warrant break?" - **NOT** "Is the evidence true?"
- **Hybrid work example:** productivity fell 10% (real evidence) does **NOT** prove hybrid caused it
- A correct conclusion **≠** sound reasoning

> **CLA:** Sales rose 15% the month after a new logo launch; manager concludes the logo caused it. Which part of the argument is weakest?
> - **The warrant linking evidence to the claim** ✓
>
> **Q:** The reasoning that connects evidence to a claim
> - **Warrant** ✓

---

### Attack and Distortion Fallacies

| Fallacy | Definition | Example |
|---------|-----------|---------|
| **Ad hominem** | Attack the **person**, not the argument | Kodak dismissing digital advocates (Kodak invented digital camera, 1975); "You've been here 5 minutes" to churn analyst |
| **Straw man** | Distort argument into **weaker / extreme version**, attack that | "Hybrid 2-3 days" -> "You want everyone home all the time?"; remove 3 features -> "strip product to nothing" |
| **False dichotomy** | Only **two choices** when many exist; creates urgency | "Cut prices or lose the market" (ignores bundling, loyalty, service) |

- **Straw man test:** put original argument beside response - same thing?
- **False dichotomy test:** "Are these the only real choices?"
- In all three, **evidence is pushed to the background**

> **CLA:** Senior executive replies to a junior analyst's declining-margin data: "You've been with us only three months, so you clearly don't understand our business."
> - **Ad hominem** ✓
>
> **CLA:** Proposal to trim 3 low-performing social channels; colleague replies "So you want to abandon social media entirely and lose all our younger customers?"
> - **Straw man** ✓
>
> **CLA:** CEO: "We either match their prices immediately or exit this market," ignoring bundling, loyalty, differentiation.
> - **False dichotomy** ✓

---

### Causal Reasoning Fallacies

| Fallacy | Definition | Corrective |
|---------|-----------|-----------|
| **Post hoc ergo propter hoc** | "**After this, therefore because of this**" (Instagram campaign -> traffic up) | Ask "What else changed at the same time?" |
| **Appeal to authority** | Accept claim because person is **important / successful / qualified** ("I've built 3 companies, Rs 499 max") | Is the person expert **in this question**? What evidence? Test with data |
| **Slippery slope** | One small step **inevitably** leads to chain of serious consequences | **Possible ≠ inevitable**; check each link (necessary and sufficient conditions) |

- **Seeing a connection ≠ establishing a cause**
- **Expertise tells you where to look**, **NOT** a substitute for evidence

> **CLA:** Board member: "If we allow one remote day a week, soon everyone will demand permanent work from home, and culture will completely collapse."
> - **Slippery slope** ✓

---

### Sampling Fallacies

| Fallacy | Definition | Example |
|---------|-----------|---------|
| **Hasty generalisation** | Small or **unusual / unrepresentative sample** -> big claim | **New Coke**: ~2 lakh tested; measured **taste preference**, **NOT** brand attachment / nostalgia -> failed |
| **Anecdotal evidence** | One person's (or a handful of) experience as a broad pattern | Founder: "I needed it, so others must" - sample size of one |

- **Lesson:** evidence must **represent the behaviour you want to predict**
- **Rule:** a story gives a question, a sample gives evidence, **neither automatically gives a conclusion**
- Ask "How much can this evidence tell me?" - is it enough and representative?

> **CLA:** Fragrance tested on 50,000 customers who preferred it; launch fails because loyal customers were attached to the original scent (never measured). The error?
> - **The test measured preference but not what the decision truly required** ✓

---

### Reasoning Shortcuts About Change

| Shortcut | Logic | Example | Ask |
|----------|-------|---------|-----|
| **Appeal to nature** | Better because **natural / organic** | "Organic growth always healthier than acquisitions" (vs Facebook - Instagram, WhatsApp) | Does it fit this business, market, time? |
| **Appeal to tradition** | "**We've always done it this way**" | Annual reviews; Adobe, Microsoft changed in 2010s | "Is this still working?" |
| **Appeal to novelty** | Better because **new** | "Newest AI platform must outperform" | Benchmarks, compared results |

- **Do NOT judge the idea by its label**

> **CLA:** Retailer rejects AI forecasting: "We have manually forecasted for thirty years, and that experience keeps us profitable," without checking current performance.
> - **Appeal to tradition** ✓

---

### Cognitive Biases and System 1 / System 2

- **Cognitive bias:** automatic shortcuts in our own thinking; intelligence / expertise give **NO immunity**; a matter of **cognitive architecture**, not character
- Based on **Daniel Kahneman**, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*

| Feature | **System 1** | **System 2** |
|---------|-------------|-------------|
| Nature | **Fast, automatic, effortless** | **Slow, deliberate, effortful** |
| Examples | Angry face, 2+2, empty-road driving, "bread and butter" | 17 x 24, job offers, DCF, tight parking |
| Role | **Default author of most judgments**; builds best story from limited evidence | **Lazy by default**; often **rubber-stamps** System 1 |

- **Intuition is not inherently wrong** - experts have calibrated pattern recognition; problem is knowing **when to override**
- **Pricing, hiring, walking away from a deal** -> need System 2

> **CLA:** Manager instantly senses a client's frustration from her expression without conscious analysis.
> - **System 1** ✓

---

### Confirmation Bias

- **Definition:** seek, notice, interpret and remember information that **confirms existing beliefs**; dismiss / explain away contradictions
- **Examples:** leading market-research questions; interview snap judgment in **first 90 seconds**
- **Antidote:** **structural, NOT willpower** - actively seek **disconfirming evidence**; "What would prove me wrong?"; formal **devil's advocate**

> **CLA:** Founder explains away 3 negative reviews of 10 as reviewers "not understanding the product" instead of investigating.
> - **Confirmation bias** ✓
>
> **CLA:** Strategy team assigns a member to argue against an acquisition and seek data proving it wrong. Structural antidote to?
> - **Confirmation bias** ✓
>
> **Q:** Seeking information that supports existing beliefs
> - **Confirmation bias** ✓

---

### Availability Heuristic

- **Definition:** System 1 replaces "How likely?" with "**How easily can I recall an example?**"
- Distorted by **recency, vividness, media coverage, memorability**
- **Examples:** plane crash feels riskier than car accident (data: car accidents kill more); Zerodha success story vs unseen failures
- **Antidote:** compare with **actual base rates / broader data**

> **CLA:** After heavy media coverage of a competitor's cyber breach, CFO calls cyberattacks the biggest risk though audit data shows supply-chain failures are far more frequent.
> - **Availability heuristic** ✓
>
> **Q:** Judging likelihood by what comes easily to mind
> - **Availability heuristic** ✓

---

### Anchoring Bias

- **Definition:** an **initial (even arbitrary) number** influences later numerical estimates
- **Spinning wheel experiment:** rigged high / low number shifted estimates of % African countries in a region
- **Whoever states the first figure sets the anchor**; counteroffers pulled toward it
- **Awareness reduces but does NOT eliminate** it (judges, real-estate agents)

| Antidote | Detail |
|----------|--------|
| **Set the anchor** | Thoughtfully, with justification |
| **Private independent estimate** | Write before hearing the other side's number |
| **Multiple estimates** | Many people, many starting points |

> **CLA:** Candidate states a very high salary first; recruiter's counteroffer ends up near it, beyond policy.
> - **Anchoring bias** ✓
>
> **CLA:** Founder privately writes her own valuation from comparables before hearing the investor's opening offer. Protects against?
> - **Being unconsciously pulled toward the investor's anchor** ✓
>
> **Q:** An initial number influences later judgements
> - **Anchoring** ✓

---

### In-group Bias and Social Proof

| Bias | Definition | Relation |
|------|-----------|----------|
| **In-group bias** | Favour / trust people of **our group** (school, function, background); doubt outsiders | **Confirmation bias at scale** |
| **Social proof** | Treat what similar / respected others believe as **evidence of correctness** | **Availability heuristic applied to consensus** |

- Same-business-school leadership = **unconscious echo chamber**; confidence **not independently earned**
- "Recurring revenue always beats one-time sales" - spread by **repetition and reinforcement**, not testing

> **CLA:** Same-school leadership team approves a risky strategy, reassured by fellow alumni agreeing, while a non-alumnus's documented objections are ignored.
> - **In-group bias and social proof** ✓

---

### Propaganda and Manufactured Consent

- **Propaganda:** **deliberate, systematic** attempt to shape perceptions, opinions, behaviour by **engineering cognitive shortcuts**, **NOT** open argument and evidence
- **Herman and Chomsky**, *Manufacturing Consent* (**1988**), **propaganda model**: institutions rarely lie outright; they set the debate, choose credible voices, repeat some facts, **bury** others

| Technique | Exploits |
|-----------|----------|
| **Repetition** | Availability heuristic |
| **Selective framing** | Confirmation bias |
| **Trusted spokespeople** | In-group bias |
| **Opening frame** | Anchoring |

| | **Legitimate persuasion** | **Manufactured consent** |
|--|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| Basis | Genuine evidence and reasoning | Selective framing, repetition, borrowed authority |
| Test | **Stands up to scrutiny** | Needs audience **NOT** seeing full picture |

- Business examples: layoffs as "right sizing"; scripted town hall; board deck leading with the one rising metric
- Techniques are neutral - **intent and what is left out** decide
- Ask: "What would this narrative need to **leave out** to stay this convincing?"

> **CLA:** Investor deck highlights only the one growing product line, omits three declining segments, repeats "market leader" without data.
> - **Manufactured consent** ✓
>
> **Q:** Deliberately shaping perceptions and behaviour at scale
> - **Propaganda** ✓

### The Credibility Checklist: Four Signals of Expertise

| Signal | Shows | Limitation |
|--------|-------|------------|
| **Knowledge** | Facts about a topic | Expertise = knowing **which facts matter** (chess masters **recognise patterns**, do **NOT** consider more options) |
| **Track record** | Hard-to-fake results; **strongest evidence** | Works **only with clear, quick feedback** (chess yes; political forecasting no) |
| **Credentials** | Degrees, licences; a **shortcut** | Show **past learning**, not skill **that day** |
| **Peer recognition** | Awards, citations; insiders judge insiders | Fields reward **fitting in / fashionable ideas** over being right |

- **Tetlock:** well-credentialed forecasters did **barely better than chance**.
- **20 years of experience** may be **1 year repeated 20 times**.
- **Nguyen:** all four signals are things **most of us cannot check ourselves**.
- **Central claim:** expertise is understood through **networks of trust**, which decide whose voices we hear.

> **CLA:** Why does an expert's track record fail to reliably signal expertise in fields like political forecasting?
> - **The domain lacks rapid, unambiguous feedback required to isolate skill from chance** ✓

> **CLA:** Why do formal credentials and peer recognition often fail as proxies for real-time operational competence?
> - **They capture past learning or social conformity rather than present tacit performance** ✓

## MODULE 6: Analytical and Cross-Cultural Thinking

### Analytical Thinking: Competing Explanations

| Concept | Key Point |
|---------|-----------|
| **Shift** | From spotting bad reasoning to **building better arguments** |
| **Core habit** | Do **NOT** stop at the first plausible explanation; **generate alternatives and test them against each other** |
| **Post hoc fix** | Ask "what else could explain it?" (ad campaign, competitor supply problem, seasonal demand) |
| **Netflix / Qwikster (2011)** | Backlash explained by **price**, **confusion** (two services/accounts/billing), **competition**; **NOT mutually exclusive** |
| **Better analyst question** | How much **evidence** for each? Which **accounts for most evidence**? Which can we **act on**? |
| **Reversal of Qwikster** | Shows what Netflix believed; does **NOT prove** why customers left |
| **System 1 trap** | Grab the explanation that protects the story you want ("market isn't ready" vs "something wrong with our product?") |
| **Method** | **Generate -> rank by plausibility and testability -> find evidence that distinguishes** |

> **Q:** Netflix reversed the Qwikster decision quickly. Does this prove why customers were leaving?
> - **No, it only shows what the company thought drove the backlash** ✓

> **Q:** Sales rose after a rebrand. What is the analytical response to the claim "the rebrand caused it"?
> - **Generate alternative explanations (advertising, competitor supply issue, season) and test them against each other** ✓

### Uncertainty, Probability, and Calibration

| Concept | Key Point |
|---------|-----------|
| **Calibration** | **Confidence matches how often you are right** (100 predictions at 70% -> about 70 correct) |
| **Overconfidence** | Treating uncertain as almost guaranteed |
| **Over-hedging** | Uncertain about everything; not useful when a leader must decide |
| **Goal** | **Quantify** uncertainty, **NOT** eliminate it; decide with incomplete information |
| **Precision vs accuracy** | "Exactly 10,000 units" is precise, **NOT** necessarily accurate |
| **Decision basis** | **Distribution of outcomes** (high demand -> capacity; low demand -> avoid lock-in) |
| **Five-year plan risk** | Assumptions in spreadsheets **start looking like facts**; hard-to-reverse commitments made as if forecast were certain |
| **Forecast questions** | How confident? What assumptions? What if wrong? **What would make us change our minds?** |

| Founder statement | Assessment |
|-------------------|------------|
| "We will hit **$2M**" | **Hides risk**; looks like knowledge, may be a guess |
| "**60%** chance of $1.5M-$2.5M; **15%** above $3M if pilot converts; **10%** below $1M if pilot stalls" | **Makes uncertainty visible**; more honest, not pessimistic |

#### Three Calibration Questions
| # | Question |
|---|----------|
| 1 | **How confident am I?** (give a number) |
| 2 | **What would have to be true for me to be wrong?** |
| 3 | **Have I been right this often when I felt this confident?** (most people skip) |

- **Inductive link:** judge **how much confidence the evidence deserves**; review later whether confidence matched reality.

> **Q:** A manager says "I'm 90% sure" but is wrong about half the time. What does this show?
> - **Confidence is not calibrated** ✓

> **Q:** Why is "we will sell 10,000 units" a weaker statement than "best estimate about 10,000, with a meaningful chance of more or less"?
> - **Precision is not accuracy; the second shows the range of outcomes and supports better decisions** ✓

### Complexity vs Confusion

| | **Complexity** | **Confusion** |
|---|---------------|---------------|
| **Lies in** | The **problem** | The **thinker** |
| **Meaning** | Interacting variables, **feedback loops, delayed effects, unexpected consequences** | Not yet worked out how the parts fit |

| Failure mode | What happens | Fix |
|--------------|--------------|-----|
| **1. Confusion mistaken for complexity** | "Too complicated to analyse" (e.g., churn) | **Break it down**: first week vs 3 months; onboarding; price rise; enterprise customers |
| **2. Complexity mistaken for confusion (oversimplifying)** | Neat single story ("sales fell due to pricing") | Ask **which variables matter, how they interact, what evidence separates effects**; explanation must **NOT** be simpler than the evidence |

- **2008 crisis:** risk reduced to **simplified models and scores**; danger = forgetting **model is not reality**; a number does **NOT** reduce underlying complexity.
- **"It's too complicated"** = a **prompt, not a conclusion**: what specifically? which variables? which can be analysed independently?
- **Two diagnostic questions:** (1) Is it genuinely many interacting parts, or have I not broken it down? (2) Does the evidence support a simpler explanation, or is a simple story just easier to tell?

> **Q:** "Complexity belongs to the problem; confusion belongs to the thinker." True or false?
> - **True** ✓

> **Q:** A team says churn is "too complicated". First step?
> - **Break it down into smaller questions (timing of exit, onboarding, price, customer segment)** ✓

#### Three Habits
| Habit |
|-------|
| **Generate competing explanations** |
| **Calibrate confidence** |
| **Distinguish complexity from confusion** |

### Analytical Frameworks

| Framework | Idea |
|-----------|------|
| **MECE** | **Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive** parts |
| **Pyramid principle** | **Conclusion first**, evidence beneath |
| **Hypothesis-driven** | Start with a possible explanation; look for evidence to **test** it |
| **OODA loop** | **Observe, Orient, Decide, Act**; fast-changing situations |
| **SWOT** | Same fact (competitor price cut) can be a **threat or opportunity**; framework does **NOT** say which box |

- **Ted Chiang (New Yorker, 2023):** ChatGPT = **blurry JPEG of the web**; **compression keeps the picture, loses detail**.
- Frameworks **compress** complexity; problem = **forgetting what was lost**.
- Use them (organise, find gaps, communicate, move decisions), then ask **what did it leave out / flatten?** Framework = **first draft, NOT final truth**.
- **Hypothesis trap:** "retention fell because of price" ignores onboarding difficulty, competitor features; **is it true or does it just look true?**
- **Clean answer is NOT necessarily true; neat framework is NOT necessarily complete; confident conclusion is NOT necessarily well supported.**

> **Q:** Why can two analysts place the same fact in different SWOT boxes?
> - **Business reality is more ambiguous than the four categories** ✓

> **Q:** What is the main risk of using a framework?
> - **Forgetting that it is a compressed simplification and what was lost** ✓

### Cultural Dimensions of Critical Thinking

| Concept | Key Point |
|---------|-----------|
| **Situated reasoning** | We reason within **language, history, religion, region, profession, family, institutional norms**; argumentation is **NOT culturally neutral** |
| **Culture shapes** | Evidence, appeal to authority, disagreement, persuasion, dignity, silence, storytelling, trust |
| **Ethnocentrism** | Own culture as the centre; critical thinking turned into **"think like me"**; familiar = valid, unfamiliar = inferior |
| **Caution** | Patterns are **tendencies, NOT laws**; no culture is monolithic; goal is **interpretation, NOT stereotyping** |
| **Adichie: Danger of a Single Story** | Single story **makes one truth the only truth**; flattens, excludes, distorts judgment |

> **Q:** According to Adichie, what is the problem with a single story?
> - **It makes one truth the only truth, not that it is entirely false** ✓

### Evidence and Authority Across Cultures

| Evidence type | Cross-cultural point |
|---------------|----------------------|
| **Dominant standard** | Written, quantified, peer-reviewed, generalisable (Western scientific/legal) = **not the only rational justification** |
| **Surat textile family; Kochi spice exporter** | Oral, relational, generational knowledge; **do not demand an RCT**, **do not dismiss as anecdote**; check **source, consistency, track record, credibility in community** |
| **Personal testimony** | Rajasthan health worker: **less statistically broad, but contextually precise** |
| **Case examples** | Powerful but risky (can persuade disproportionately); make abstractions concrete; **structural in Nyaya** |
| **Research studies** | Shaped by questions, methods, samples, variables, interpretation; may **not travel**; global benchmark can **obscure local realities** |
| **Key question to add** | **Whose evidence is automatically strong or weak, and why?** |

| Authority basis | Example |
|-----------------|---------|
| **Institutional** | Degrees, certifications, positions (chartered accountant) |
| **Relational** | Seniority, lineage, community standing (elder trader) |
| **Intellectual** | Retired professor |
| **Moral** | Local leader |

| Pattern | Feature |
|---------|---------|
| **Hierarchical** | Age, designation, family status, pedigree decide who speaks (Rajkot family firm, Delhi government office) |
| **Flattened** | Juniors challenge seniors (Bangalore start-up, Pune research lab) |

- **German-Indian negotiation:** Germans want formal decision-maker; Indians send respected senior -> read as **evasive** vs **cold**; **neither is necessarily wrong**.
- **Appeal to authority is reasonable when** authority is **expert, impartial, relevant**; **a problem when** used to **shut down inquiry, on unrelated matters, or to hide lack of evidence**.
- Ask: does authority establish **credibility, experience, trust, legitimacy, or merely power**?

> **Q:** A distant analyst dismisses a rural health worker's account of a failing vaccination drive as "merely anecdotal". What is the critical failure?
> - **Ignoring testimony that is contextually precise even if not statistically broad** ✓

> **Q:** When is an appeal to authority reasonable?
> - **When the authority is genuinely expert, impartial, and relevant** ✓

### High-Context Communication and Face-Saving

| Feature | **Low-context** | **High-context** (Edward T. Hall) |
|---------|-----------------|-----------------------------------|
| Meaning carried by | **Explicit words** | Words + **relationship, status, silence, timing, tone, gestures, shared history** |
| Values | Clarity, directness, precision | **Harmony, dignity, restraint, social perception** |

- **Face** = social dignity, honour, being respected in relationships (not vanity); Indian parallel **Izat**.
- Direct: "This plan will not work." Indirect: "It's a bold idea; we will need to examine it carefully." -> need **critical translation**.
- Disagreement is **real but encoded**, not absent.

| Phrase | Possible meaning |
|--------|------------------|
| **"We will revert"** | Will respond, or **not ready to respond** |
| **"Kindly do the needful"** | Can be a **deferral** |
| **"We will take this forward"** | Movement, **not necessarily agreement** |
| **"There may be some difficulty"** | Gentle but firm signal of **impossibility** |

- **Better manager questions:** What obstacles make compression difficult? What scope changes if the date stays? Reduce features rather than compress testing?
- **Indirectness is NOT bad; it needs different questioning skills.**
- Listen to **dictionary meaning and social meaning**.

> **Q:** An Indian team lead says "We will study the matter and do the needful" about a compressed timeline. What may this signal?
> - **Difficulty or need for more time, encoded to avoid open confrontation** ✓

> **Q:** Face-saving communication is mainly motivated by what?
> - **Preserving the relationship and dignity, not evasiveness** ✓

### Five Cross-Cultural Mistakes

| # | Mistake | Point |
|---|---------|-------|
| 1 | **Unfamiliar = irrational** | Story, analogy, reputation, indirection are not poor thinking |
| 2 | **Romanticising other cultures** | Also a single story; Indian logic can exclude, oral traditions can be unjust, high-context can **conceal power imbalances** |
| 3 | **Directness = honesty; indirectness = deceit** | Moral quality depends on **intention, context, effect** |
| 4 | **Ignoring power** | Who defines evidence, who speaks, whose accent is professional, whose story is the benchmark |
| 5 | **Overcorrecting into stereotype** | Turning high/low-context into **fate** ends critical thinking |

- **Persuasion needs logical validity AND social receptivity**; **manner of argument is part of the argument**.

> **Q:** Assuming non-Western traditions are always wiser or more holistic is which mistake?
> - **Romanticising other cultures (another single story)** ✓

### Practical Cross-Cultural Strategies

| Setting | Strategy |
|---------|----------|
| **Emails** | Low-context: explicit request, deadline, rationale. High-context: **soften, acknowledge relationship, invite not demand** |
| **Meetings** | Notice who speaks/hesitates/is silent; silence of juniors **NOT** agreement; use **small groups, written feedback, one-on-ones, anonymous polling** |
| **Negotiations** | Warmth **NOT** weakness; formality **NOT** hostility; relationship phase (chai) = **trust and character assessed** |
| **Presentations** | **Layered evidence: claim -> data -> example -> apply to present case** (Nyaya structure) |
| **Feedback** | Separate **critique from humiliation**; frame as **shared problem** ("report did not yet give the decision support we need") |
| **Conflict resolution** | Offer a **face-saving exit**; **strategic empathy** (people are social beings, not logic engines) |

> **Q:** Which is the culturally sensitive phrasing for a high-context email?
> - **"We would appreciate your thoughts by Friday so we can align next steps with your expectations"** ✓

> **Q:** Silence from junior participants in a meeting most likely indicates what?
> - **The meeting structure may not allow safe disagreement; it does not mean agreement** ✓

### Nyaya: Five-Step Reasoning

| | Western syllogism | Nyaya |
|---|-------------------|-------|
| Steps | **3** (major premise, minor premise, conclusion) | **5** |
| Origin | **Aristotle, 4th century BCE** | Gautama/Akshapada; **Nyaya Sutras ~2nd century BCE-2nd century CE**; one of **6 Astika schools** |
| Direction | General -> particular; abstract; deductive | Pursuit of **right knowledge / valid cognition** |
| Example | Optional/none required | **Essential and structural** |

- **Nyaya** = **Ni (lead) + Aya (go)**: that by which one is led to a conclusion; also means **justice** (Nyayalaya = court, Nyayadhish = judge, Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita).

| Step | Term | Meaning | Hill example |
|------|------|---------|--------------|
| 1 | **Pratijna** | **Proposition** (claim) | There is fire on that hill |
| 2 | **Hetu** | **Reason** | Because there is smoke |
| 3 | **Udaharana** | **Example / analogy** | Wherever smoke, fire, as in a kitchen |
| 4 | **Upanaya** | **Application** to the particular case | This hill has smoke invariably linked with fire |
| 5 | **Nigamana** | **Conclusion** | Therefore, there is fire on that hill |

| Business case | Udaharana (shared experience) | Conclusion |
|---------------|-------------------------------|------------|
| **Arjun, checkout feature** (reason: payment-confusion tickets rising) | Simplified login last year raised completion by **12%** | Redesign checkout before festive launch |
| **Hyderabad call centre attrition** (inconsistent shift allocation) | Transparent night-shift rotation last year cut attrition | Restore transparency in shift allocation |

- Udaharana **anchors reasoning in shared experience**, lets the listener **verify through what they already know**, and works where **relational memory** matters or **trust in data is uneven**.

> **Q:** Match the Nyaya steps to the hill argument:
> - Pratijna = **There is fire on that hill** ✓
> - Hetu = **Because there is smoke** ✓
> - Udaharana = **Wherever there is smoke there is fire, as in a kitchen** ✓
> - Upanaya = **This hill has smoke linked with fire** ✓
> - Nigamana = **Therefore there is fire on that hill** ✓

> **Q:** How does Nyaya's treatment of the example differ from the Western syllogism?
> - **The example (Udaharana) is an essential structural step, not decoration** ✓

### Tarka

| Concept | Key Point |
|---------|-----------|
| **Tarka** | Hypothetical / dialectical reasoning; **tests a claim by supposing its opposite and tracing absurd consequences**; like **reductio ad absurdum**; later logic called **Tarka Shastra** |
| **Collaborative form** | "If you accept this assumption, what else must follow? Does it match what we observe?" |
| **Logistics app case** | Cyberattack claim predicts unusual traffic/suspicious IPs/data exfiltration; logs show **normal external traffic** and **database issues at peak load** -> **internal scaling failure** |
| **Function** | **Disciplines the imagination**; blocks dramatic but unsupported explanations |
| **Cross-cultural value** | Reduces blame: "Let's explore what follows if we accept this idea" **preserves dignity** with rigour intact |
| **Argument cultures** | Defeat the opponent ("win at all costs") vs **clarify while preserving the relationship** |

- Rigorous logic **need not be aggressive**; Nyaya and Tarka are a **live tradition** relevant to business, governance, technology, law, public communication, entrepreneurship.

> **Q:** What is Tarka in the strict Nyaya sense?
> - **Testing a claim by supposing its opposite and showing absurd or unacceptable consequences follow** ✓

> **Q:** Logs show normal external traffic but database errors at peak orders. What does Tarka reasoning conclude about a cyberattack claim?
> - **It does not fit the evidence; internal scaling failure is more probable** ✓

## Quick Reference: Analytical and Cross-Cultural Thinking

| Situation | Action/Answer |
|-----------|---------------|
| Data shows a pattern | Generate **several explanations**, rank, find **distinguishing evidence** |
| Asked "how sure are you?" | Give a **number**; check **past hit rate** |
| Forecast presented as one number | Ask for **range, assumptions, what if wrong, what changes our mind** |
| "It's too complicated" | **Break it down** (confusion, not complexity) |
| Neat single-cause story | Check for **oversimplification** (complexity) |

| Indirect reply ("do the needful") | Ask **specific, face-saving questions** |
| Public error by a counterpart | Offer a **face-saving exit** (strategic empathy) |
| Need persuasive structure | **Nyaya five steps** (claim, reason, example, application, conclusion) |
| Testing a dramatic claim | **Tarka**: trace its consequences against observed evidence |

## Key Decision Rules Summary

| Rule | Detail |
|------|--------|
| Competing explanations | Never stop at the first plausible story; explanations are **NOT mutually exclusive** |
| Calibration | Confidence should match hit rate; quantify, do not eliminate, uncertainty |
| Precision | Precise is **NOT** accurate; decide on the distribution of outcomes |
| Complexity vs confusion | Complexity = problem; confusion = thinker |
| Simplification | Explanation must **NOT** be simpler than the evidence |
| Frameworks | Useful first drafts; always ask what was lost |
| Single story | One truth made the only truth distorts judgment |
| Evidence | Culturally mediated; testimony can be contextually precise |
| Authority | Reasonable if expert, impartial, relevant; check for power or concealment |
| Communication | Low-context = explicit words; high-context = relationship, tone, silence |
| Face | Preserve dignity; indirectness is encoded disagreement, not absence of it |
| Style | Manner of argument is part of the argument; judge by intention, context, effect |
| Nyaya | Pratijna, Hetu, Udaharana, Upanaya, Nigamana; example is structural |
| Tarka | Suppose the opposite, trace consequences, preserve the relationship |
