## 1.1 Principles of Finance

### 1.1.1 Time Value of Money Principle

#### Definition
- **Principle 1: Money has a time value.**
- **A rupee received today is worth more than a rupee received in the future.**

#### Key Concept
- Examined through a choice between receiving money **today** or in the **future**.

| Choice | Payoff | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Choice 1 | ₹1,00,000 **today** (100% probability) | None |
| Choice 2 | ₹1,00,000 **after one year** (100% probability) | None |

- Both choices are **risk-free**; only **timing** differs.
- A rational person prefers **Choice 1**.
- **Reason:** ₹1,00,000 received today can be **invested** (e.g., bank fixed deposit) to earn interest, giving **more than ₹1,00,000** after one year.
- Money today is more valuable because of its **investment potential**; **timing influences financial value**.

### 1.1.2 Risk and Return Principle

#### Definition
- **Principle 2: Risk and return go hand in hand.**
- **A safe rupee is worth more than a risky rupee.**
- **Investors are risk-averse** and **demand extra return for taking additional risk**.

#### Formula
- **Expected value** = probability-weighted outcome:

$$E(X) = \sum_{i} p_i \times X_i$$

- $p_i$ = probability of outcome $i$; $X_i$ = value of outcome $i$.

#### Key Concept

| Choice | Payoff | Expected Value | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Choice 1 | ₹1,00,000 with 100% probability | $1,00,000 \times 1 = ₹1,00,000$ | **None** |
| Choice 2 | ₹2,00,000 with 50%; ₹0 with 50% | $2,00,000 \times 0.5 + 0 \times 0.5 = ₹1,00,000$ | **Risky** |

- **Same expected value**, but **risk differs**.
- Salary analogy: **fixed salary** of ₹1 lakh per month (Choice 1) vs fully **variable salary** of ₹2 lakh if the company does well and nothing otherwise, at 50% each (Choice 2).
- Most people prefer **Choice 1** because human beings are generally **risk-averse** and prefer a **safe rupee over a risky rupee at any given point in time**.

#### Compensation for Risk
- To make an investor **indifferent**, Choice 2 must be improved, e.g., probability of ₹2,00,000 raised to **60%** (₹0 at 40%).
- New expected value of Choice 2:

$$2,00,000 \times 0.6 + 0 \times 0.4 = ₹1,20,000$$

- The extra ₹20,000 of expected value is the **additional return (risk premium)** required to compensate for the **extra risk**.
- **Bearing extra risk → demand for additional return or premium.**
- Both principles are used **throughout corporate finance**.

## 1.2 Forms of Business Organisation

### 1.2.1 Sole Proprietorship and Partnership

#### Definition
- **Sole proprietorship:** a business **owned by one person**; most appropriate when the business is **small and not expected to grow much** (e.g., a **freelancer**).
- **Partnership (general partnership):** **two or more persons** come together to form a business; owners often **know each other intimately**.

#### Key Concept

| Feature | Sole Proprietorship | Partnership |
|---|---|---|
| Owner called | **Proprietor** | **Partners** |
| Number of owners | **Only one** | **2 – 50** |
| Regulating law (India) | **No specific law** | **Indian Partnership Act, 1932** |
| Liability | **Unlimited** | **Unlimited** |
| Life | **Limited** | **Limited** |
| Separate tax on business income | **No** | Avoids double taxation |

#### Key Disadvantages
- **Unlimited liability:** liability of owners is **not limited** to the wealth contributed when starting the business; **personal wealth is at stake** and can be **seized** if the business cannot repay its borrowings.
- **Limited life:** the business **comes to an end** if the sole proprietor or any of the partners **dies**.

#### Key Advantage
- **Not subject to separate income tax on business:** business income becomes part of the **personal income** of the proprietor/partner, taxed at their **personal income tax slab rate**.
- Both forms **avoid double taxation**.

| Form (India) | Tax Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Sole proprietorship** | Business does **not** attract separate tax on business income; income is part of the proprietor's personal income, taxed at the **personal income tax slab rate** |
| **Partnership** | Business pays a **flat 30% income tax** on taxable income; **partners do not pay income tax** on their share of profits |

### 1.2.2 Companies

#### Definition
- **Company / Corporation:** a **separate legal entity**, distinct from its owners; it **can sue others and can be sued by others**.
- Focus form of business in corporate finance.

#### Key Concept

| Feature | Company |
|---|---|
| Owners called | **Shareholders** |
| Number of owners | Depends on whether it is a **private** or **public** limited company |
| Regulating law | **Companies Act, 2013** |
| Legal status | **Separate legal entity** |

#### Key Advantages
- **Limited liability:** shareholders' liability is **limited to the amount contributed** when buying shares; they **can lose no more** than that. **Personal wealth is not at stake** even if the company cannot repay its debts.
- **Perpetual life (perpetual succession / going concern):** even if managers **quit or are dismissed**, the company **survives**; today's shareholders can **sell their shares to new investors without disrupting operations**.
- **Going concern:** continues **on and on** until an event such as **bankruptcy or an acquisition**.

#### Key Disadvantage: Double Taxation
- Company is subject to **separate income tax on business income** at the **corporate income tax rate**.
- In addition, **dividends** (and capital gains) are **taxable in the hands of shareholders**.
- Income is therefore taxed **twice** → **double taxation**.

### 1.2.3 Private and Public Limited Companies

#### Key Concept

| Feature | Private Limited Company | Public Limited Company |
|---|---|---|
| Minimum shareholders | **2** | **7** |
| Maximum shareholders | **200** | **No restriction** |
| Minimum directors | **2** | **3** |
| Minimum paid-up capital | **None** (earlier ₹1,00,000) | **₹5,00,000** |
| Share transferability | **Restricted** | **Freely transferable** |
| Suffix | **Pvt. Ltd.** | **Ltd.** |
| Example | Tata Sons Pvt. Ltd. | Tata Steel Ltd. (listed) |

- **Key distinguishing difference:** **transferability of shares**.
- A private limited company can be started with **virtually no capital**.

#### Names and Legal Form
- Name ending in **Pvt. Ltd.** → **private limited company**.
- Name ending simply in **Limited** (without "Private") → **public limited company**.
- **"Limited"** in both names indicates that the **liability of the owners is limited**.

#### Public Limited vs Listed Company
- A public limited company **need not be listed**; it can be **unlisted**.

| Company | Status |
|---|---|
| **BSE Limited** (Bombay Stock Exchange) | Public limited, **listed** (shares listed on **NSE**) |
| **NSE India Limited** (National Stock Exchange) | Public limited, **unlisted**; planning an **IPO** to become listed (on **BSE**) |

- In India, regulations **do not permit a stock exchange to list its shares on itself**.

### 1.2.4 Hybrid Forms of Business Organisation

#### Definition
- **Hybrids** combine features (e.g., **tax advantage**) of a sole proprietorship/partnership with those (e.g., **limited liability**) of a corporation.

#### Key Concept

| Hybrid | Combination | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Limited Liability Partnership (LLP)** | **Partnership + Corporation** | **Separate legal entity**, **perpetual existence**, **limited liability for limited partners** |
| **One Person Company (OPC)** | **Sole Proprietorship + Corporation** | **Limited liability to its sole member**; **taxed like a corporation** (disadvantage); regulated under **Companies Act, 2013** |

#### Summary of Forms of Business Organisation

| Form | Liability | No. of Owners | Owners Called | Regulated Under |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sole Proprietorship | **Unlimited** | 1 | Proprietor | No specific law |
| Partnership | **Unlimited** | 2 – 50 | Partners | Indian Partnership Act, 1932 |
| Corporation | **Limited** | 2 – 200 (Private); ≥ 7 (Public) | Shareholders | Companies Act, 2013 |
| Limited Liability Partnership | **Limited** | ≥ 2 | General Partners and Limited Partners | Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008 |
| One Person Company | **Limited** | 1 | Shareholder | Companies Act, 2013 |

## 1.3 Corporate Investment and Financing Decisions

### 1.3.1 Investments, Real Assets and Financial Assets

#### Definition
- **Investment:** spending money **today** in the hope of making more money (or getting some benefit) in the **future**.
- Investment can be in **real assets** or **financial assets**.
- **Real assets:** assets **used to produce/sell goods and services**; can be **tangible or intangible**.
- **Financial assets:** **financial claims on the cash flows generated by real assets** (e.g., **shares, bonds**).

#### Key Concept

| Asset Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Tangible real asset** | Property, plant, equipment; **unsold goods on store shelves** |
| **Intangible real asset** | **Patent**; **company's reputation for customer service**; **negotiation skills of the sales force** |
| **Financial asset** | Shares; bonds; **a 5% stake in the main supplier** (holding 5% of its shares) |

- Chain: **real assets → goods and services → cash flows → financial assets are claims on these cash flows**.

### 1.3.2 Investment and Financing Decisions

#### Key Concept
- Firms invest in real assets to **produce goods and services** so as to **generate income**.
- Investment in real assets **requires money**.

| Source of Funds | Description |
|---|---|
| **Existing cash reserves** | Internal funds from existing operations |
| **Borrowing** | From banks / financial institutions |
| **Issuing bonds** | Borrowing from the public at large |
| **Issuing additional shares** | Raising equity |

#### Definition
- The financial manager faces **two broad financial questions**:
- **Investment decision:** **What investments should the firm make?** → **spending money for the purchase of real assets**.
- **Financing decision:** **How should it pay for those investments?** → **raising money from the sale of financial assets**.
- Corporate finance is primarily about **investment and financing decisions**.

| Investment Decisions (capital expenditure / capital budgeting decisions) | Financing Decisions |
|---|---|
| **Purchase** of real assets | **Sale of financial assets** to raise cash from banks, bondholders, shareholders |
| **Managing** assets already in place | **Meeting obligations** to banks, bondholders, shareholders (e.g., repaying a loan with interest over its term) |
| **Disposing** of assets once their utility is over | |

#### Example
- **Investment decision:** **Meta (Facebook) acquired WhatsApp for $22 billion in 2014** (an **inorganic** investment).
- **Financing decision:** Meta paid with a **mix of cash and its own shares**.

### 1.3.3 Cash Flows Between Corporations and Financial Markets

#### Definition
- **Firm:** a **bundle of real assets**.
- **Financial market:** investors holding **financial assets** such as shares and bonds.
- **Financial manager** stands **in between** the firm and the financial markets.

#### Key Concept

| Step | Flow of Cash |
|---|---|
| 1 | Cash is **raised** by the financial manager **from the financial market** (sale of financial assets) |
| 2 | Cash is **invested in the firm** to purchase **real assets** |
| 3 | Real assets produce goods and services; sales to customers **generate cash** |
| 4a | Part of the cash is **retained and reinvested** in the firm |
| 4b | Part is **paid out as dividends** to shareholders or **returned in another form** to investors |

## 1.4 Financial Goal of the Corporation

### 1.4.1 Shareholder Wealth Maximisation

#### Key Concept
- Firms have **hundreds of thousands** (even millions) of shareholders; e.g., **Infosys had 2,748,596 shareholders holding 4,055,591,723 shares (as of March 31, 2026)**.
- All shareholders **cannot be actively involved** in running the firm (it would lead to chaos).
- Shareholders **delegate their authority to professional managers**.
- Shareholders differ in **age, tastes, time horizon, risk tolerance, and investment strategy**.
- **Delegation works as long as shareholders have a common objective.**

#### Definition
- **Common objective of shareholders:** **maximise their wealth**, i.e., **maximise the market value of shares (current stock price)**.
- Rationale: people prefer **more wealth over less wealth** at any point in time.
- **How managers maximise shareholder wealth:** by **accepting value-adding investment projects**.

### 1.4.2 Shareholder Wealth Maximisation vs Profit Maximisation

#### Definition
- **Stock variable:** measured **at a particular point in time** (e.g., **wealth**).
- **Flow variable:** measured **for a period of time** (e.g., **income or profit**).

#### Key Concept

| Basis | Stock Variable | Flow Variable |
|---|---|---|
| Measured | **At a point in time** | **Over a period of time** |
| Example | **Wealth** | **Income / Profit** |
| Financial statement | **Balance sheet** ("as of 31st March 2026") – assets, liabilities, equity | **Income statement / P&L** ("for the year") |

- **Shareholder wealth maximisation is NOT strictly the same as profit maximisation**: maximising a stock variable is not the same as maximising a flow variable.
- **Maximising current year's profit is not a good objective:**
  - Managers can raise current profit by **cutting R&D expenses** and **employee training expenses**.
  - This **reduces long-term profit** and long-term value.
- **Shareholder wealth maximisation** provides a **broader basis** for corporate financial decisions.

## 1.5 Evaluating Investment Decisions

### 1.5.1 Investment Decision Rule and Opportunity Cost

#### Key Concept
- **Given:** the corporation has an **investment project** and **sufficient cash** to finance it.
- **Objective:** **maximise shareholder wealth** (current market value of stock).
- **Decision dilemma:** **invest cash in the project** or **pay it out as dividend** so shareholders invest on their own in the financial market.

| Alternative | Return | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Invest in the **project** | $R_1$ | $\beta$ |
| Pay **dividend**; shareholders invest in financial assets | $R_2$ | $\beta$ (same) |

#### Formula (Decision Rule)

$$\text{Invest in the project if } R_1 > R_2$$

- $R_1$ = expected return on the project; $R_2$ = return available to shareholders in the financial market at the **same risk** $\beta$.

#### Definition
- **Opportunity cost:** the **benefit that you forego when you choose one alternative over another at the same level of risk**.
- **Same level of risk** because investors care about **both return and risk**.
- **Opportunity cost of the project = $R_2$**: shareholders **forego the opportunity to earn $R_2$** on their own if cash is invested in the project.
- A project is accepted when its **expected return exceeds** the return on an alternative investment of the **same risk**.

### 1.5.2 Opportunity Cost of Capital and Project Risk

#### Definition
- **Opportunity cost of capital for a project:** the **expected return in the financial market at the same level of risk as the project**.

#### Key Concept
- In the financial market, **more risky assets are expected to give higher returns**.
- **Stocks** are expected to give a **higher return than bonds** because stocks are **more risky**.
- **The greater the risk of an investment, the greater the return required by investors.**
- Opportunity cost **depends on the risk of the project**: **higher project risk → higher opportunity cost**.

| Project Type | Opportunity Cost |
|---|---|
| **Completely safe project** | **Interest rate on government bonds** (risk-free rate) |
| **Risky project** | Determined using **CAPM** = risk-free rate + risk premium |

- **Government bonds** are the **safest asset**: the government is the **last entity to default**; it can **print more money** to service its obligations.
- To find opportunity cost, look to **assets in the financial market with the same risk** as the project.

#### Formula (CAPM Intuition)

$$\text{Opportunity cost} = \text{Risk-free rate} + \text{Risk premium}$$

- **Risk-free rate** = interest rate on government bonds.
- **Risk premium** increases with the **risk of the project**, represented by **beta ($\beta$)**.
- If the project is **not risky**, risk premium = **0** → opportunity cost = **risk-free rate**.

## 1.6 Agency Costs

### 1.6.1 Agency Relationship and Agency Costs

#### Definition
- **Agency relationship:** a relationship in which someone (**principal**) **hires** another person (**agent**) to **represent his/her interests**.
- Shareholder–manager relationship: **shareholders = principals**, **managers = agents**.
- **Agency problem / Principal–agent problem:** the **possibility of a conflict of interest** between principal and agent.
- **Agency costs:** the **costs of the conflicts of interest between shareholders and managers**.

#### Key Concept
- Arises because **everything in the interest of shareholders may not be in the interest of managers**.
- Managers may worry more about the **safety of their jobs** than about **maximising shareholder value**.
- **Example:** managers may **shy away from attractive (value-adding) but risky projects**, since failure may get them **fired**.

| Form of Agency Cost | Example |
|---|---|
| **Corporate expenditure that benefits managers but costs shareholders** | **Luxurious and unneeded corporate jet**; **Tyco International** CEO spent $2 million on his wife's birthday bash and **charged $1 million to the company** (extreme form, like fraud) |
| **Expense of monitoring managerial actions** | Paying **outside auditors** to assess the accuracy of financial statements |
| **Cost of lost opportunity** | **Not undertaking an attractive (value-adding) but risky project** |

### 1.6.2 Mitigating Agency Problems and Corporate Governance

#### Key Concept

| Mechanism | How It Works |
|---|---|
| **Aligning managers' goals with shareholders' goals** | Give managers **shares** (they become shareholders); make **employee stock options** part of compensation (**higher stock price → more valuable options**) |
| **Monitoring managers** | **Supervision by board of directors**; **auditors** (often **mandatory by law**) |
| **Replacing managers** | Managers replaced if they do not pursue shareholder goals; **hostile takeovers** – market plays a **disciplining role** |
| **Punishing managers** | **Legal sanctions for self-dealing**; e.g., **insider trading** (trading on **unpublished price-sensitive information**) is **prohibited by law** |

#### Definition
- **Hostile takeover:** a takeover of a company **against the wishes of its management**.
- **Poorly managed firms** (managers not maximising value) are **more attractive targets** of hostile takeovers.
- **Corporate governance:** all these mechanisms together form a **good corporate governance system**, ensuring managers act in **shareholders' interest**.

## 2.1 Overview of Time Value of Money

### 2.1.1 Overview

- FV, PV and NPV are computed through **compounding** and **discounting**.
- **Shortcuts**: perpetuities, growing perpetuities, annuities, growing annuities.
- **APR** (how interest is quoted) and **EAR** (how interest is actually paid).

#### Key Concept: Two Foundational Facts

| Fact | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Money has time value** | A rupee today is worth more than a rupee tomorrow |
| **Risk and return go hand in hand** | A safe rupee is worth more than a risky rupee |

## 2.2 FV and PV of a Single Cash Flow

### 2.2.1 Future Value

#### Definition

- **Future Value (FV):** value at some point in the future of cash invested today.

#### Key Concept: Bank Deposit Example (₹100 at 7% p.a., compounded annually)

| Year | Opening principal | Interest | Closing value |
|------|-------------------|----------|---------------|
| 1 | 100 | 7.00 | $100 \times 1.07 = 107$ |
| 2 | 107 | 7.49 | $100 \times 1.07^2 = 114.49$ |
| t | – | – | $100 \times 1.07^t$ |

- Interest in Year 2 is higher because **interest is earned on interest already earned** → **compound interest regime**.
- Under **simple interest**, interest would be **₹7 every year**.
- The course follows the **compound interest regime** throughout.

#### Formula

$$FV = C_0 \times (1 + r)^t$$

- $C_0$ = investment today; $r$ = annual rate (compounded annually); $t$ = years
- **Longer horizon (t) → higher FV**; **higher rate (r) → higher FV**
- Cash flows are **compounded** to get FV.

### 2.2.2 Present Value

#### Definition

- **Present Value (PV):** value **today** of a cash flow to be received in the future.
- Finding PV is **exactly the reverse** of finding FV (discounting reverses compounding).
- Example: $PV = 114.49 / 1.07^2 = ₹100$

#### Formula

$$PV = \frac{C_t}{(1 + r)^t} = C_t \times DF_t, \quad DF_t = \frac{1}{(1+r)^t}$$

- $C_t$ = cash flow at end of year $t$
- $r$ = **discount rate** (it discounts future cash flows); also called **opportunity cost of capital**
- $DF_t$ = **t-year discount factor** = PV of ₹1 received after $t$ years

#### Key Concept: Why r Is the Opportunity Cost

- Using money today in a project means losing the chance to invest it elsewhere at the **prevailing market rate on a similar-risk investment**.

#### Key Concept: Inferences from the PV Formula

| Driver | Effect on PV | Reason |
|--------|--------------|--------|
| Higher **t** (longer wait) | **Lower PV** | Money has time value |
| Higher **r** | **Lower PV** | – |
| More **risky** cash flow | **Lower PV** | Investors care about risk, not just return |

- **Discount rate ≠ discount factor**: they are **inversely related** (higher rate → lower factor).
- **Discount** cash flows → PV; **compound** cash flows → FV.

### 2.2.3 Comparing Cash Flows Using PV and FV

#### Key Concept: Decision Depends on Opportunity Cost

- ₹100 today vs ₹100 in 2 years → **₹100 today** (time value).
- ₹100 today vs ₹110 in 2 years → depends on what ₹100 earns in the financial market at the **same risk** for 2 years:
  - Market gives **less than ₹110** → prefer ₹110 later
  - Market gives **more than ₹110** → prefer ₹100 today

#### Key Concept: Two Equivalent Approaches (r = 10%)

| Approach | ₹100 today | ₹110 after 2 years | Choice |
|----------|-----------|--------------------|--------|
| **PV (at t = 0)** | 100 | $110/1.1^2 \approx 91$ | ₹100 today |
| **FV (at t = 2)** | $100 \times 1.1^2 = 121$ | 110 | ₹100 today |

- Objective: an **apples-to-apples comparison** at a **common point in time**.
- Choose higher PV today or higher FV later; **both approaches give the same decision**.

### 2.2.4 Negative Interest Rates and Time Value of Money

#### Key Concept: Positive vs Negative Interest Rates

- As long as $r > 0$, PV of $C_t$ is **less than** $C_t$.
- $r$ can be **negative** in real life (e.g., **Japan and some European countries**); India has had positive rates.

| Environment | Meaning |
|-------------|---------|
| **Positive interest rate** | Commercial bank pays depositors interest; central bank pays commercial banks interest on their deposits |
| **Negative interest rate** | Bank **charges** depositors interest instead of paying it |

#### Key Concept: Why Negative Rates Are Used

- A **monetary policy tool** used by central banks during **downturns** (falling economic activity) with **deflation** (falling prices; opposite of inflation).
- In deflation consumers **postpone purchases**, intensifying the downturn.
- Negative rates:
  - **Discourage depositing** money (lockers also carry charges) → **incentivise consumer spending**
  - Make **borrowing cheaper** for corporations
  - Aim to **prop up the economy**
- Negative rates **violate** the time value of money principle, but are **temporary**; the course assumes **positive interest rates**.

## 2.3 PV, NPV and Rate of Return for Projects

### 2.3.1 Present Value of Multiple Cash Flows

#### Formula: Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)

$$PV = \frac{C_1}{(1+r)^1} + \frac{C_2}{(1+r)^2} + \dots + \frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t} = C_1 DF_1 + C_2 DF_2 + \dots + C_t DF_t$$

- Discount **each cash flow individually**, then **sum**.
- **Initial investment ($C_0$) is NOT included** in PV.
- DCF can value **any asset** (bond, share, project, company) as long as its cash flows can be estimated.

### 2.3.2 Net Present Value and the NPV Decision Rule

#### Definition

- **NPV** = PV **net of initial investment** = PV − initial investment.

$$NPV = C_0 + \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t} \quad (C_0 < 0 \text{ for an outflow})$$

#### Key Concept: NPV Rules

| Rule | Detail |
|------|--------|
| **Decision rule** | **Accept if NPV > 0**; reject if NPV < 0 |
| **Rationale** | Goal is to maximise shareholder wealth; positive-NPV projects are **value-adding** |
| **Discount rate effect** | Higher $r$ → **lower NPV** |
| **Sign convention** | Cash **inflow = +**, cash **outflow = −** |
| **Initial investment** | **Included** in NPV (excluded from PV) |

### 2.3.3 Rate of Return as a Project Evaluation Criterion

#### Definition

- **Rate of return** = **profit as a proportion of initial investment**.
- Example: invest ₹100, receive ₹150 after a year → $50/100 = 50\%$.

#### Formula

$$\text{Rate of Return} = \frac{\text{Profit}}{\text{Investment}} = \frac{\text{Payoff} - \text{Investment}}{\text{Investment}}$$

- Applies to a **single payoff after one period** (single-year project); **NOT** for multi-year projects.
- **Decision rule:** accept if **rate of return > opportunity cost of capital** (a merely positive return is not enough).

### 2.3.4 Applying NPV and Rate of Return to a Project

#### Key Concept: Office Block Example 1

- Cost **$700,000** today; sure sale next year for **$800,000**; Treasury (safe) = **7%**; stock market (risky) = **12%** average.
- **Opportunity cost** = return in the financial market at the **same risk** → depends on the risk of the project's cash flows.
- Cash flow is **certain** → risk-free → government securities rate → **r = 7%**.
- Timeline: $t = 0$: −700,000; $t = 1$: +800,000.

| Criterion | Calculation | Result | Decision |
|-----------|-------------|--------|----------|
| **NPV** | $-700{,}000 + 800{,}000/1.07$ | **$47,664** | NPV > 0 → **Accept** |
| **Rate of return** | $(800{,}000 - 700{,}000)/700{,}000$ | **14.3%** | 14.3% > 7% → **Accept** |

- For a **single-year project**, both criteria give the **same decision**.

### 2.3.5 Market Value, Project Risk and NPV

#### Key Concept: Market Value = Present Value

- Assets are bought/sold at **market value (MV)**.
- If markets **price assets fairly** (assumed throughout the course) → **MV = PV**.
- Selling immediately after construction → $800{,}000/1.07 = \$747{,}664$; wealth rises by NPV ($47,664) because the project is **value-adding**.

#### Key Concept: Effect of Uncertainty

- **Unrealistic assumption:** the sale price of $800,000 is certain; it is only the **best forecast**.
- If uncertain → **opportunity cost rises** → **PV falls** below $747,664 → investors would not pay $747,664.
  - $747,664 in government securities gives a **certain** $800,000; the building cannot.
- Seller must **cut the asking price**.
- If the project is as risky as the stock market → **r = 12%**:

$$NPV = -700{,}000 + \frac{800{,}000}{1.12} = \$14{,}286$$

- **Higher risk → higher discount rate → lower PV → lower NPV**; NPV still positive → accept.

### 2.3.6 Comparing Project Alternatives Using NPV

#### Key Concept: Office Block Example 2 (r = 12%, stock-market risk)

- NPV requires **(1) expected cash flows** and **(2) opportunity cost of capital**.

| Option | Cash flows | NPV |
|--------|-----------|-----|
| **Option 1** (sell after 1 year) | $t_0$: −700,000; $t_1$: +800,000 | $-700{,}000 + 800{,}000/1.12 =$ **$14,286** |
| **Option 2** (rent 2 years at $30,000, then sell for $840,000) | $t_0$: −700,000; $t_1$: +30,000; $t_2$: +870,000 | $-700{,}000 + 30{,}000/1.12 + 870{,}000/1.12^2 =$ **$20,344** |

- Prefer the **higher NPV** → **Option 2**.
- **Unrealistic assumption:** building is ready **instantly** (needed to earn rent from Year 1).

#### Key Concept: Maximum Upfront Investment

- Maximum a rational investor would pay = investment that makes **NPV = 0** = **PV of the option's cash flows**.

$$PV_{\text{Option 2}} = \frac{30{,}000}{1.12} + \frac{870{,}000}{1.12^2} = \$720{,}344$$

## 2.4 Present Value Shortcuts

### 2.4.1 Perpetuities and Shifting Cash Flows

#### Definition

- **Perpetuity:** the **same cash flow every year forever**.

#### Formula

$$PV = \frac{C}{1+r} + \frac{C}{(1+r)^2} + \frac{C}{(1+r)^3} + \dots = \frac{C}{r}$$

- Derived from an **infinite geometric series** $a, ax, ax^2, \dots$ with sum $\frac{a}{1-x}$, valid for $-1 < x < 1$.
- First term $a = \frac{C}{1+r}$; common ratio $x = \frac{1}{1+r}$; with $r > 0$, $0 < x < 1$ → **finite sum** despite infinite terms.
- A series has a finite sum only if it is **decreasing** (each term a fraction of the previous); an increasing series **explodes**.

#### Key Concept: Value One Period Before the First Cash Flow

- $\frac{C}{r}$ is the value **one period before the first cash flow**.
- First cash flow at $t = 1$ → $C/r$ is the value at $t = 0$ (PV).
- First cash flow at $t = 3$ → $C/r$ is the value at $t = 2$; $PV = \frac{C/r}{(1+r)^2}$.

#### Key Concept: Shifting Cash Flows

| Shift | Operation |
|-------|-----------|
| 1 period **left** (earlier) | Divide by $(1+r)$ |
| t periods **left** | Divide by $(1+r)^t$ |
| 1 period **right** (later) | Multiply by $(1+r)$ |
| t periods **right** | Multiply by $(1+r)^t$ |

### 2.4.2 Applying Perpetuities

| Case | Working | PV |
|------|---------|----|
| ₹1 billion/year from $t = 1$, $r = 10\%$ | $1/0.10$ | **₹10 billion** |
| No cash for 3 years; first ₹1 billion at $t = 4$ | Value at $t = 3$ = 10 billion; $10/1.1^3$ | **₹7.51 billion** |

- Delayed perpetuity has **lower PV** because no cash flows are received in the first 3 years.

### 2.4.3 Annuities and Their Present Value

#### Definition

- **Annuity:** the **same cash flow every year for a fixed number of years** (t-year annuity).

#### Formula

$$PV = \frac{C}{r}\left[1 - \frac{1}{(1+r)^t}\right] = C \times \underbrace{\frac{1}{r}\left[1 - \frac{1}{(1+r)^t}\right]}_{\text{t-year annuity factor}}$$

- Derived from a **finite geometric series** sum $\frac{a(1-x^n)}{1-x}$, $x \ne 1$, with $a = \frac{C}{1+r}$, $x = \frac{1}{1+r}$, $n = t$.

#### Key Concept: Tiburon Autos Example

- $5,000 at end of each year for 5 years, no down payment, $r = 7\%$:

$$PV = \frac{5{,}000}{0.07}\left[1 - \frac{1}{1.07^5}\right] = 5{,}000 \times 4.100 = \$20{,}501$$

- The car **really costs $20,501**.

### 2.4.4 Annuities with Beginning-of-Year Payments

#### Key Concept: Lottery Example (₹12.167 million × 30 years, r = 6%)

- Advertised ₹365 million = $12.167 \times 30$ → **ignores time value** (adding apples and oranges).

| Payment timing | Working | PV |
|----------------|---------|----|
| **End of each year** | $\frac{12.167}{0.06}\left[1 - \frac{1}{1.06^{30}}\right]$ | **₹167.5 million** (less than half the advertised value) |
| **Beginning of each year** | First payment at $t = 0$ → formula gives value at $t = -1$; shift right: $167.5 \times 1.06$ | **₹177.5 million** |

- The annuity formula gives value **one period before the first cash flow**.
- **Earlier payments → higher PV**.

$$PV_{\text{beginning}} = PV_{\text{end}} \times (1 + r)$$

### 2.4.5 Loan Amortisation with Equal Annual Payments

#### Definition

- An **amortised loan** requires the borrower to repay parts of the loan over time.
- **Amortising a loan** = paying it off through regular **principal reductions**; each repayment reduces outstanding principal.
- **EMI** = **Equated Monthly Instalment**; part goes to interest, part to principal.

| Variant | Structure |
|---------|-----------|
| **Variant 1** (most common) | **Equal total payment** every period |
| **Variant 2** | Interest each period + **fixed principal** amount |

#### Formula: Annual Payment (Variant 1)

- Loan amount = PV of an annuity of payments $C$.

$$1{,}000 = \frac{C}{0.10}\left[1 - \frac{1}{1.10^4}\right] \Rightarrow C = \$315.47$$

#### Formula: Amortisation Table Rules

- $\text{Interest}_t = \text{Rate} \times \text{Beginning Balance}_t$
- $\text{Principal}_t = \text{Total Payment}_t - \text{Interest}_t$
- $\text{Ending Balance}_t = \text{Beginning Balance}_t - \text{Principal}_t$
- $\text{Beginning Balance}_{t+1} = \text{Ending Balance}_t$

#### Key Concept: Variant 1 Table ($1,000, 4 years, 10%)

| Year | Beginning balance | Total payment | Interest | Principal | Ending balance |
|------|-------------------|---------------|----------|-----------|----------------|
| 1 | 1,000.00 | 315.47 | 100.00 | 215.47 | 784.53 |
| 2 | 784.53 | 315.47 | 78.45 | 237.02 | 547.51 |
| 3 | 547.51 | 315.47 | 54.75 | 260.72 | 286.79 |
| 4 | 286.79 | 315.47 | 28.68 | 286.79 | 0.00 |
| **Total** | | **1,261.88** | **261.88** | **1,000.00** | |

- Ending balance in final year must be **zero** (near zero only due to rounding; otherwise an error).
- **Interest declines** (outstanding balance falls), **principal rises**, **total payment constant**.

### 2.4.6 Loan Amortisation with Equal Principal Repayments

#### Key Concept: Variant 2 Table ($1,000, 4 years, 10%, $250 principal per year)

| Year | Beginning balance | Total payment | Interest | Principal | Ending balance |
|------|-------------------|---------------|----------|-----------|----------------|
| 1 | 1,000 | 350 | 100 | 250 | 750 |
| 2 | 750 | 325 | 75 | 250 | 500 |
| 3 | 500 | 300 | 50 | 250 | 250 |
| 4 | 250 | 275 | 25 | 250 | 0 |
| **Total** | | **1,250** | **250** | **1,000** | |

- Total payment = interest + fixed principal.

#### Key Concept: Variant 1 vs Variant 2

| Item | Variant 1 (equal payments) | Variant 2 (equal principal) |
|------|---------------------------|-----------------------------|
| **Interest** | Declines | Declines |
| **Principal** | Increases | **Constant** |
| **Total payment** | **Constant** | Declines |
| **PV of payments** | $1,000 | $1,000 (same in PV terms) |

### 2.4.7 Growing Perpetuities

#### Definition

- **Growing perpetuity:** cash flows **grow at a constant rate g forever**; $C$, $C(1+g)$, $C(1+g)^2$, …

#### Formula

$$PV = \frac{C}{1+r} + \frac{C(1+g)}{(1+r)^2} + \frac{C(1+g)^2}{(1+r)^3} + \dots = \frac{C}{r - g}, \quad g < r$$

- Common ratio $\frac{1+g}{1+r}$; lies between 0 and 1 only if **g < r**.
- If **g ≥ r**, each term ≥ previous term → series explodes → **infinite PV** (impossible for a real asset).
- $C$ = cash flow at **end of first year**.
- $\frac{C}{r-g}$ = value **one period before the first cash flow**; first cash flow at $t = 3$ → value at $t = 2$ → divide by $(1+r)^2$.

### 2.4.8 Growing Annuities and Their Present Value

#### Definition

- **Growing annuity:** cash flows **grow at constant rate g for a fixed number of years t**; last cash flow $C(1+g)^{t-1}$.

#### Formula

$$PV = \frac{C}{r - g}\left[1 - \left(\frac{1+g}{1+r}\right)^t\right], \quad g \ne r$$

- From finite geometric series with $a = \frac{C}{1+r}$, $x = \frac{1+g}{1+r}$; valid only if $x \ne 1$ → **g ≠ r**.

### 2.4.9 Growing Annuity When Growth Equals the Discount Rate

#### Key Concept: Why a Separate Formula

- If $g = r$, the standard formula fails, but PV is **NOT infinite**: a finite number (t) of finite terms always has a finite sum.
- Each term $\frac{C(1+r)^{k-1}}{(1+r)^k}$ simplifies to $\frac{C}{1+r}$, appearing **t times**.

#### Formula

$$PV = \frac{C \times t}{1 + r}, \quad g = r$$

### 2.4.10 Growing Annuity Application

#### Key Concept: Club Membership (3 years, r = 10%)

| Option | Cash flows | PV |
|--------|-----------|----|
| **Annual renewal** | ₹5,000 at end Y1, growing 6% p.a. | $\frac{5{,}000}{0.10-0.06}\left[1 - \left(\frac{1.06}{1.10}\right)^3\right] =$ **₹13,146.51** |
| **3-year membership in one go** | ₹12,750 today | **₹12,750** |

- Alternative: discount individually: $\frac{5{,}000}{1.1} + \frac{5{,}000(1.06)}{1.1^2} + \frac{5{,}000(1.06)^2}{1.1^3}$
- For payments, choose the **lower PV** (cheaper) → **one-time payment of ₹12,750**.

### 2.4.11 Connecting the Four Present Value Shortcuts

| Pattern | Substitution into growing annuity formula | Result |
|---------|-------------------------------------------|--------|
| **Ordinary annuity** | $g = 0$ | $\frac{C}{r}\left[1 - \frac{1}{(1+r)^t}\right]$ |
| **Growing perpetuity** | $t = \infty$, $g < r$ → $\left(\frac{1+g}{1+r}\right)^\infty \to 0$ | $\frac{C}{r-g}$ |
| **Ordinary perpetuity** | $g = 0$, $t = \infty$ | $\frac{C}{r}$ |

- Knowing the **growing annuity formula** gives all other shortcuts.

### 2.4.12 Applying the Four Present Value Shortcuts

#### Key Concept: $1 Billion Schemes at r = 8%

| Part | Pattern | Formula | PV |
|------|---------|---------|----|
| a. $1 bn/year forever | Perpetuity | $1/0.08$ | **$12.5 bn** |
| b. $1 bn growing 4% forever | Growing perpetuity | $1/(0.08 - 0.04)$ | **$25 bn** |
| c. $1 bn/year for 20 years | Annuity | $\frac{1}{0.08}\left[1 - \frac{1}{1.08^{20}}\right]$ | **$9.818 bn** |
| d. $1 bn growing 4% for 20 payments | Growing annuity ($g \ne r$) | $\frac{1}{0.04}\left[1 - \left(\frac{1.04}{1.08}\right)^{20}\right]$ | **$13.247 bn** |
| e. $1 bn growing 8% for 20 payments | Growing annuity ($g = r$) | $\frac{1 \times 20}{1.08}$ | **$18.519 bn** |

- Part e > Part d because payments **grow faster**.

## 2.5 APR, EAR and Periodic Compounding

### 2.5.1 Annual and Periodic Compounding

#### Key Concept: Need

- Cash flows may occur **more than once a year**, or interest may be **compounded more than annually**.
- Government bonds pay interest **annually** in France and Germany; **semi-annually** in India, US, Britain, Canada and Japan.

#### Key Concept: Bank A vs Bank B (₹100, 10% p.a.)

| Bank | Compounding | Value after 1 year |
|------|-------------|--------------------|
| **A** | Annual | ₹110 |
| **B** | Semi-annual (5% per half year) | $100 \to 105 \to$ **₹110.25** |

- 10% per year compounded semi-annually ≡ **10.25% per year compounded annually**.

#### Definition

| Term | Definition | Role |
|------|-----------|------|
| **Periodic interest rate** | Rate per period (e.g., 5% per half year) | – |
| **APR (Annual Percentage Rate)** | Periodic rate **annualised using simple interest**; yearly rate with **any compounding interval** (must be specified) | Rate at which interest is **quoted** |
| **EAR (Effective Annual Rate)** | Periodic rate **annualised using compound interest**; yearly rate **compounded annually** | Rate at which interest is **paid** |

### 2.5.2 Relationship Between APR and EAR

#### Formula

$$1 + EAR = \left(1 + \frac{APR}{m}\right)^m$$

- $m$ = number of **compounding intervals in a year**

| Compounding | m | EAR for 10% APR |
|-------------|---|-----------------|
| Annual | 1 | 10.00% |
| Semi-annual | 2 | 10.25% |
| Quarterly | 4 | 10.38% |
| Monthly | 12 | 10.47% |
| Weekly | 52 | – |
| Daily | 365 | 10.52% |

- **EAR increases with compounding frequency**.
- $m = 1$ → **EAR = APR**; $m > 1$ → **EAR > APR**.

#### Formula: FV with Compounding Frequency

$$FV = C_0 (1 + EAR)^t = C_0\left(1 + \frac{APR}{m}\right)^{mt}$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| $APR/m$ | **Periodic interest rate** (rate per period) |
| $mt$ | **Total number of periods** |
| $t$ | Number of years |

- Unit of time changes from a **year to a period** (day, week, month, quarter, half year or year).

$$PV = \frac{C_t}{\left(1 + \frac{APR}{m}\right)^{mt}}$$

### 2.5.3 APR and EAR in Banking Practice

- Banks **quote APR** (deposits and loans); interest is **actually paid at EAR**.
- Indian banks almost all use **quarterly compounding**.

#### Key Concept: Yes Bank FD (₹1,00,000, 6.65% p.a. compounded quarterly, 365 days)

$$1 + EAR = \left(1 + \frac{0.0665}{4}\right)^4 \Rightarrow EAR \approx 6.82\%$$

- FV = **₹1,06,820**, **NOT** ₹1,06,650.
- On **loans**, borrower pays at EAR, which **exceeds** the quoted APR.

### 2.5.4 Compounding Frequency and Future Value

#### Key Concept: $10 Million at 6% for 4 Years

| Compounding | Working | FV |
|-------------|---------|----|
| **Annual** (APR = EAR) | $10 \times 1.06^4$ | **$12.625 million** |
| **Monthly** | $10 \times (1 + 0.06/12)^{48}$ | **$12.705 million** |

- **More frequent compounding → higher FV** at the same quoted APR.

### 2.5.5 Discounting Periodic Cash Flows

#### Key Concept: Matching Rule

| Cash flow frequency | Discount using |
|---------------------|----------------|
| **Annual** | Annually compounded rate (EAR, or APR with annual compounding) |
| **Periodic** (half-yearly, monthly…) | **Periodic rate** = $APR/m$ |

- Same shortcut formulas apply with **r = rate per period** and **t = number of periods**.

#### Key Concept: Half-Yearly Annuity Example

- $5 every 6 months for 3 years; 10% p.a. compounded semi-annually.
- Periods = $2 \times 3 = 6$ half-years; rate = $10\%/2 = 5\%$ per half-year.

$$PV = \frac{5}{0.05}\left[1 - \frac{1}{1.05^6}\right] = \$25.38$$

## Quick Revision Summary

### Key Definitions

- **FV:** value in the future of cash invested today
- **PV:** value today of future cash; reverse of FV
- **Discount rate / opportunity cost of capital:** return on a **similar-risk** investment forgone
- **Discount factor:** PV of ₹1 received after t years
- **NPV:** PV minus initial investment
- **Rate of return:** profit ÷ initial investment (single-period only)
- **Perpetuity / Annuity:** equal cash flows forever / for fixed years
- **Growing perpetuity / Growing annuity:** constant-growth cash flows forever / for fixed years
- **Loan amortisation:** reducing outstanding principal through regular repayments
- **APR:** quoted rate, annualised by simple interest; **EAR:** paid rate, annualised by compound interest

### Important Formulas

| Item | Formula | Condition |
|------|---------|-----------|
| FV | $C_0(1+r)^t$ | – |
| PV | $C_t/(1+r)^t$ | – |
| DCF | $\sum C_t/(1+r)^t$ | Excludes $C_0$ |
| Rate of return | $(\text{Payoff} - \text{Investment})/\text{Investment}$ | One period |
| Perpetuity | $C/r$ | None |
| Annuity | $\frac{C}{r}\left[1 - \frac{1}{(1+r)^t}\right]$ | None |
| Growing perpetuity | $C/(r-g)$ | $g < r$ |
| Growing annuity | $\frac{C}{r-g}\left[1 - \left(\frac{1+g}{1+r}\right)^t\right]$ | $g \ne r$ |
| Growing annuity | $Ct/(1+r)$ | $g = r$ |
| Annuity due | $PV_{\text{ordinary}} \times (1+r)$ | Payments at start |
| EAR | $(1 + APR/m)^m - 1$ | – |
| FV with m | $C_0(1 + APR/m)^{mt}$ | – |

### Must-Remember Points

- Higher t, higher r, or higher risk → **lower PV**
- PV and FV approaches give the **same decision**
- **Accept NPV > 0**; accept if **rate of return > opportunity cost**
- Fair pricing → **market value = PV**
- Shortcut formulas give value **one period before the first cash flow**; shift left ÷ $(1+r)^t$, right × $(1+r)^t$
- Shortcut formulas assume first cash flow at **t = 1** and r = **EAR**
- Equal payment loan: interest ↓, principal ↑, total constant; equal principal loan: interest ↓, total ↓
- **EAR = APR only if m = 1**; otherwise EAR > APR
- Periodic cash flows → **periodic rate** and **number of periods**
- Negative rates: temporary monetary policy tool; course assumes **positive rates**

## 3.2 Bonds and Their Key Features

### 3.2.1 What is a Bond

#### Definition
- **Bond:** a **financial asset** issued by **companies and governments** to **raise money** from investors
- Investors receive **promised payments in the future** till the **date of maturity**

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Who issues? | Corporations and governments |
| Why? | To raise money |
| To whom? | Investors |
| What do investors get? | Promised future payments |
| Till when? | Date of maturity |

### 3.2.2 Bond Payments and Life Cycle

#### Key Concept
- **Coupons:** promised periodic interest payments
- **Face value / par value:** notional value on bond certificate, repaid **at maturity**; never changes

| Stage | Flow |
|---|---|
| Purchase | Investor pays money; issuer gives bond |
| Holding period | Issuer pays periodic coupons |
| Maturity | Last coupon **+ face value** |

### 3.2.3 Coupon Rate

#### Key Concept
- Coupon determined using the **coupon rate**
- Coupon rate is expressed **(i) as % of face value** and **(ii) as an APR**
- Coupon rate per period = APR / m (m = coupon payments per year)

#### Formula
$$C = \frac{\text{Coupon Rate (APR)}}{m} \times F$$

| Example | Coupon |
|---|---|
| $100 FV, 10%, annual | $100 × 10% = **$10 per year** |
| $100 FV, 10%, semiannual | $100 × 10%/2 = **$5 every six months** |

### 3.2.4 Bond Certificate

#### Key Concept
- Specifies **face value, coupon rate, frequency of payment, date of maturity**
- These **do not change irrespective of market conditions**
- Exception: coupon rate of a **floating-rate bond**

## 3.3 Valuing Bonds

### 3.3.1 Fundamentals of Bond Valuation

#### Key Concept
- **Basic principle:** PV(Asset) = PV(Expected future cash flows from the asset)
- PV(Bond) = **PV(Coupon payments)** + **PV(Face value)**
- Coupons are equal and for a fixed number of periods → **annuity**
- Face value → **one-time payment** at maturity
- In a **fairly functioning market**, market price = present value (neither over- nor undervalued)
- **Bond prices are usually expressed as a percentage of face value**

### 3.3.2 Bond Pricing and Yield to Maturity

#### Formula
$$P = \frac{C}{r}\left[1 - \frac{1}{(1+r)^t}\right] + \frac{F}{(1+r)^t}$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $F$ | Face value paid at maturity |
| $C$ | Coupon per period = coupon rate per period × F |
| $t$ | Periods to maturity |
| $r$ | Yield per period (YTM) |

#### Yield to Maturity (YTM)
- Also called the bond's **yield**
- **Opportunity cost of capital for bondholders**
- Depends on **riskiness**: greater risk → **higher YTM**
- **Return investors expect from buying a bond today and holding it until maturity**
- Expressed as an **APR**; yield per period = APR / m

#### Government Bond Conventions
- **US government bonds: face value $1,000**; **Indian government bonds: face value ₹100**
- US and Indian government bonds pay coupons **semiannually**

#### Example: Finding Price
- 3-year US govt bond, coupon 11.25% semiannual, FV $1,000 → C = $56.25 per half-year, t = 6
- (a) Required return 0.17% p.a. (r = 0.085% per half-year):
$$P = \frac{56.25}{0.00085}\left[1-\frac{1}{1.00085^6}\right] + \frac{1000}{1.00085^6} = \$1{,}331.41$$
- (b) Required return 8% p.a. (r = 4%): $P = \$1{,}085.18$ → higher discount rate, **lower price**

### 3.3.3 Bond Price, YTM and Bond Classification

#### Finding YTM (Trial and Error)
- €100 FV, 4.25% French govt bond, annual coupons, 4 years, price 108.57%
$$108.57 = \frac{4.25}{(1+r)} + \frac{4.25}{(1+r)^2} + \frac{4.25}{(1+r)^3} + \frac{4.25 + 100}{(1+r)^4}$$
- Equation of degree 4 → solved by **trial and error** → **r = 2%** (less than 4.25% coupon rate)
- At r = 4.25% (= coupon rate), price = €100 = face value

#### Proof: Coupon Rate = YTM ⇒ P = F
- $C = r \times F \Rightarrow C/r = F$
- $P = F\left[1 - \frac{1}{(1+r)^t}\right] + \frac{F}{(1+r)^t} = F$

#### Classification by Price
| Type | Price vs Face Value | Coupon Rate vs YTM |
|---|---|---|
| **Par bond** | P = F | Coupon rate = YTM |
| **Premium bond** | P > F | Coupon rate > YTM |
| **Discount bond** | P < F | Coupon rate < YTM |

- Relations hold **both ways** (if and only if)
- A **premium bond can become a discount bond** (and vice versa) during its tenor, because YTM changes with market conditions

### 3.3.4 Types of Bonds Based on Payments

| Type | Coupon | Face Value | Key Point |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Coupon bond** | Yes, regular | Yes, at maturity | Traditional bond |
| **Zero-coupon / pure discount bond** | No | Yes, single payment | Always trades at **discount** (r > 0); e.g., **Treasury bills** |
| **Perpetual bond** | Yes, forever | Never (no maturity) | Not redeemable; e.g., **AT1 bonds** of Indian commercial banks |

- Zero-coupon price: $P = \frac{F}{(1+r)^t} < F$ when r > 0
- **AT1 (Additional Tier 1) bonds** are **callable**: bank has an option (not obligation) to call back; if never called, they remain perpetual

### 3.3.5 Government Bonds and Bond Quotes

#### Classification by Maturity
| Treasury Security | Maturity |
|---|---|
| **Treasury Bills** | 1 year or less |
| **Treasury Notes** | 1–10 years |
| **Treasury Bonds** | More than 10 years (10–30 years) |

#### Payment Frequency and Face Value
- **Most euro bonds** pay interest **annually**
- Bonds in **India, US, UK, Canada, Japan** pay **semiannually**
- Face value: **US $1,000**, **India ₹100**

#### Identifying Government Bonds
- Uniquely identified by **coupon rate + year of maturity**
- Governments do not issue two bonds with same coupon and same maturity year

| Country | 7.10% coupon maturing 2030 |
|---|---|
| US | **7.10s of 2030** |
| India | **7.10 GS 2030** (GS = Government Security) |

#### Bid, Ask and Spread
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Bid price** | Price at which dealer is willing to **buy**; investor **sells** at this price |
| **Ask price** | Price at which dealer is willing to **sell**; investor **buys** at this price |

### 3.3.6 Reading and Verifying Government Bond Quotes

#### Example: 5.5s of 2028 (August 2025)
- Coupon per half-year = 5.5%/2 = 2.75% × $1,000 = **$27.50**
- Yield per half-year = 3.694%/2 = **1.847%**
- Time to maturity = 3 years = **6 half-years**
$$P = \frac{27.5}{0.01847}\left[1-\frac{1}{1.01847^6}\right] + \frac{1000}{1.01847^6} \approx \$1{,}050 = 105.0\%$$

### 3.3.7 Bond Prices and Interest Rate Risk

#### Price–Yield Relationship
- 10-year US Treasury yields (1900–2026) ranged from about **2% to 14%**
- In the pricing equation, C and F are constant, t falls predictably; **r is the only unpredictable variable**
- **Bond prices and yields move in opposite directions**
- **Flight to safety:** in a crisis, investors buy government bonds (safest asset) → demand up → **yields fall, prices rise**

#### Definition
- **Interest rate risk:** **sensitivity of bond prices to changes in interest rates**

#### Maturity and Interest Rate Risk
| r | 3-year zero (F = 1,000) | 30-year zero (F = 1,000) |
|---|---|---|
| 0% | 1,000 | 1,000 |
| 10% | $1000/1.1^3$ (large) | $1000/1.1^{30}$ (very small) |

- **30-year zero-coupon bond carries greater interest rate risk**
- Reason: PV of **near-term cash flows** is affected **less** by discount-rate changes than PV of **distant cash flows**
- **Long-maturity bond prices are more sensitive** to interest rate changes than otherwise identical short-maturity bonds

### 3.3.8 Holding Period Return on Bonds

#### Formula
$$\text{Rate of Return} = \frac{\text{Total Income}}{\text{Investment}} = \frac{\text{Coupon Income} + \text{Change in Price}}{\text{Initial Price}}$$

| Measure | Nature | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| **Rate of return / HPR** | **Realised** (actual) return | **Backward-looking** |
| **YTM** | **Expected** return | **Forward-looking** |

#### Example: 4.375s of 2038 (2008 Crisis)
- Issued May 2008 at 96.38% (**$963.80**), YTM 4.60%; coupon Aug 2008 = 4.375%/2 × 1,000 = **$21.875**
- Mid-December price 138.05% (**$1,380.50**), yield fell to 2.5%
$$\text{Return} = \frac{21.875 + (1380.50 - 963.80)}{963.80} = 0.455 = 45.5\%$$
- Approximate 7-month return; bond issued at a **discount** became a **premium** bond due to flight to safety

### 3.3.9 Impact of Yield Changes on Bond Returns

#### Example Set-up
- 6-year govt bond, 5% annual coupon, FV assumed $100 (choice of FV does not affect answer), initial YTM 3%
$$P_0 = \frac{5}{0.03}\left[1-\frac{1}{1.03^6}\right] + \frac{100}{1.03^6} = \$110.834$$
- One year later, **5 years** remain

| Yield after 1 year | $P_1$ | Return | vs Initial YTM |
|---|---|---|---|
| Falls to 2% | $114.140 | (5 + 3.306)/110.834 = **7.49%** | **Higher** |
| Stays at 3% | $109.159 | (5 − 1.675)/110.834 = **3.00%** | **Equal** |
| Rises | Lower price | Lower return | **Lower** |

#### Rules
- YTM **unchanged** → return **=** initial YTM
- YTM **increases** → return **<** initial YTM
- YTM **decreases** → return **>** initial YTM

## 3.4 Nominal and Real Rates of Interest

### 3.4.1 Nominal and Real Cash Flows

#### Definitions
- **Nominal cash flow:** actual amount received (₹100 invested → ₹110 received; nominal CF = ₹110)
- **Inflation:** rise in prices; **Deflation:** fall in prices
- **CPI (Consumer Price Index):** measures **retail inflation** by tracking prices of a basket of consumer goods and services; change in CPI year to year = **inflation rate**
- **Real cash flow:** cash flow in terms of **today's purchasing power**

#### Formula
$$C_{real,t} = \frac{C_{nominal,t}}{(1+i)^t}$$
- Example: 7% inflation → ₹110/1.07 = **₹102.80** real
- Cash flows in financial markets are usually given in **nominal terms**

### 3.4.2 Nominal and Real Interest Rates in Valuation

#### Formula
$$1 + r_{real} = \frac{1 + r_{nominal}}{1 + i}$$
$$r_{real} \approx r_{nominal} - i \quad (\text{when inflation is low})$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $r_{nominal}$ | Nominal interest rate |
| $r_{real}$ | Real interest rate |
| $i$ | Expected inflation rate |

- Derivation: $(1+r_{real})(1+i) = 1 + r_{nominal}$ → $1 + i + r_{real} + r_{real}\,i = 1 + r_{nominal}$; product $r_{real}\,i \approx 0$ when inflation is low
- Example: 10% nominal, 7% inflation → $1.10/1.07 - 1 = $ **2.8% real** (approx. 3%)
- **Interest rates in financial markets are usually given in nominal terms**

#### Consistency Rule
| Cash Flows | Discount Rate | Valid? |
|---|---|---|
| Nominal | Nominal | **Yes** |
| Real | Real | **Yes** |
| Nominal | Real | **No** |
| Real | Nominal | **No** |

- PV/NPV is **the same** as long as inflation is treated consistently

### 3.4.3 Inflation-Indexed Bonds

#### Key Concept
- Ordinary bonds: **fixed nominal** coupon and face value → real return uncertain → subject to **inflation risk** (higher-than-expected inflation lowers real return)
- **Inflation-indexed bonds:** earn a **fixed real rate of return**; **real cash flows fixed**, **nominal coupon and face value rise with CPI**
- Examples: **TIPS** (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities, US); **IIBs** (Inflation-Indexed Bonds, India)

#### Example: 3% 20-year TIPS at $1,000, CPI rises 10%
| Item | Nominal | Real |
|---|---|---|
| Coupon | 30 × 1.10 = **$33** | 33/1.10 = $30 |
| Face value | 1,000 × 1.10 = **$1,100** | 1,100/1.10 = $1,000 |

- Investor buying at issue price earns a **real interest rate of 3%**

### 3.5.1 Dealers, Brokers and Stock Markets

#### Dealer vs Broker
| Basis | Dealer | Broker |
|---|---|---|
| Inventory | **Maintains inventory**; ready to buy/sell anytime (car dealer) | **No inventory**; brings buyers and sellers together (real estate broker) |

#### Primary vs Secondary Market
| Market | Feature |
|---|---|

- Principal stock exchanges: **NYSE, Nasdaq** (US); **NSE, BSE** (India)

### 3.5.2 Market Orders and Bid-Ask Prices

#### Definitions
- **Market order:** executed **immediately** at best available price; specify **ticker and quantity**
- **Bid price:** price at which investors are willing to **buy** a share → relevant for a **seller**
- **Ask price:** price at which investors are willing to **sell** a share → relevant for a **buyer**

| Order | Executed at |
|---|---|
| **Market buy** | **Lowest ask price** |
| **Market sell** | **Highest bid price** |

#### Definition

| Order | Executed at |
|---|---|
| **Limit buy** | Limit price **or lower** (≤ limit) |
| **Limit sell** | Limit price **or higher** (≥ limit) |

- Example: buy 10 Infosys at not more than ₹1,000; sell 5 Infosys at not less than ₹1,100

- **Bid prices: descending order** (highest bid at top — best for sellers)
- **Ask prices: ascending order** (lowest ask at top — best for buyers)

| 263.76 | 1,100 | 264.07 | 200 |
| 263.73 | 100 | 264.12 | 1 |
| 263.67 | 100 | 264.13 | 100 |
| 263.61 | 100 | 264.18 | 200 |

- Market sell 100 shares → highest bid **$263.76**

## 3.6 Valuation of Common Stocks

### 3.6.1 Approaches to Stock Valuation

#### Why Book Value ≠ Market Value
| Limitation | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Historical cost** | Records cost at purchase; does not incorporate inflation; differs from current worth |
| **Intangibles excluded** | E.g., brand name |
| **No going-concern value** | Book value = sum of individual assets; ignores **synergies** |

- **Going-concern value:** created when a collection of assets is organised into a **healthy operating business**

#### Two Methods
| Method | Also Called |
|---|---|
| **Valuation by comparables** | Comparable companies analysis, **relative valuation** |
| **Valuation by fundamentals** | **Discounted cash flow (DCF) valuation**, **Dividend Discount Model (DDM)** |

### 3.6.2 Stock Valuation by Comparables

#### Steps
| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | Identify similar listed firms (same industry) as **comparables** |
| 2 | Choose a suitable **multiple**: P/E, P/B |
| 3 | Find **mean (or median)** of multiple for comparables |
| 4 | Value stock assuming it trades at that multiple |

#### Formula
$$\text{Stock Price} = \text{Mean } (P/E)_{\text{comparables}} \times EPS_{\text{firm}}$$
- **P/E ratio** = price per share / EPS → how much investors pay for **₹1 of earnings**
- Firms trade at different P/E because of **expected differences in future earnings growth** (higher growth → higher P/E)

### 3.6.3 Applying Comparable Companies Analysis

#### Example: Infosys (April 20, 2026)
| Comparable | P/E | P/B |
|---|---|---|
| TCS | 18.97 | 7.97 |
| HCL Tech | 23.55 | 5.15 |
| Wipro | 16.10 | 2.40 |
| **Average** | **19.54** | 5.17 |

- Comparables chosen on **industry (IT services) and size** (large IT firms)
- Estimate = 68.97 × 19.54 = **₹1,347.67** vs actual **₹1,312.60** → close
- Useful for estimating price of an **unlisted** firm

### 3.6.4 Challenges of Valuation by Comparables

| Challenge | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Finding comparables** | No two firms alike; needs deeper study of industry and close competitors |
| **Unsuitable multiples** | **P/E almost useless for start-ups** (negative/no earnings → absurd negative price) |
| **Wide variation within industry** | HUL comparables P/E range **42 to 62** (Dabur 42.43, Godrej 62.07; avg 49.75) → low confidence |

### 3.6.5 Stock Valuation by Fundamentals (DCF Approach)

#### Key Concept
- Value of any asset = **PV of expected future cash flows**
- Fair pricing: price = PV; **overpricing:** price > PV; **underpricing:** price < PV
- Stock cash flows: **cash dividends** (during holding) + **selling price** (end of holding)

#### Formulas
$$P_0 = \frac{Div_1}{1+r} + \frac{P_1}{1+r} \quad (\text{1 year})$$
$$P_0 = \frac{Div_1}{1+r} + \frac{Div_2}{(1+r)^2} + \frac{P_2}{(1+r)^2} \quad (\text{2 years})$$
$$P_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{H}\frac{Div_t}{(1+r)^t} + \frac{P_H}{(1+r)^H} \quad (H \text{ years})$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $r$ | **Cost of equity / market capitalisation rate**; required return; shareholders' opportunity cost of capital (higher risk → higher r) |
| $P_H$ | **Terminal or horizon value** |

- Bond: r = **YTM**; Stock: r = **cost of equity**

#### Example: XYZ Company
$$P_0 = \frac{3}{1.12} + \frac{3.24}{1.12^2} + \frac{3.50 + 94.48}{1.12^3} = \$75.00$$

### 3.6.6 Dividend Discount Model and Types of Stocks

#### DDM (H → ∞)
- Corporations are **going concerns** with perpetual life unless **bankruptcy or acquisition**
- As H → ∞, PV of terminal price **approaches zero**
$$P_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{Div_t}{(1+r)^t} = \text{PV(Expected future dividends)}$$

#### Types of Stocks
| Type | Dividend Pattern | Valuation Tool |
|---|---|---|
| **Zero growth** | g = 0; same dividend forever | Perpetuity: $P_0 = \frac{Div_1}{r}$ |
| **Constant growth** | g constant forever | Growing perpetuity: $P_0 = \frac{Div_1}{r-g}$, **requires g < r** |
| **Differential growth** | g1 for some years, then g2 forever (g1 > g2) | Growing annuity + perpetuity/growing perpetuity |

- Growth phase: g1 may exceed r; maturity: g2 below cost of equity

### 3.6.7 Valuation of Zero, Constant and Differential Growth Stocks

#### Example (r = 10%)
| Stock | Data | Value |
|---|---|---|
| A (zero growth) | $10 forever | 10/0.10 = **$100** |
| B (constant growth) | Div1 = $5, g = 4% | 5/(0.10 − 0.04) = **$83.33** |
| C (differential) | Div1 = $5, 20% growth yrs 2–6, 0% after | **$104.50** (most valuable) |

#### Stock C Working
$$P_0 = \frac{Div_1}{r-g_1}\left[1-\left(\frac{1+g_1}{1+r}\right)^6\right] + \frac{1}{(1+r)^6}\times\frac{Div_7}{r-g_2}$$
- PV growing annuity (yrs 1–6) = $\frac{5}{0.10-0.20}\left[1-\left(\frac{1.2}{1.1}\right)^6\right] = 34.28$
- Perpetuity from year 7: $Div_7 = 5 \times 1.2^5$; value at t = 6 = $Div_7/r$; PV = $\frac{5 \times 1.2^5}{0.1 \times 1.1^6} = 70.23$
- $P_0 = 34.28 + 70.23 \approx$ **$104.50**
- **C/r gives value one period before the first cash flow**

### 3.6.8 Valuing a Constant-Growth Stock

#### Example: Dividend $1.35, g = 2.75%, r = 9.5%
| Case | Treatment | Price |
|---|---|---|
| (a) **Just paid** $1.35 | **Ignore** past dividend; $Div_1 = 1.35 \times 1.0275$ | $\frac{1.35 \times 1.0275}{0.095-0.0275}$ = **$20.55** |
| (b) **About to pay** $1.35 | Include $1.35 received today | 1.35 + 20.55 = **$21.90** |

#### Alternative Method for (b)
- First cash flow at t = 0 → $\frac{C}{r-g} = \frac{1.35}{0.0675} = 20$ is value at **t = −1**
- Shift one period right: 20 × 1.095 = **$21.90**
- **Do NOT include dividends already paid**; price = PV of **future** dividends only

### 3.6.9 Challenges of Stock Valuation by Fundamentals

| Challenge | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Dividends far in future** | Growing firms may not pay dividends (e.g., **Amazon never paid a cash dividend**); reinvesting maximises value when investments earn more than the opportunity cost of capital |
| **Forecast uncertainty** | **Small changes in g → large changes** in estimated price ($Div_1/(r-g)$) |
| **Share repurchases** | Do **not invalidate** DDM but are **irregular and unpredictable** → hard to forecast |

## Quick Revision Summary

### Key Definitions
- **Coupon:** periodic interest = coupon rate (APR)/m × face value
- **Face/par value:** repaid at maturity; fixed on bond certificate
- **YTM:** expected return if bought today and held to maturity; bondholders' opportunity cost (APR)
- **Par / premium / discount bond:** P = F / P > F / P < F
- **Interest rate risk:** sensitivity of bond prices to interest rate changes
- **Holding period return:** realised, backward-looking return
- **Investment grade:** BBB and above; **junk:** BB and below
- **Inflation-indexed bonds (TIPS, IIBs):** fixed real return
- **Market order:** immediate; **limit order:** conditional on limit price
- **Cost of equity:** required return on stock
- **DDM:** price = PV of all expected future dividends

### Important Formulas
- $P = \frac{C}{r}\left[1-\frac{1}{(1+r)^t}\right] + \frac{F}{(1+r)^t}$
- Zero-coupon: $P = \frac{F}{(1+r)^t}$
- $\text{HPR} = \frac{\text{Coupon} + (P_1 - P_0)}{P_0}$
- $C_{real,t} = \frac{C_{nominal,t}}{(1+i)^t}$
- $1 + r_{real} = \frac{1+r_{nominal}}{1+i}$; $r_{real} \approx r_{nominal} - i$
- $P_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{H}\frac{Div_t}{(1+r)^t} + \frac{P_H}{(1+r)^H}$
- Zero growth: $P_0 = Div_1/r$; Constant growth: $P_0 = Div_1/(r-g)$, g < r
- Comparables: Price = mean P/E of comparables × EPS

### Must-Remember Points
- Coupon rate, face value, frequency, maturity **never change** with market conditions
- Price and YTM move **inversely**; longer maturity → **greater** interest rate risk
- YTM falls → return > initial YTM; unchanged → equal; rises → lower
- Zero-coupon bonds always trade at a **discount**; perpetual bonds have **no maturity**
- T-bills ≤ 1 yr; T-notes 1–10 yrs; T-bonds > 10 yrs
- US govt FV $1,000; Indian govt FV ₹100; both pay semiannually
- Corporate yields > government yields due to **default and liquidity risk**
- Discount nominal CFs at nominal rates, real CFs at real rates
- Market buy → lowest ask; market sell → highest bid; bids descending, asks ascending
- Dealer: inventory, bid-ask spread, bonds; broker: no inventory, commission, stocks
- Ignore dividends **already paid**; include dividend **about to be paid**
- P/E unsuitable for start-ups; DDM sensitive to g

# Module 4: Capital Budgeting

## 4.1 Introduction to Capital Budgeting

### 4.1.1 Meaning and Scope

#### Definition
- **Capital budgeting** is the process a firm uses to **evaluate potential investment projects** and decide which to accept and which to reject.
- Techniques covered: **Net Present Value (NPV)**, **Payback Period**, **Discounted Payback Period**, **Internal Rate of Return (IRR)**, plus **estimation of project cash flows**.

#### Key Concept: Properties of a Sound Investment Rule
An investment rule leads to **inferior decisions** if it:

| Flaw | Why it is a problem |
|------|--------------------|
| **Does not consider time value of money** | Time has money value |
| **Is affected by tastes and preferences of managers** | Managerial preference has nothing to do with value addition |
| **Is influenced by choice of accounting method** | Accounting method does not affect cash flows |
| **Depends on profitability of existing business** | Existing profitability does not decide whether a project adds value |

- **Goal:** accept projects that **increase shareholder wealth**; reject those that decrease it (financial goal = **shareholder wealth maximisation**).

### 4.1.2 Survey of CFOs (Graham and Harvey)

#### Key Concept: Usage of Techniques

| Technique | % of US CFOs using it |
|-----------|----------------------|
| **IRR** | 76% |
| **NPV** | 75% |
| **Payback** | 57% |
| **Book rate of return** | 20% (least used; not covered) |

- Percentages sum to **more than 100%** because a manager can use **more than one technique** for the same project.

## 4.2 Net Present Value and the NPV Decision Rule

### 4.2.1 NPV Fundamentals

#### Definition
- **NPV = Present value net of initial investment**

$$NPV = PV - \text{Initial Outlay} = C_0 + \sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t}$$

- $C_0$ = initial cash flow (outflow, negative); $C_t$ = cash flow at end of year $t$; $r$ = **opportunity cost of capital**.
- NPV depends **solely** on (1) **forecasted project cash flows** and (2) **opportunity cost of capital**.
- NPV **uses cash flows**, uses **all** cash flows, and **discounts them properly** (according to riskiness).

#### Key Concept: NPV Decision Rule
- **Accept if NPV > 0**; reject if NPV < 0.
- Positive NPV **increases shareholder wealth**; negative NPV decreases it → NPV is **consistent with shareholder wealth maximisation**.

#### Formula: Adding-Up Property
$$NPV(A+B) = NPV(A) + NPV(B)$$
- NPVs can be added because all are measured in **today's rupees**.
- **Only NPV** satisfies the adding-up property; no other technique does.

### 4.2.2 Advantages and Disadvantages of NPV

| Advantages | Disadvantage |
|-----------|--------------|
| **Recognises time value of money** | **Not as easy to communicate** to a layperson (e.g., "NPV = Rs 100" is hard to interpret) |
| **Clear benchmark** (zero) — independent of managers' tastes | |
| **Not affected by choice of accounting method** (uses cash flows) | |

- **NPV is the most superior** of all capital budgeting techniques.

## 4.3 Payback and Discounted Payback

### 4.3.1 Payback Period

#### Definition
- **Payback period:** time (number of years) it takes for a project to **recover its initial investment**, i.e., years before **cumulative cash flow equals initial outlay**.

#### Key Concept: Acceptance Criterion
- **Accept if payback period ≤ specified cutoff period** (set by **management**).

#### Key Concept: Pros and Cons

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Easy to understand and calculate** | **No clear benchmark** — cutoff period is **arbitrary** (depends on managers' tastes) |
| **Biased towards liquidity** (favours quick payback) | **Biased against long-term projects** — **ignores all cash flows after cutoff** |
| | **Ignores time value of money** — gives **equal weight** to all cash flows before cutoff |

#### Key Concept: Example (OCC = 10%, cutoff = 2 years)

| Project | C0 | C1 | C2 | C3 | Payback | NPV@10% |
|---------|----|----|----|----|---------|---------|
| A | −2,000 | 500 | 500 | 5,000 | 3 (Reject) | +2,624 (Accept) |
| B | −2,000 | 500 | 1,800 | 0 | 2 (Accept) | −58 (Reject) |
| C | −2,000 | 1,800 | 500 | 0 | 2 (Accept) | +50 (Accept) |

- Payback **rejects A** (should be accepted) and **accepts B** (should be rejected).
- A's 5,000 in year 3 is ignored (after cutoff); B and C are treated the same despite different timing (no time value).

### 4.3.2 Discounted Payback Period

#### Definition
- **Discounted payback period:** time it takes for a project to recover its initial investment **on a discounted basis** (after considering time value of money).
- Modification of the simple payback method.

#### Key Concept: Acceptance Criterion
- **Accept if discounted payback ≤ specified cutoff period** (set by management).

#### Key Concept: Pros and Cons

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Will never accept a negative NPV project** | **No clear benchmark** — cutoff arbitrary |
| **Biased towards liquidity** | **Biased against long-term projects** — ignores cash flows after cutoff |
| **Considers time value of money** | |

- **Why never accepts negative NPV:** a negative NPV project **never recovers** its investment in discounted terms → discounted payback is **not defined**.

#### Key Concept: Example (OCC = 10%, cutoff = 2 years)

| Project | PV C0 | PV C1 | PV C2 | PV C3 | Disc. Payback | NPV@10% |
|---------|-------|-------|-------|-------|---------------|---------|
| A | −2,000 | 455 | 413 | 3,757 | 3 (Reject) | +2,624 (Accept) |
| B | −2,000 | 455 | 1,488 | 0 | Not defined (Reject) | −58 (Reject) |
| C | −2,000 | 1,636 | 413 | 0 | 2 (Accept) | +50 (Accept) |

- Only mistake: **A rejected** though it should be accepted (3,757 after cutoff ignored).

## 4.4 Internal Rate of Return

### 4.4.1 IRR and the IRR Decision Rule

#### Definition
- **IRR:** the **discount rate that makes NPV = 0**.

$$0 = C_0 + \sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{C_t}{(1+IRR)^t}$$

- Single-period example: invest 100, receive 150 → $100 = \frac{150}{1+IRR}$ → **IRR = 50%** (same as profit/investment).
- For **multi-year projects**, rate of return must be computed through IRR.

#### Key Concept: Acceptance Criterion
- **Accept if IRR > opportunity cost of capital**; reject if IRR < OCC.

#### Key Concept: Advantages and Disadvantages

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Considers time value of money** | **Does not distinguish investing vs borrowing** |
| **Clear benchmark** (OCC — not managers' tastes) | **IRR may not exist** |
| **Easy to understand and communicate** (e.g., "at least 20% return") | **Multiple IRRs** possible |
| | **Problems with mutually exclusive projects** (scale, economic life) |

### 4.4.2 Computing IRR and Relationship with NPV

#### Key Concept: Example
- Machine costs $4,000; cash flows $2,000 (Year 1), $4,000 (Year 2).

$$-4000 + \frac{2000}{1+IRR} + \frac{4000}{(1+IRR)^2} = 0 \Rightarrow IRR = 28.08\%$$

- Quadratic equation; only **real and positive** roots are of interest.
- Accept if **OCC < 28.08%**.

#### Key Concept: Why "Internal"
- IRR requires only **magnitude and timing of cash flows**, which are **internal to the project**.
- IRR calculation **does not require OCC**; OCC is needed only for the **accept/reject decision**.

#### Key Concept: NPV Profile and Decision Consistency

| Condition | NPV |
|-----------|-----|
| **IRR > OCC** | **+NPV** |
| **IRR = OCC** | **0 NPV** |
| **IRR < OCC** | **−NPV** |

- **NPV and IRR give the same decision** as long as **NPV is a continuously declining function of the discount rate**.
- If NPV **increases** with discount rate, the two rules disagree.

#### Key Concept: IRR vs Opportunity Cost of Capital

| IRR | Opportunity Cost of Capital |
|-----|-----------------------------|
| **Profitability measure** | **Expected return in financial markets at the same level of risk** |
| Depends solely on **amount and timing** of project cash flows | Depends on **risk of the project** (higher risk → higher OCC) |

### 4.4.3 Pitfalls of IRR

#### Key Concept: Pitfall 1 — No IRR

| Project | C0 | C1 | C2 | IRR | NPV@10% |
|---------|----|----|----|-----|---------|
| F | −1,000 | +3,000 | −2,500 | None | −339 |

- Project F has **negative NPV at all discount rates**; NPV curve never cuts the discount-rate axis.
- A project with **positive NPV at all rates** also has no IRR.
- No IRR → nothing to compare with OCC.

#### Key Concept: Pitfall 2 — Multiple IRRs
- **Project G:** −30 bn at t=0; +10 bn each year for years 1–9; −65 bn cleanup at year 10.
- **Descartes' rule of signs:** there can be **as many positive real roots as there are changes of sign**.
- G has **2 sign changes** (−30 → +10; +10 → −65) → up to **2 IRRs**: **3.5% and 19.54%**.
- Sign changes are a **necessary but not sufficient** condition for that many roots.
- Problem: unclear **which IRR to compare** with OCC.
- Take up G if **OCC is between 3.5% and 19.54%**.

#### Key Concept: Pitfall 3 — Investing (Lending) vs Borrowing

| Project | C0 | C1 | IRR | NPV@10% |
|---------|----|----|-----|---------|
| D (Investing/Lending) | −1,000 | +1,500 | 50% | +364 |
| E (Borrowing) | +1,000 | −1,500 | 50% | −364 |

| Feature | Investing / Lending | Borrowing |
|---------|--------------------|-----------|
| Cash flow pattern | **− + + +** | **+ − − −** |
| NPV vs discount rate | **Decreases** | **Increases** |
| Objective | Earn as high a return as possible | Lender earns as low a return as possible |
| **IRR rule** | **Accept if IRR > OCC** | **Accept if IRR < OCC** |

- Same IRR, opposite NPVs → IRR **cannot distinguish** investing from borrowing.
- **Sale of insurance** resembles borrowing: premiums received up-front, claims paid later.

#### Key Concept: Types of Projects

| Type | Definition | Decision |
|------|-----------|----------|
| **Independent** | Acceptance/rejection does not prevent acceptance of others (A or B or both) | Accept all meeting minimum criteria |
| **Dependent / Contingent** | Acceptance depends on acceptance of other project(s) (if A, only then B) | — |
| **Mutually Exclusive** | Acceptance prevents acceptance of alternatives (either A or B, **NOT** both) | **Rank and select the best** |

#### Key Concept: Pitfall 4A — Mutually Exclusive Projects Differing in Scale

| Project | C0 | C1 | IRR | NPV@10% |
|---------|----|----|-----|---------|
| H | −10,000 | +20,000 | 100% | +8,182 |
| I | −20,000 | +35,000 | 75% | +11,818 |
| **I − H** | −10,000 | +15,000 | **50%** | +3,636 |

- H has higher IRR, I has higher NPV → **IRR is unreliable** when projects differ in **scale of investment**.
- **NPV measures change in shareholder wealth** → choose **I**.
- **Incremental IRR** (50%) > OCC (10%) → **I is incrementally superior** to H.

#### Key Concept: Pitfall 4B — Mutually Exclusive Projects Differing in Economic Lives

| Project | C0 | C1 | C2 | C3 | C4, C5… | IRR | NPV@10% |
|---------|----|----|----|----|---------|-----|---------|
| J (3 years) | −9,000 | +6,000 | +5,000 | +4,000 | 0 | 33% | +3,592 |
| K (perpetual) | −9,000 | +1,800 | +1,800 | +1,800 | +1,800 | 20% | +9,000 |
| **K − J** | 0 | −4,200 | −3,200 | −2,200 | +1,800 | **15.6%** | +5,408 |

- Same scale, different lives (or cash flow patterns) → **IRR unreliable**.
- At OCC = **15.6%**, both have equal NPV; OCC < 15.6% → **K superior**; OCC > 15.6% → **J superior**.
- OCC = 10% → choose **K**; incremental IRR 15.6% > 10% confirms.

#### Key Concept: Rules for the Incremental Cash Flow Approach
- Compute incremental cash flows so that the **first cash flow is negative** (resembles a **traditional investment project**: outflow followed by inflows).
- Usable **only if projects have the same risk** and hence **same OCC**.
- Incremental cash flows may themselves have **multiple IRRs or no IRR** → **not foolproof**.
- Given a choice, **always prefer NPV**.

#### Key Concept: Why IRR Is Still Popular
- IRR **does not require OCC** for computation; managers who do not know the exact OCC can still compute IRR, but not a precise NPV.

### 4.4.4 Capital Budgeting Practice

#### Key Concept: Problem (OCC = 10%, cutoff = 3 years)

| Project | C0 | C1 | C2 | C3 | C4 | C5 | NPV | Payback | Disc. Payback |
|---------|----|----|----|----|----|----|-----|---------|---------------|
| A | −1,000 | 1,000 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | −91 (Reject) | 1 (Accept) | Not defined (Reject) |
| B | −2,000 | 1,000 | 1,000 | 4,000 | 1,000 | 1,000 | +4,045 (Accept) | 2 (Accept) | 3 (Accept) |
| C | −3,000 | 1,000 | 1,000 | 0 | 1,000 | 1,000 | +39 (Accept) | 4 (Reject) | 5 (Reject) |

- PVs at 10%: 1,000 → 909 (Y1), 826 (Y2), 3,005 (B's Y3), 683 (Y4), 621 (Y5).
- **NPV:** accept B and C.
- **Payback (cutoff 3):** accept A and B.
- **Discounted payback (cutoff 3):** accept **only B**.
- A (negative NPV) is **never accepted** by discounted payback, whatever the cutoff.

### 4.4.5 Mutually Exclusive Projects and Incremental IRR

#### Key Concept: Problem (OCC = 9%, $ thousands)

| Project | C0 | C1 | C2 | IRR | NPV@9% |
|---------|----|----|----|-----|--------|
| A | −400 | 250 | 300 | 23% | 81.86 |
| B | −200 | 140 | 179 | 36% | 79.10 |
| **A − B** | −200 | 110 | 121 | **10%** | 2.76 |

- Choosing B for higher IRR is **wrong**: projects are mutually exclusive and differ in scale; larger A may have **lower IRR but higher NPV**.
- Use **A − B** (first cash flow negative).

#### Formula: Solving Incremental IRR
$$-200 + \frac{110}{1+IRR} + \frac{121}{(1+IRR)^2} = 0$$
- Let $x = 1 + IRR$: $-200x^2 + 110x + 121 = 0$
$$x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a} = \frac{-110 \pm 330}{-400}$$
- $x_1 = -0.55$ (rejected), $x_2 = 1.1$ → **IRR = 10%**.
- $b^2 - 4ac$ = **discriminant**; $\ge 0$ → real roots.
- 10% > 9% → **A incrementally superior**; NPV confirms (81.86 > 79.10).

### 4.4.6 Computing IRR Using Excel

#### Key Concept: Excel IRR Function
- **=IRR(values, [guess])** — only required argument is the **array of cash flows**.
- Example: −4,000, 2,000, 4,000 → **28.08%**.
- Optional **guess** defaults to **10%**; Excel returns the IRR **closest to the guess**.
- **Varying the guess** helps find **multiple IRRs**.

## 4.5 Estimating Project Cash Flows

### 4.5.1 Project Cash Flows and Operating Cash Flow

#### Key Concept: Rule 1 — Do Not Confuse Cash Flow with Accounting Income
- Accounting income / net profit / **PAT** may be more or less than project cash flow.
- **Purpose of accounting income:** show how well a firm is performing.
- **Project cash flow (Net Cash Flow, NCF / Free Cash Flow, FCF):** net cash generated or required by a project in a year **after paying operating costs, taxes, and necessary capital expenditures**.

#### Formula: Net Cash Flow

| Approach | Formula |
|----------|---------|
| **Direct** | $NCF = \text{Cash Inflow} - \text{Cash Outflow}$ |
| **Indirect** | $NCF = OCF + CF_{\text{capital investment \& disposal}} + CF_{\text{changes in working capital}}$ |

- Cash inflow has **+ve** sign; cash outflow has **−ve** sign.

#### Formula: Operating Cash Flow (Three Approaches)

| Approach | Formula | Why the name |
|----------|---------|-------------|
| **Bottom-Up** | $OCF = PAT + \text{Depreciation}$ | Starts from **PAT (bottom line)** |
| **Top-Down** | $OCF = \text{Sales} - \text{Cash Expenses} - \text{Taxes}$ | Starts from **Sales (top line)** |
| **Tax Shield** | $OCF = (\text{Sales} - \text{Cash Exp.})(1 - T) + \text{Dep.} \times T$ | Separates the depreciation tax shield |

- Depreciation is added back because it is a **non-cash expense** subtracted in computing PAT.
- In top-down, depreciation **cancels out**.
- All three approaches give the **same OCF**.

#### Definition: Depreciation Tax Shield
$$\text{Depreciation Tax Shield} = \text{Depreciation} \times T$$
- $(\text{Sales} - \text{Cash Exp.})(1-T)$ = **after-tax income assuming no depreciation**.
- Depreciation is **non-cash but tax-deductible** → **reduces taxes**; "shields" income from tax.
- **Depreciation tax shield = reduction in taxes attributable to depreciation.**

### 4.5.2 Working Capital and Project Cash Flows

#### Definition
- **Working capital:** capital needed for **day-to-day operations** of the project/firm.
- Main components: **Inventory**, **Accounts Receivable (AR)**, **Accounts Payable (AP)**.

#### Key Concept: Cash Cycle
- Firm buys raw materials on credit → pays suppliers later (**cash goes out**) → converts to finished goods → sells on credit → collects from customers later (**cash comes back**).
- **Cash cycle:** time from cash going out to cash coming back; represents cash **tied up in operations**.
- **Net working capital** measures this cash tied up.

#### Formula: Net Working Capital
$$NWC = CA - CL = \text{Inventory} + AR - AP$$
$$\Delta NWC_t = NWC_t - NWC_{t-1}$$
$$CF \text{ from change in NWC} = -\Delta NWC$$

| Event | Cash Flow |
|-------|-----------|
| **Initial investment** in working capital | **Outflow** |
| **Increase** in NWC (e.g., sales growth) | **Outflow** |
| **Decrease** in NWC | **Inflow** |
| **Recovery** at project end (inventory sold, AR collected) | **Inflow** |

#### Key Concept: Example (₹, 4-year project)

| | 2026 | 2027 | 2028 | 2029 | 2030 |
|---|------|------|------|------|------|
| AR | 0 | 1,50,000 | 2,25,000 | 1,90,000 | 0 |
| Inventory | 75,000 | 1,30,000 | 1,30,000 | 95,000 | 0 |
| AP | 25,000 | 50,000 | 50,000 | 35,000 | 0 |
| **NWC** | 50,000 | 2,30,000 | 3,05,000 | 2,50,000 | 0 |
| **Change in NWC** | 50,000 | 1,80,000 | 75,000 | −55,000 | −2,50,000 |
| **CF from ΔNWC** | −50,000 | −1,80,000 | −75,000 | +55,000 | +2,50,000 |

### 4.5.3 Estimating Incremental Project Cash Flows

#### Key Concept: Rule 2 — Estimate Cash Flows on an Incremental Basis
- Project value depends on **all additional/incremental cash flows** that follow from project acceptance.

| Item | Treatment |
|------|-----------|
| **Negative incidental effect** (cannibalises existing sales, e.g., EV project reducing IC-engine two-wheeler sales) | **Outflow** |
| **Positive incidental effect** (helps existing business) | **Inflow** |
| **After-sales cash flows** (services, spare parts) | **Inflow** |
| **Salvage value (net of taxes)** | **Inflow** |
| **Opportunity cost of existing resource** | **Outflow** |
| **Allocated existing overheads** (e.g., CEO salary) | **Not** a cash flow (include only **extra** overhead expenses) |
| **Sunk costs** (past, irreversible) | **Ignore** — no cash flow |

#### Key Concept: Opportunity Cost of Existing Resource
- Relevant even when **no cash changes hands**; it is the cash flow the resource could generate if the project were rejected.

| Project life | Opportunity cost |
|-------------|------------------|
| **Perpetual** | **Market price** of the resource (e.g., land worth ₹50,00,000) |
| **Finite** | **Market rent** of the resource over the project life |

#### Key Concept: Overheads and Sunk Costs
- **Overheads:** expenses to support the company's business; not tied to a project but must be paid; accountant allocates a charge.
- Accountant's allocation may **not represent true extra expenses**.
- **Sunk costs:** accept/reject decision **does not affect** them → ignore.

### 4.5.4 Additional Rules for Estimating Project Cash Flows

#### Key Concept: Rule 3 — Treat Inflation Consistently

| Cash flows | Discount rate |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Nominal** | **Nominal** rate |
| **Real** | **Real** rate |

- Both give the **same NPV**.
- **Never** discount real cash flows at nominal rates or nominal cash flows at real rates.
- Interest rates are **usually quoted in nominal terms**.

#### Key Concept: Rule 4 — Separate Investment and Financing Decisions
- Forecast cash flows assuming the project is **all-equity financed** (ignore financing side effects).
- Additional value from financing is considered **separately**.
- Reason: to know whether the project **in itself** is value-adding.
- Debt proceeds, interest and principal repayments → **ignore**.

#### Key Concept: Rule 5 — Forecast Cash Flows After Taxes
- **Taxes are a cash expense** like wages and raw materials → estimate cash flows on an **after-tax basis**.

### 4.5.5 Identifying Incremental Cash Flows

#### Key Concept: Classification

| Activity | Classification |
|----------|---------------|
| Capital expenditure | **Incremental outflow** |
| Disposal of plant with salvage value | **Incremental inflow** |
| Initial investment in working capital | **Incremental outflow** |
| Increase in working capital | **Incremental outflow** |
| No change in working capital | **No incremental cash flow** |
| Decrease in working capital | **Incremental inflow** |
| Recovery of working capital at end | **Incremental inflow** |
| Opportunity cost of existing resource | **Incremental outflow** |
| Sunk cost | **No incremental cash flow** |
| Positive incidental effect | **Incremental inflow** |
| Negative incidental effect | **Incremental outflow** |
| Allocation of existing overhead | **No incremental cash flow** |
| Tax savings due to project losses (offset against profits elsewhere) | **Incremental inflow** |
| Borrowing proceeds for capex | **Ignore** (financing) |
| Interest on loan for capex | **Ignore** (financing) |

### 4.5.6 Incremental Cash Flows for a Manufacturing Project

#### Key Concept: New Plant on Owned Site (buildings to be demolished)

| Item | Incremental? | Reason |
|------|-------------|--------|
| Market value of site and existing buildings | **Yes** (outflow) | Opportunity cost |
| Demolition and site clearance costs | **Yes** (outflow) | Extra cost due to project |
| New access road built last year | **No** | Sunk cost |
| Lost earnings on other products due to executive time | **Yes** (outflow) | Negative incidental effect |
| Share of president's jet lease | **No** | Allocated overhead |
| Future depreciation of new plant | **No** | Non-cash allocation of cost |
| Tax reduction from tax depreciation | **Yes** (inflow) | Depreciation tax shield |
| Initial investment in raw material inventories | **Yes** (outflow) | Working capital |
| Money already spent on engineering design | **No** | Sunk cost |

### 4.5.7 Inflation and Project NPV

#### Formula: Conversions
$$\text{Nominal CF}_t = \text{Real CF}_t \times (1 + i)^t$$
$$r_{real} = \frac{1 + r_{nominal}}{1 + i} - 1$$
- $i$ = inflation rate.

#### Key Concept: Example
- Outlay ₹100; real cash flows ₹35, ₹50, ₹30 (Years 1–3); nominal rate 15%; inflation 10%.

| Year | Real CF | Nominal CF |
|------|---------|-----------|
| 0 | −100 | −100 |
| 1 | 35 | 35 × 1.10 = 38.50 |
| 2 | 50 | 50 × 1.10² = 60.50 |
| 3 | 30 | 30 × 1.10³ = 39.93 |

- **Method 1:** nominal CFs at 15% → **NPV ≈ ₹5.5**.
- **Method 2:** $r_{real} = 1.15/1.10 - 1 = 4.5\%$; real CFs at 4.5% → **NPV ≈ ₹5.5**.
- Both methods give the **same NPV**.

## Quick Revision Summary

### Key Definitions
- **Capital budgeting:** process of evaluating potential investment projects.
- **NPV:** PV of cash flows net of initial investment.
- **Payback period:** years to recover initial investment (undiscounted).
- **Discounted payback:** years to recover initial investment in discounted terms.
- **IRR:** discount rate making NPV = 0.
- **Opportunity cost of capital:** expected return in financial markets at the same risk.
- **Mutually exclusive projects:** either A or B, not both.
- **Depreciation tax shield:** reduction in taxes attributable to depreciation.
- **Net working capital:** Inventory + AR − AP.
- **Sunk cost:** past, irreversible outflow — ignore.
- **Incidental effect:** project's effect on firm's existing business.

### Important Formulas
- $NPV = C_0 + \sum C_t/(1+r)^t$; $NPV(A+B) = NPV(A) + NPV(B)$
- $0 = C_0 + \sum C_t/(1+IRR)^t$
- $NCF = OCF + CF_{capex \& disposal} + CF_{\Delta NWC}$
- $OCF = PAT + Dep = Sales - Cash\ Exp - Taxes = (Sales - Cash\ Exp)(1-T) + Dep \times T$
- $CF_{\Delta NWC} = -(NWC_t - NWC_{t-1})$
- $\text{After-tax salvage} = SV - T(SV - BV)$
- $\text{SLM Depreciation} = (Cost - Salvage)/Life$
- $\text{Nominal CF}_t = \text{Real CF}_t(1+i)^t$; $r_{real} = (1+r_{nom})/(1+i) - 1$

### Must-Remember Points
- **NPV is the most superior** technique; only NPV has the **adding-up property**.
- **Payback** ignores time value and post-cutoff cash flows; cutoff is **arbitrary**.
- **Discounted payback never accepts a negative NPV project**, but still ignores post-cutoff cash flows.
- NPV and IRR agree when NPV **declines continuously** with the discount rate.
- IRR pitfalls: **no IRR**, **multiple IRRs** (sign changes, Descartes' rule), **borrowing** (accept if IRR < OCC), **mutually exclusive** projects differing in **scale** or **life**.
- For mutually exclusive projects: use **incremental IRR** with first cash flow negative; valid only for **same-risk** projects.
- Excel **=IRR(values, guess)**; default guess 10%; vary guess to find multiple IRRs.
- Cash flow rules: (1) cash flow ≠ accounting income; (2) incremental basis; (3) inflation consistently; (4) separate investment and financing; (5) after taxes.
- Opportunity cost: **market price** (perpetual) vs **market rent** (finite life).

## 5.2 Risk and Return Basics

### 5.2.1 Introduction to Risk

#### Definition

- **Risk = variability of returns** (variability in the thing we are interested in, here return)
- There is **no universally agreed-upon definition** of risk; returns on most assets vary unpredictably over time
- First measures of risk: **variance** and **standard deviation**

#### Formula

$$\sigma_X^2 = \frac{\sum (X_i - \bar{X})^2}{N-1}$$

$$\sigma_X = \sqrt{\sigma_X^2}$$

- Divide by **N − 1** because variance is **estimated from a sample** of observed returns
- **Standard deviation** is the **positive square root** of variance; it **cannot be negative**
- Standard deviation is in the **same units as the variable**

#### Worked Example

| Year | Actual Return (%) | Mean (%) | Deviation (%) | Squared Deviation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 15 | 10.5 | 4.5 | 20.25 |
| 2 | 9 | 10.5 | −1.5 | 2.25 |
| 3 | 6 | 10.5 | −4.5 | 20.25 |
| 4 | 12 | 10.5 | 1.5 | 2.25 |
| Total | | | 0 | 45 |

- Variance $= 45/(4-1) = 15$
- Standard deviation $= \sqrt{15} = 3.873\%$

### 5.2.2 Risk and Return of Asset Classes

#### Key Concept

| Portfolio | Features | Risk Intuition |
|---|---|---|
| **US Treasury Bills (T-Bills)** | Govt issued, mature in < 1 year; no default risk; short maturity, stable prices | Least risky (minimal interest rate risk) |
| **US Treasury Bonds (T-Bonds)** | Govt issued, 1–10 year maturity; no default risk; prices fluctuate with rates | Middle (subject to **interest rate risk**) |
| **US Common Stocks** | Prices move with ups and downs of issuing companies | Most risky |

- **Long-term bond prices are more sensitive to interest rate changes** than short-term bond prices
- **Interest rate ↑ ⇒ bond price ↓**, and vice versa

#### Historical Evidence (1900–2023)

| Portfolio | Std. Dev. (σ) | Variance (σ²) | Avg. Nominal Return | Avg. Risk Premium |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T-Bills | 2.7% | 7.5 | 3.4% | 0 |
| T-Bonds | 9.3% | 79.7 | 5.0% | 1.6% |
| Common Stocks | 19.6% | 383.6 | 11.5% | 8.1% |

- T-Bills were **least variable** and earned the **lowest return**; stocks were **most variable** and earned the **highest return**
- Higher variability has historically offered **higher average return**
- T-Bills are the safest asset and are treated as the **risk-free asset**

#### Formula

- **Risk premium** = return earned over and above the return on T-Bills (risk-free rate)

$$\text{Risk Premium}_i = r_i - r_f$$

$$\text{Return} = \text{Risk-free Rate} + \text{Risk Premium}$$

- T-Bond risk premium $= 5.0 - 3.4 = 1.6\%$
- Common stock risk premium $= 11.5 - 3.4 = 8.1\%$

### 5.2.3 Security Risk and Return

#### Key Concept

- Security risk is expressed by listing **all possible states**, the **return in each state**, and the **probability of each state**
- States are **mutually exclusive** (only one can occur at a time) and **exhaustive** (one of them must occur)

#### Formula

$$E(R) = \bar{R} = \sum P_i R_i$$

$$\sigma = \left[\sum P_i (R_i - \bar{R})^2\right]^{1/2}$$

- $P_i$ = probability of state $i$; $R_i$ = return in state $i$; $\bar{R}$ = expected (mean) return

#### Worked Example

| State | Probability | Return on A | Return on B |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boom | 0.25 | 28% | 10% |
| Normal | 0.50 | 15% | 13% |
| Recession | 0.25 | −2% | 10% |
| **Expected Return** | | **14.0%** | **11.5%** |
| **Std. Deviation** | | **10.65%** | **1.50%** |

- $E(R_A) = 0.25(28) + 0.5(15) + 0.25(-2) = 14\%$
- $E(R_B) = 0.25(10) + 0.5(13) + 0.25(10) = 11.5\%$
- $\sigma_A = [0.25(28-14)^2 + 0.5(15-14)^2 + 0.25(-2-14)^2]^{1/2} = 10.65\%$
- $\sigma_B = [0.25(10-11.5)^2 + 0.5(13-11.5)^2 + 0.25(10-11.5)^2]^{1/2} = 1.50\%$

### 5.2.4 Portfolio Risk and Return

#### Worked Example (Equal Investment in A and B)

| State | Probability | A | B | Portfolio (0.5A + 0.5B) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boom | 0.25 | 28% | 10% | 19% |
| Normal | 0.50 | 15% | 13% | 14% |
| Recession | 0.25 | −2% | 10% | 4% |
| Expected Return | | 14% | 11.5% | **12.75%** |
| Std. Deviation | | 10.65% | 1.50% | **5.45%** |

- Portfolio expected return $= 0.25(19) + 0.5(14) + 0.25(4) = 12.75\%$
- Weighted average of security returns $= 0.5(14) + 0.5(11.5) = 12.75\%$
- Portfolio σ $= [0.25(19-12.75)^2 + 0.5(14-12.75)^2 + 0.25(4-12.75)^2]^{1/2} = 5.45\%$
- Weighted average of security σ's $= 0.5(10.65) + 0.5(1.50) = 6.075\%$

#### Key Concept

- **Expected portfolio return = weighted average of expected returns** of the securities

$$E(R_P) = \sum x_i E(R_i)$$

- **Portfolio standard deviation < weighted average of individual standard deviations**
- Reason: **benefit of diversification**

## 5.3 Diversification

### 5.3.1 Covariance, Correlation and Portfolio Risk

#### Definition: Covariance

$$\sigma_{XY} = \frac{\sum (X_i - \bar{X})(Y_i - \bar{Y})}{N-1}$$

- Tells the **direction** of the relationship between two variables
- **Positive** covariance ⇒ positively related (X ↑, Y ↑); **negative** ⇒ negatively related
- **No bounds**: ranges from **−∞ to +∞**
- **Covariance of a variable with itself = its variance**: $\sigma_{XX} = \sigma_X^2$

#### Definition: Correlation

$$\rho_{XY} = \frac{\sigma_{XY}}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y}$$

- Bounded between **−1 and +1**
- **+1** = perfectly positively correlated; **−1** = perfectly negatively correlated
- Gives **both direction and strength** of the relationship

| Feature | Covariance | Correlation |
|---|---|---|
| Symbol | $\sigma_{XY}$ | $\rho_{XY}$ |
| Range | −∞ to +∞ | −1 to +1 |
| Tells | Direction only | Direction and strength |
| Relationship captured | Linear only | Linear only |

- Both capture **only linear** relationships, **not non-linear** ones

#### Formula: Two-Stock Portfolio Variance

- Portfolio risk depends on **riskiness of securities** and **relationship among them**
- Built using a **2×2 matrix**: diagonal = **variance terms**, off-diagonal = **covariance terms**; portfolio variance = **sum of all four boxes**

| | Stock 1 | Stock 2 |
|---|---|---|
| **Stock 1** | $x_1^2\sigma_1^2$ | $x_1x_2\sigma_{12} = x_1x_2\rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2$ |
| **Stock 2** | $x_1x_2\sigma_{12} = x_1x_2\rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2$ | $x_2^2\sigma_2^2$ |

$$\sigma_P^2 = x_1^2\sigma_1^2 + x_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2x_1x_2\sigma_{12}$$

$$\sigma_P^2 = x_1^2\sigma_1^2 + x_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2x_1x_2\rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2$$

- $x_1, x_2$ = weights; $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$ = standard deviations; $\sigma_{12} = \rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2$ = covariance

#### Special Cases of Correlation

| Correlation | Result |
|---|---|
| $\rho_{12} = +1$ | $\sigma_P = x_1\sigma_1 + x_2\sigma_2$ (**weighted average** of σ's; no diversification benefit) |
| $\rho_{12} = -1$ | $\sigma_P = \lvert x_1\sigma_1 - x_2\sigma_2 \rvert$ |
| $\rho_{12} = -1$ and $x_1\sigma_1 = x_2\sigma_2$ | $\sigma_P = 0$ |

- $\rho = -1$ is **almost impossible in real life**, so $\sigma_P$ is **never 0** in practice

### 5.3.2 Applying Diversification

#### Worked Example: Two-Stock Portfolio

- Invest \$55 in Stock 1 ($r = 10\%$, $\sigma = 17.1\%$) and \$45 in Stock 2 ($r = 20\%$, $\sigma = 20.8\%$); $\rho = 0.65$
- Weights: $x_1 = 0.55$, $x_2 = 0.45$
- $\sigma_P^2 = 0.55^2(17.1^2) + 0.45^2(20.8^2) + 2(0.55)(0.45)(0.65)(17.1)(20.8) = 290.50$
- $\sigma_P = 17.04\%$ — **lower than both** individual σ's (17.1% and 20.8%)
- $E(R_P) = 0.55(10) + 0.45(20) = 14.5\%$

#### Effect of Correlation on Portfolio Risk

| Correlation | 1 | 0.8 | 0.5 | 0 | −1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Portfolio σ | 18.77% | 17.80% | 16.25% | 13.27% | 0.04% |

- Weighted average σ $= 0.55(17.1) + 0.45(20.8) = 18.77\%$
- Portfolio σ **equals weighted average** when $\rho = 1$
- Portfolio σ **declines as correlation falls** from +1 to −1
- Portfolio σ is **minimum** when $\rho = -1$

#### Worked Example: Adding a Third Stock

| | Existing Portfolio (1 & 2) | Stock 3 |
|---|---|---|
| Return | 14.5% | 25% |
| Std. Deviation | 17.04% | 22.00% |
| Weight | 0.50 | 0.50 |
| Correlation | 0.5 | |

- New portfolio return $= 0.5(14.5) + 0.5(25) = 19.75\%$
- New portfolio σ $= 16.95\%$ (lower than 17.04%)
- **Lower risk and yet higher return** through diversification (possible in some cases, not always)

#### Key Concept

- **Even a little diversification** gives a **substantial reduction in variability**
- Average portfolio σ **keeps falling as number of stocks increases**
- **Most of the benefit** comes with **relatively few stocks**
- Diversification works because stock prices **do not move exactly together** (correlation **< 1**)

### 5.3.3 Specific Risk and Market Risk

#### Definition

| Feature | Specific Risk | Market Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Other names | **Unsystematic, unique, idiosyncratic, diversifiable** risk | **Systematic, non-diversifiable** risk |
| Source | Peculiar to a **firm or industry** | **Economy-wide / market-wide** fluctuations shared by most businesses |
| Example | **Pilot strike** (affects aviation, little effect elsewhere) | **Global financial crisis, COVID-19** |
| Diversification | **Can be eliminated** | **Cannot be eliminated** |

#### Formula

$$\text{Total Risk} = \text{Specific Risk} + \text{Market Risk}$$

- Adding more securities (especially from other industries) **keeps reducing specific risk**
- A **reasonably well-diversified portfolio** carries **only (primarily) market risk**

## 5.4 Beta and CAPM

### 5.4.1 Measuring Market Risk: Beta

#### Definition

- **Beta (β)** = **sensitivity of a stock's return to the return on the market portfolio**
- Beta = **slope of the regression line** of individual stock returns (y-axis) on market returns (x-axis)
- **Market portfolio**: in theory, **all assets in the economy**; in practice, a broad index like **BSE SENSEX** or **NSE NIFTY 50**

#### Formula

$$\beta_i = \frac{\sigma_{im}}{\sigma_m^2} = \frac{\rho_{im}\,\sigma_i\,\sigma_m}{\sigma_m^2} = \frac{\rho_{im}\,\sigma_i}{\sigma_m}$$

- $\sigma_{im}$ = covariance of stock and market returns; $\sigma_m^2$ = market variance; $\rho_{im}$ = correlation of stock and market; $\sigma_i$ = stock σ; $\sigma_m$ = market σ

#### Key Beta Values

| Asset | Beta | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| **Market** | **1** (always) | $\sigma_{mm} = \sigma_m^2$, so $\beta_m = 1$ |
| **Risk-free asset (T-Bill)** | **0** (always) | Covariance with market is 0 |
| β = 1 | — | As much market risk as the market |
| β > 1 | — | More market risk than the market |
| β < 1 | — | Less market risk than the market |

#### Beta by Type of Company

| Company Type | Example | Beta | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Luxury goods** (cyclical) | BMW | **β > 1** | Sales and earnings highly sensitive to the economy |
| **Necessity goods** (defensive) | FMCG, Tobacco | **0 < β < 1** | People keep buying in boom or downturn |
| **Countercyclical** | Insolvency/restructuring practice | **β < 0** | Does well when market falls, poorly when it rises |

#### Portfolio Beta

$$\beta_P = \sum x_i \beta_i$$

- **Portfolio beta = weighted average of betas** of the securities
- A well-diversified portfolio carries **no specific risk**, only market risk
- **Risk of a well-diversified portfolio is proportional to portfolio beta**

### 5.4.2 Capital Asset Pricing Model

#### Key Concept

| Asset | Beta | Risk Premium |
|---|---|---|
| Treasury bills (risk-free) | 0 | 0 |
| Market portfolio | 1 | $r_m - r_f$ |
| Any security i | $\beta_i$ | $\beta_i(r_m - r_f)$ |

- CAPM given by **William Sharpe, John Lintner, and Jack Treynor**
- Answers: what is the expected risk premium **when beta is neither 0 nor 1?**
- In a competitive market, the **expected risk premium varies in direct proportion to beta**
- For a well-diversified portfolio, **appropriate measure of risk is β**

#### Formula

$$r_i - r_f = \beta_i(r_m - r_f)$$

$$r_i = r_f + \beta_i(r_m - r_f)$$

- $r_i$ = expected (required) return on security i; $r_f$ = risk-free rate; $r_m$ = expected market return; $(r_m - r_f)$ = **market risk premium**
- Expected return is **positively related to beta**
- CAPM gives the **opportunity cost of capital (discount rate)** for an investment; **higher project beta ⇒ higher required return**

#### Security Market Line (SML)

- **SML = plot of expected return (y-axis) against beta (x-axis)**
- Passes through **risk-free asset (β = 0, r = $r_f$)** and **market portfolio (β = 1, r = $r_m$)**
- **Intercept** $= r_f$; **slope** $= \frac{r_m - r_f}{1 - 0} = r_m - r_f$
- Equation of SML: $r = r_f + \beta(r_m - r_f)$ — **SML is the graphical form of CAPM**

### 5.4.3 Asset and Project Cost of Capital

#### Definition: Asset Cost of Capital

- A firm is a **collection of assets** funded by **debt (D)** and **equity (E)**
- **Asset cost of capital** = **opportunity cost of capital for investment in all the firm's assets** = **expected return on a portfolio of all the firm's outstanding debt and equity**
- For an **all-equity firm** (e.g., Infosys) = **expected return on the firm's stock**
- Also called **company cost of capital** or simply **cost of capital**
- **Appropriate discount rate for average-risk projects** (expansion of existing business)
- **Not appropriate** for new projects **more or less risky** than existing business

#### Project Cost of Capital

- Project risk is measured by its **beta**: **high beta ⇒ high risk**, **low beta ⇒ low risk**
- Per SML, **high-risk projects require a high rate of return** (high opportunity cost of capital)

| Project Position | Return vs Required | NPV | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Above SML** | Actual return > required return | Positive | **Accept** |
| **Below SML** | Actual return < required return | Negative | **Reject** |

#### Asset Cost of Capital Rule

- **Rule:** accept any project, **regardless of its risk**, if its return > company's asset cost of capital ($r_A$)
- Consequences:

| Project Type | Rule's Decision | Error |
|---|---|---|
| High-beta project below SML but above $r_A$ | Accept | **Accepts negative NPV projects** |
| Low-beta project above SML but below $r_A$ | Reject | **Rejects positive NPV projects** |

- **Conclusion:** discount rate depends on **project risk**, **not on the company** undertaking it

#### Why Estimate Asset Cost of Capital

- Many projects are **expansions of existing business**; for these **average-risk projects** $r_A$ is the right rate
- Managers have good intuition about **relative risk** but **not absolute risk**
- $r_A$ is a **useful starting point**: easier to **add to / subtract from** it than to estimate project cost from scratch (add a **markup** for riskier projects)

## 5.5 Cost of Equity, Debt and Capital

### 5.5.1 Cost of Equity and Cost of Capital

#### Formula: Asset Cost of Capital

- Market value of firm: $V = D + E$
- Firm viewed as a **portfolio**: assets on one side; debt and equity on the other

$$r_A = r_D\frac{D}{V} + r_E\frac{E}{V}$$

- $r_A$ = asset cost of capital (also called **pretax WACC**); $r_D$ = cost of debt; $r_E$ = cost of equity
- Weights use **market values, not book values**
- **E** = shares outstanding × price per share = **market capitalization**
- **MV of debt ≈ BV of debt** (book value usable for debt); **book value of equity cannot be used** as market value

#### Ranking of Costs

$$r_E \ge r_A \ge r_D$$

- **Debt is safer than equity**: interest (income to bondholders) is paid from EBIT **before** dividends (income to shareholders)
- **Equity is a residual claim** on the firm's cash flows; it **stands behind debt**
- Hence $r_D < r_E$; $\beta_D < \beta_A < \beta_E$; $r_A$ lies **between** $r_D$ and $r_E$

| Income Statement Step | Claim |
|---|---|
| Sales − COGS = Gross Profit | — |
| Gross Profit − Operating Expenses = **EBIT** | — |
| EBIT − Interest = **EBT** | Interest → **bondholders** |
| EBT − Taxes = **PAT** | — |
| PAT → Dividends + Retained earnings | Dividends → **shareholders** |

#### Measuring Cost of Equity

$$r_E = r_f + \beta_E(r_m - r_f)$$

- **CAPM** is commonly used; $r_E$ = expected return on the firm's common stock
- $\beta_E$ = slope from **regressing stock returns on market returns**

#### Measuring Cost of Debt

| Method | Formula / Rule |
|---|---|
| Traded debt | **YTM** on debt taken as $r_D$ |
| Yield spread | $r_D = r_f + \text{Yield Spread}$; spread depends on **credit rating** (higher rating ⇒ lower spread) |
| CAPM | $r_D = r_f + \beta_D(r_m - r_f)$; $\beta_D$ from regressing bond returns on market returns (if traded) |

- Similarly, $r_A = r_f + \beta_A(r_m - r_f)$; $\beta_A$ = weighted average of $\beta_D$ and $\beta_E$
- On the SML: debt beta > 0 (slightly riskier than risk-free); **$\beta_D < \beta_A < \beta_E$** ⇒ **$r_D < r_A < r_E$**

### 5.5.2 Risk and Beta Practice

#### Worked Example

| Stock | Std. Deviation | Beta |
|---|---|---|
| Stock 1 | 12% | 0.4 |
| Stock 2 | 25% | 1.4 |
| Stock 3 | 40% | 0.6 |

| Investor Type | Relevant Risk Measure | Safest Stock |
|---|---|---|
| **Diversified** | **Beta** (only market risk remains) | Stock 1 (lowest β = 0.4) |
| **Undiversified** (all money in one stock) | **Standard deviation** (total risk) | Stock 1 (lowest σ = 12%) |

- In corporate finance, investors are **assumed diversified**, so **beta** is the relevant risk measure
- Equal-weighted portfolio beta $= \frac{1}{3}(0.4) + \frac{1}{3}(1.4) + \frac{1}{3}(0.6) = 0.8$

## 5.6 Applications of Cost of Capital

### 5.6.1 Asset Cost of Capital Problem

#### Worked Example

- Book balance sheet: 50% debt, 50% equity; debt trades near book value at 4%; **market cap = 2 × book equity**; $\beta_E = 1$; $r_f = 2\%$; MRP = 7%
- Let book assets = X ⇒ BV debt = 0.5X = MV debt; BV equity = 0.5X ⇒ MV equity = X
- $D/V = 0.5X/1.5X = 1/3$; $E/V = 2/3$
- $r_E = 2\% + 1 \times 7\% = 9\%$
- $r_A = 4\% \times \frac{1}{3} + 9\% \times \frac{2}{3} = 1.33\% + 6\% = 7.33\%$

### 5.6.2 Cost of Capital and Project Discount Rate

#### Worked Example

- E = ₹60 million, D = ₹40 million; $\beta_E = 1.5$; MRP = 6%; $r_f = 6\%$; debt risk-free ($\beta_D = 0$); no taxes

| Part | Working | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| Required return on stock | $r_E = 6\% + 1.5 \times 6\%$ | **15%** |
| Cost of debt | $r_D = 6\% + 0 \times 6\%$ | **6%** (= $r_f$) |
| Asset cost of capital | $r_A = 0.4 \times 6\% + 0.6 \times 15\%$ | **11.4%** |
| Discount rate for expansion | Same risk as firm's assets ⇒ use $r_A$ | **11.4%** |

- Discount rate depends on **project risk**; **expansion of present business** has the **same risk as the firm's assets**, so **asset cost of capital** is the right discount rate

## Quick Revision Summary

### Key Definitions

- **Risk**: variability of returns, measured by **variance / standard deviation**
- **Risk premium**: $r_i - r_f$; T-Bills are the **risk-free** asset
- **Covariance**: direction of relationship (−∞ to +∞); **Correlation**: direction + strength (−1 to +1)
- **Specific risk**: firm/industry risk, **diversifiable**; **Market risk**: economy-wide, **non-diversifiable**
- **Beta**: sensitivity of stock return to market return; measures **market (systematic) risk**
- **SML**: expected return vs beta; graphical CAPM
- **Asset cost of capital**: expected return on portfolio of all debt and equity; discount rate for **average-risk projects**

### Important Formulas

| Concept | Formula |
|---|---|
| Sample variance | $\sigma^2 = \frac{\sum (R_i - \bar{R})^2}{N-1}$ |
| Expected return (states) | $E(R) = \sum P_i R_i$ |
| Std. dev. (states) | $\sigma = [\sum P_i(R_i - \bar{R})^2]^{1/2}$ |
| Correlation | $\rho_{XY} = \sigma_{XY}/(\sigma_X\sigma_Y)$ |
| Portfolio return | $E(R_P) = \sum x_i E(R_i)$ |
| Two-stock variance | $\sigma_P^2 = x_1^2\sigma_1^2 + x_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2x_1x_2\rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2$ |
| Beta | $\beta_i = \sigma_{im}/\sigma_m^2 = \rho_{im}\sigma_i/\sigma_m$ |
| Portfolio beta | $\beta_P = \sum x_i\beta_i$ |
| CAPM | $r_i = r_f + \beta_i(r_m - r_f)$ |
| Asset cost of capital | $r_A = r_D(D/V) + r_E(E/V)$ |
| Cost of debt (spread) | $r_D = r_f + \text{Yield Spread}$ |

### Must-Remember Points

- Portfolio return = **weighted average**; portfolio σ **< weighted average σ** unless $\rho = +1$
- Portfolio σ falls as correlation falls; minimum at $\rho = -1$; zero only if $\rho = -1$ and $x_1\sigma_1 = x_2\sigma_2$
- **β market = 1**, **β risk-free = 0**; luxury β > 1, necessity 0 < β < 1, countercyclical β < 0
- **Accept** projects above SML, **reject** below; asset cost of capital rule accepts negative-NPV and rejects positive-NPV projects
- Discount rate depends on **project risk**, not the company
- Use **market value weights**; MV debt ≈ BV debt
- $r_E \ge r_A \ge r_D$ and $\beta_E \ge \beta_A \ge \beta_D$
- Diversified investor → **beta**; undiversified investor → **standard deviation**
