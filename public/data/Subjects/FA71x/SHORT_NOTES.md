### Principles of Finance

| Principle | Statement | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Time value of money** | **₹1 today > ₹1 in the future** | Money today can be **invested** to earn interest |
| **2. Risk and return** | **A safe rupee > a risky rupee**; risk and return go hand in hand | Investors are **risk-averse**; demand **extra return (premium)** for extra risk |

$$E(X) = \sum_i p_i X_i \quad (p_i = \text{probability},\ X_i = \text{outcome})$$

- **Example:** ₹1,00,000 sure vs ₹2,00,000 at 50% / ₹0 at 50% → both $E = ₹1,00,000$; risk-averse investor picks the **sure** option.
- **Indifference:** raising probability to 60% → $E = 2,00,000 \times 0.6 = ₹1,20,000$; extra ₹20,000 = **compensation for risk**.

> **CLA:** Which statement best reflects a fundamental principle of finance?
> - **Investors are generally risk-averse, so they choose a safe rupee over a risky rupee at any given point in time** ✓
>
> **CLA:** Investments A and B (mutually exclusive, value-adding) have the same outlay, life and expected return; A has substantially lower risk. A rational risk-averse investor prefers:
> - **Investment A** ✓

**CLA:** Which are correct? I. ₹1 today is worth more than ₹1 tomorrow. II. A real asset cannot be an intangible asset. III. A project adds value when expected return > opportunity cost of capital.

> *Why: I = time value of money; III = investment decision rule; II is false because real assets can be intangible (patent, reputation).*
> **Answer = I and III** ✓
> *(Real assets can be **tangible or intangible**.)*

### Investment Decision Rule and Opportunity Cost of Capital

| Alternative | Return | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Invest cash in **project** | $R_1$ | $\beta$ |
| Pay **dividend**, shareholders invest in market | $R_2$ | $\beta$ |

$$\text{Accept project if } R_1 > R_2 \quad (R_2 = \text{opportunity cost of capital})$$

- **Opportunity cost:** benefit foregone choosing one alternative over another **at the same level of risk**.
- **Opportunity cost of capital (project):** **expected return in the financial market at the same risk as the project**.
- **Higher project risk → higher opportunity cost** (stocks riskier → higher return than bonds).

$$\text{Opportunity cost} = \text{Risk-free rate} + \text{Risk premium}$$

| Project | Opportunity Cost |
|---|---|
| **Completely safe** | **Government bond rate** (risk premium = 0) |
| **Risky** | **CAPM**: risk premium rises with **beta ($\beta$)** |

> **Q:** When should a financial manager invest cash in a project rather than pay dividends?
> - **When the project's return exceeds shareholders' return on similar-risk investments** ✓
>
> **CLA:** A ₹50 lakh machine with 5-year cash flows should be undertaken if it is expected to:
> - **Increase shareholder wealth by generating returns that exceed the opportunity cost of capital** ✓

**CLA:** Company A has ₹20 lakh surplus cash. Project X is expected to earn 14%; a comparable-risk financial asset earns 9%. Most appropriate conclusion?

> *Why: the comparable-risk market return is the opportunity cost of capital; accept if project return exceeds it.*
> $\text{Opportunity cost} = 9\%$
> $R_1 = 14\% > R_2 = 9\%$
> **Answer = Accept Project X – expected return exceeds its opportunity cost of capital** ✓
> *(The 9% alternative is **NOT** irrelevant – it is the benchmark.)*

### Time Value of Money Basics

| Principle | Meaning |
|-----------|---------|
| **Time value of money** | A rupee today is worth more than a rupee tomorrow |
| **Risk–return** | A safe rupee is worth more than a risky rupee |
| **Compounding** | Moves money forward → **FV** |
| **Discounting** | Moves money backward → **PV** |
| **Compound interest** | Interest earned on interest (course default); simple interest = same interest each year |

---

### Future Value

$$FV = C_0 \times (1 + r)^t$$

- $C_0$ = amount today, $r$ = annual rate (compounded annually), $t$ = years
- **Longer t or higher r → higher FV**
- Example: ₹100 at 7% → ₹107 (Y1), ₹114.49 (Y2)

**CLA:** Ramesh deposits ₹20,000 in an FD for 4 years at 7% p.a. compounded annually. Maturity amount? (A ₹24,626 / B ₹26,616 / C ₹26,216 / D ₹25,626)

> *Why: Single sum grown forward → FV formula.*
> $FV = 20{,}000 \times 1.07^4 = 20{,}000 \times 1.3108$
> **Answer = ₹26,216 (C)** ✓

---

### Present Value, Discount Rate and Opportunity Cost

$$PV = \frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t} = C_t \times DF_t, \qquad DF_t = \frac{1}{(1+r)^t}$$

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Discount rate (r)** | Rate used to discount future cash flows |
| **Opportunity cost of capital** | Return forgone on a **similar-risk** investment in the market |
| **Discount factor ($DF_t$)** | PV of ₹1 received after t years; **inversely related** to discount rate |

- **Higher t → lower PV**; **higher r → lower PV**; **more risk → lower PV**

**CLA:** Priya receives ₹50,000 after 3 years; opportunity cost 12%. Approximate PV? (A ₹35,589 / B ₹32,000 / C ₹44,648 / D ₹30,000)

> *Why: Single future sum → discount back 3 years.*
> $PV = 50{,}000 / 1.12^3 = 50{,}000 / 1.4049$
> **Answer = ₹35,589 (A)** ✓

> **CLA:** Neha's ₹2,00,000 after 4 years valued at 8% and at 12%. Which is correct?
> - **The present value will be higher at 8%** ✓ (lower discount rate → higher PV)
>
> **CLA:** Which statements are correct? I. Compounding gives FV of amount invested today. II. Discounting gives PV of a future cash flow. III. Higher discount rate gives higher PV. IV. More risky cash flow has higher PV.
> - **I and II** ✓ (III and IV are false: higher rate/risk **lowers** PV)
>
> **CLA:** Arjun can earn 13% in a project or 9% in the financial market at the same risk. The 9% represents:
> - **The opportunity cost of capital** ✓
>
> **Q:** Role of the opportunity cost of capital in project evaluation:
> - **It reflects the return available on an investment with similar risk** ✓

### Comparing Cash Flows Across Time

| Approach | ₹100 today | ₹110 in 2 yrs (r = 10%) | Decision |
|----------|-----------|--------------------------|----------|
| **PV at t = 0** | 100 | $110/1.1^2 \approx 91$ | ₹100 today |

### PV of Multiple Cash Flows, NPV and Rate of Return

$$PV = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t} \;\;(\text{DCF; excludes } C_0) \qquad NPV = PV - \text{Initial Investment}$$

$$\text{Rate of Return} = \frac{\text{Payoff} - \text{Investment}}{\text{Investment}} \;\;(\text{single period only})$$

| Criterion | Accept if | Notes |
|-----------|-----------|-------|
| **NPV** | **NPV > 0** | Value-adding; higher r → lower NPV; inflows +, outflows − |
| **Rate of return** | **Return > opportunity cost** | **NOT** for multi-year projects |

- **DCF** values any asset: bond, share, project, company
- **Market value = PV** when markets price assets fairly
- **Higher risk → higher r → lower PV → lower NPV**
- **Max upfront investment = PV of cash flows** (NPV = 0)

| Example | Working | Result |
|---------|---------|--------|
| Office block, sure $800k, r = 7% | $-700k + 800k/1.07$ | NPV **$47,664**; return 14.3% > 7% → accept |
| Same, stock-market risk, r = 12% | $-700k + 800k/1.12$ | NPV **$14,286** |
| Option 2: rent $30k × 2 + sell $840k | $-700k + 30k/1.12 + 870k/1.12^2$ | NPV **$20,344** → prefer; max investment **$720,344** |

### Perpetuity and Shifting Cash Flows

$$PV_{\text{perpetuity}} = \frac{C}{r}$$

- Same cash flow **forever**; value is **one period before the first cash flow**
- **Shift left t periods:** ÷ $(1+r)^t$; **shift right t periods:** × $(1+r)^t$
- ₹1 bn at 10% from t = 1 → ₹10 bn; first cash at t = 4 → $10/1.1^3 =$ ₹7.51 bn

**CLA:** Mahesh gets ₹10,000 every year forever starting one year from today; r = 10%. PV? (A ₹1,00,000 / B ₹1,10,000 / C ₹1,50,000 / D ₹2,00,000)

> *Why: Equal cash flows forever starting t = 1 → perpetuity.*
> $PV = 10{,}000 / 0.10$
> **Answer = ₹1,00,000 (A)** ✓

### Annuity (Ordinary and Beginning-of-Year)

$$PV_{\text{annuity}} = \frac{C}{r}\left[1 - \frac{1}{(1+r)^t}\right] \qquad FV_{\text{annuity}} = \frac{C}{r}\left[(1+r)^t - 1\right]$$

#### Beginning-of-Year Payments
$$PV_{\text{beginning}} = PV_{\text{end}} \times (1+r)$$

| Example | Result |
|---------|--------|
| Car: $5,000 × 5 yrs at 7% | **$20,501** (annuity factor 4.100) |
| Lottery ₹12.167 m × 30 yrs at 6%, end of year | **₹167.5 m** (not ₹365 m) |
| Same, beginning of year | $167.5 \times 1.06 =$ **₹177.5 m** |

> **CLA:** Equal annual instalments of ₹2 lakh at the end of each year for 7 years represent a:
> - **Annuity** ✓

### Loan Amortisation

$$\text{Loan} = \frac{C}{r}\left[1 - \frac{1}{(1+r)^t}\right] \Rightarrow C = \frac{\text{Loan}}{\text{PVA}(r,t)}$$

| Table rule | Formula |
|------------|---------|
| Interest | Rate × Beginning balance |
| Principal | Total payment − Interest |
| Ending balance | Beginning balance − Principal |
| Outstanding balance | PV of remaining payments |

| Item | **Variant 1: Equal payments** | **Variant 2: Equal principal** |
|------|------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Interest | Declines | Declines |
| Principal | Increases | Constant |
| Total payment | Constant | Declines |
| $1,000, 4 yrs, 10% | C = **$315.47**; Y3: interest 54.75, principal 260.72, balance 286.79 | Y3: payment 300 (50 + 250), balance 250 |

- Both variants: PV of payments = loan amount ($1,000); final balance = 0

> **Q:** In a loan with equal principal repayments, why does the total payment decline over time?
> - **The interest payment declines as the outstanding balance falls** ✓

### Growing Perpetuity and Growing Annuity

| Pattern | PV | Condition |
|---------|----|-----------|
| **Growing perpetuity** | $\dfrac{C}{r-g}$ | $g < r$ (else PV infinite) |
| **Growing annuity** | $\dfrac{C}{r-g}\left[1 - \left(\dfrac{1+g}{1+r}\right)^t\right]$ | $g \ne r$ |
| **Growing annuity** | $\dfrac{C \times t}{1+r}$ | $g = r$ |

- $C$ = first cash flow at **end of Year 1**; $g$ = constant growth rate
- **Growing annuity = fixed number of periods**; growing perpetuity = forever
- Club: ₹5,000 growing 6%, 3 yrs, r = 10% → PV **₹13,146.51** > ₹12,750 lump sum → **pay lump sum**

> **Q:** Which feature distinguishes a growing annuity from a growing perpetuity?
> - **A growing annuity lasts for a fixed number of periods** ✓

**CLA:** Rahul gets ₹10,000 at end of Year 1, growing 5% each year, 5 payments; r = 10%. Approximate PV? (A ₹37,155 / B ₹40,000 / C ₹41,506 / D ₹43,294)

> *Why: Constant growth for fixed periods, g ≠ r → growing annuity.*
> $PV = \frac{10{,}000}{0.10 - 0.05}\left[1 - \left(\frac{1.05}{1.10}\right)^5\right]$
> $= 2{,}00{,}000 \times (1 - 0.7925)$
> **Answer = ₹41,506 (C)** ✓

### APR, EAR and Compounding Frequency

$$1 + EAR = \left(1 + \frac{APR}{m}\right)^m \qquad FV = C_0\left(1 + \frac{APR}{m}\right)^{mt}$$

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **APR** | Periodic rate annualised by **simple** interest; **quoted** rate |
| **EAR** | Periodic rate annualised by **compound** interest; **paid** rate |
| **m** | Compounding intervals per year (2, 4, 12, 52, 365) |
| **APR/m** | Periodic rate; **mt** = total periods |

- **m = 1 → EAR = APR**; **m > 1 → EAR > APR**; EAR rises with frequency
- 10% APR: semi-annual 10.25%, quarterly 10.38%, monthly 10.47%, daily 10.52%
- Yes Bank 6.65% quarterly → EAR 6.82% → ₹1,06,820 (**NOT** ₹1,06,650)
- $10 m at 6%, 4 yrs: annual $12.625 m; monthly $12.705 m

> **Q:** Why can EAR be higher than APR when interest is compounded more than once a year?
> - **EAR captures the effect of compounding within the year** ✓

**CLA:** APR of 6% per year compounded quarterly. Approximate EAR? (A 6.00% / B 6.08% / C 6.14% / D 6.50%)

> *Why: Convert APR to EAR using m = 4.*
> $EAR = (1 + 0.06/4)^4 - 1 = 1.015^4 - 1 = 0.06136$
> **Answer = 6.14% (C)** ✓

### Discounting Periodic Cash Flows

- Periodic cash flows → discount at **periodic rate (APR/m)** with **t = number of periods**
- $5 every 6 months for 3 yrs, 10% semi-annual: $r = 5\%$, $t = 6$ → $PV = \frac{5}{0.05}\left[1 - \frac{1}{1.05^6}\right] =$ **$25.38**

## Quick Reference: Which Formula to Use

| Situation | Action/Answer |
|-----------|---------------|
| Single sum today → future | $C_0(1+r)^t$ |
| Single future sum → today | $C_t/(1+r)^t$ |
| Uneven cash flows | Discount each, sum (DCF) |
| Equal, forever | $C/r$ |
| Equal, fixed years | $\frac{C}{r}[1 - (1+r)^{-t}]$ |
| Growing, forever ($g<r$) | $C/(r-g)$ |
| Growing, fixed years ($g \ne r$) | $\frac{C}{r-g}[1 - (\frac{1+g}{1+r})^t]$ |
| Growing, fixed years ($g = r$) | $Ct/(1+r)$ |
| Payments at beginning of year | Ordinary PV × $(1+r)$ |
| First cash flow at t = n | Formula gives value at t = n − 1; ÷ $(1+r)^{n-1}$ |
| Loan instalment | Loan ÷ annuity factor |
| Outstanding loan balance | PV of remaining instalments |
| Quoted APR with m compounding | EAR = $(1+APR/m)^m - 1$ |
| Non-annual cash flows | Rate = APR/m; periods = mt |

## Key Decision Rules Summary

| Rule | Detail |
|------|--------|
| NPV rule | Accept if NPV > 0; choose higher NPV among alternatives |
| Rate of return rule | Accept if return > opportunity cost (single-period only) |
| Opportunity cost | Return on equal-risk alternative; sure cash flow → risk-free rate |
| Risk effect | Higher risk → higher r → lower PV and NPV |
| PV vs FV comparison | Same decision either way; compare at common date |
| Costs comparison | Choose option with lower PV of payments |
| Max investment | = PV of future cash flows (NPV = 0) |
| Fair market | Market value = PV |
| Growing perpetuity | Valid only if g < r |
| Growing annuity | Use $Ct/(1+r)$ when g = r |
| APR vs EAR | EAR = APR only when m = 1; else EAR > APR |
| Equal-payment loan | Interest ↓, principal ↑, total constant |
| Equal-principal loan | Interest ↓, principal constant, total ↓ |

### Bond Basics and Bond Certificate

|------|---------|------------|

**CLA:** A bond has a face value of ₹1,000 and pays ₹75 in annual interest. What is the bond's coupon rate?

> *Why: Coupon rate is the annual coupon as a % of face value.*
> $\text{Coupon rate} = 75 / 1000$
> **Answer = 7.50%** ✓

### Bond Pricing and Yield to Maturity

$$P = \frac{C}{r}\left[1 - \frac{1}{(1+r)^t}\right] + \frac{F}{(1+r)^t}$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| $C$ | Coupon per period |
| $r$ | YTM per period (APR / m) |
| $t$ | Periods to maturity |
| $F$ | Face value |

- **PV(Bond) = PV(coupons, an annuity) + PV(face value, one-time)**
- **YTM:** bondholders' opportunity cost; higher risk → higher YTM; quoted as **APR**
- **YTM found by trial and error** when price is known (e.g., 4.25% French bond at 108.57% → YTM 2%)
- **US govt FV = $1,000; Indian govt FV = ₹100**; both pay **semiannually**; prices quoted as **% of FV**

**CLA:** A five-year U.S. government bond has a coupon rate of 8%, paid semiannually, face value $1,000. Investors demand 12% per year compounded semiannually. What is the price?

> *Why: Semiannual bond — halve coupon rate and yield, double periods.*
> $C = 8\%/2 \times 1000 = 40;\; r = 6\%;\; t = 10$
> $P = \frac{40}{0.06}\left[1 - \frac{1}{1.06^{10}}\right] + \frac{1000}{1.06^{10}} = 294.40 + 558.39$
> **Answer = $852.80** ✓
> *(Coupon 8% < YTM 12% → discount bond, price < 1,000)*

**CLA:** A 15-year corporate bond, face value ₹1,000, coupon 6% paid semiannually, YTM 5% per year compounded semiannually. Approximate price?

> *Why: Standard bond pricing with semiannual periods.*
> $C = 30;\; r = 2.5\%;\; t = 30$
> $P = \frac{30}{0.025}\left[1 - \frac{1}{1.025^{30}}\right] + \frac{1000}{1.025^{30}} = 627.92 + 476.74$
> **Answer = ₹1,104.65** ✓
> *(Coupon 6% > YTM 5% → premium bond)*

**CLA:** A 4-year bond, 9% annual coupon, face value ₹1,000. At end of Year 2 (2 years left), YTM falls to 7% compounded annually. Approximate price at end of Year 2?

> *Why: Price uses remaining 2 years and the new YTM.*
> $P = \frac{90}{1.07} + \frac{1090}{1.07^2} = 84.11 + 952.05$
> **Answer = ₹1,036.16** ✓

**CLA:** Yield to maturity is the return that investors expect from buying a bond today and holding it till maturity.

> - **True** ✓ *(HPR is the realised return; YTM is NOT historical)*

### Interest Rate Risk

- **Interest rate risk:** sensitivity of bond prices to changes in interest rates
- **Price and YTM move in opposite directions**
- **Longer maturity → greater interest rate risk** (30-yr zero > 3-yr zero); distant CFs more sensitive to discount rate
- **Flight to safety** in crisis → govt bond demand up → yields down, prices up
- Rates rise → **coupon rate and FV unchanged**, YTM up, price down

**CLA:** The sensitivity of bond prices to changes in interest rates is called _____________.
> - **Interest rate risk** ✓

**CLA:** A 10-year bond, face value ₹1,000, coupon ₹50 per year. If interest rates increase shortly after issue, YTM will (a)____ and price will (b)____.
> - (a) **Increase**, (b) **Decrease** ✓

### Holding Period Return and Yield Changes

$$\text{Rate of Return} = \frac{\text{Coupon Income} + (P_1 - P_0)}{P_0}$$

| Measure | Nature |
|---------|--------|
| **HPR / rate of return** | Realised, **backward-looking** |
| **YTM** | Expected, **forward-looking** |

| YTM over period | Realised return vs initial YTM |
|-----------------|-------------------------------|
| **Falls** | **Higher** |
| **Unchanged** | **Equal** |
| **Rises** | **Lower** |

- Lecture: 4.375s of 2038 in 2008 crisis → (21.875 + 1380.50 − 963.80)/963.80 = **45.5%** in 7 months
- Lecture: 6-yr 5% bond, YTM 3% → 2%: P0 = 110.834, P1 = 114.140 → **7.49%**; YTM stays 3% → **3.00%**

**CLA:** Meera buys a bond for ₹980, receives ₹90 coupon after one year and sells it for ₹1,020. Rate of return for the year?

> *Why: HPR = (coupon + price change) / initial price.*
> $\frac{90 + (1020 - 980)}{980} = \frac{130}{980}$
> **Answer = 13.27%** ✓

**Q:** A six-year government bond, face value ₹1,000, 6% annual coupon, YTM 4% at issuance. One year later yield falls to 3%. What return did the bondholder earn during the year?

> *Why: Price today at 4% (6 yrs), price after 1 yr at 3% (5 yrs), then HPR.*
> $P_0 = \frac{60}{0.04}\left[1-\frac{1}{1.04^6}\right] + \frac{1000}{1.04^6} = 1{,}104.84$
> $P_1 = \frac{60}{0.03}\left[1-\frac{1}{1.03^5}\right] + \frac{1000}{1.03^5} = 1{,}137.39$
> $\text{Return} = \frac{60 + (1137.39 - 1104.84)}{1104.84}$
> **Answer = 8.38%** ✓
> *(YTM fell → return 8.38% > initial YTM 4%)*

**CLA:** If YTM on a bond decreases over a period, the rate of return over the period will be higher than the initial YTM.
> - **True** ✓ *(It is NOT lower — falling YTM raises price)*

### Nominal and Real Cash Flows and Rates

$$C_{real,t} = \frac{C_{nominal,t}}{(1+i)^t}$$
$$1 + r_{real} = \frac{1 + r_{nominal}}{1+i} \qquad r_{real} \approx r_{nominal} - i$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| $i$ | Expected inflation rate (change in **CPI**) |
| $r_{nominal}$ | Nominal rate (market-quoted) |
| $r_{real}$ | Real rate |

- Example: 10% nominal, 7% inflation → **2.8% real**; ₹110 nominal = **₹102.80** real
- Lecture: 12% nominal, 8% real → inflation **3.70%** (precise), **4%** (approx.)

### Stock Valuation by Fundamentals (DDM)

$$P_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{H}\frac{Div_t}{(1+r)^t} + \frac{P_H}{(1+r)^H} \;\xrightarrow{H\to\infty}\; P_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{Div_t}{(1+r)^t}$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| $r$ | **Cost of equity** (required return; market capitalisation rate) |
| $P_H$ | **Terminal / horizon value** |

| Stock Type | Formula |
|------------|---------|
| **Zero growth** | $P_0 = Div_1 / r$ |
| **Constant growth** | $P_0 = Div_1/(r-g)$, **g < r** |
| **Differential growth** | PV growing annuity (g1) + PV of perpetuity/growing perpetuity (g2) discounted back |

- Lecture: XYZ (3, 3.24, 3.50 + 94.48 at 12%) → **$75**
- Lecture (r = 10%): Stock A $10 forever → **$100**; Stock B Div1 5, g 4% → **$83.33**; Stock C 20% for yrs 2–6 then 0 → **$104.50**
- **Just paid** dividend → ignore (1.35, g 2.75%, r 9.5% → **$20.55**); **about to pay** → add it (**$21.90**)
- **Challenges:** dividends far in future (Amazon); small change in g → large price change; irregular **share repurchases**

**Q:** According to the Dividend Discount Model, the current value of a stock is based on the present value of:
> - **All expected future dividends** ✓

**CLA:** A stock is expected to pay ₹7 every year forever. Cost of equity 14%. Present value?

> *Why: Zero-growth stock = perpetuity.*
> $P_0 = 7 / 0.14$
> **Answer = ₹50** ✓

**CLA:** A company will pay a dividend of ₹6 one year from today, growing at 4% per year; cost of equity 10%. Present value of the stock?

> *Why: Constant growth with g < r → growing perpetuity.*
> $P_0 = \frac{6}{0.10 - 0.04}$
> **Answer = ₹100** ✓

**Q:** Bright Co. will pay ₹5 after a year; dividends then grow 15% per year for five years (years 2–6) and 5% forever thereafter. Cost of equity 10%. Value today?

> *Why: Differential growth — PV of years 1–6 dividends + PV of terminal value at year 6.*
> $D_1 = 5,\; D_6 = 5 \times 1.15^5 = 10.06$
> $\text{PV}(D_1..D_6) = \frac{5}{0.10-0.15}\left[1 - \left(\frac{1.15}{1.10}\right)^6\right] = 34.30$
> $P_6 = \frac{D_7}{0.10-0.05} = \frac{10.56}{0.05} = 211.2;\; \text{PV} = \frac{211.2}{1.1^6} = 119.22$
> $P_0 = 34.30 + 119.22$
> **Answer = ₹149.78** ✓
> *(Growing annuity formula works even when g1 > r; terminal growth g2 must be < r)*

### Capital Budgeting and Sound Investment Rules

- **Capital budgeting:** Process of evaluating potential investment projects (accept/reject)
- **Goal:** Accept projects that **increase shareholder wealth**

| A rule is inferior if it... | Reason |
|------|--------|
| **Ignores time value of money** | Time has money value |
| **Depends on managers' tastes** | Irrelevant to value addition |

| **Depends on existing business profitability** | Irrelevant to project value |

| CFO survey (Graham and Harvey) | Usage |
|------|------|
| **IRR** | 76% |
| **NPV** | 75% |
| **Payback** | 57% |
| Book rate of return | 20% |

### Net Present Value (NPV)

$$NPV = PV - \text{Initial Outlay} = C_0 + \sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t}$$

- $C_t$ = cash flow at year $t$; $r$ = **opportunity cost of capital (OCC)**
- **Rule:** Accept if **NPV > 0**
- **Adding-up property:** $NPV(A+B) = NPV(A) + NPV(B)$ — **only NPV** has it

| Advantages | Disadvantage |
|------|------|
| Recognises **time value of money** | **Harder to communicate** to a layperson |
| **Clear benchmark** (0) | |
| **Not affected by accounting method** (uses cash flows) | |

> **CLA:** Which statement about NPV is NOT correct?
> - **"NPV of a project is affected by the company's choice of accounting method" is NOT correct** ✓ *(NPV uses cash flows, so it is not affected by accounting method)*

**CLA:** A project requires ₹8,00,000 today; year-end cash flows ₹3,00,000, ₹3,00,000, ₹4,00,000 (Years 1–3). OCC = 10%. Approximate NPV?

> *Why: NPV = sum of discounted cash flows minus initial outlay.*
> $\frac{3,00,000}{1.1} = 2,72,727$; $\frac{3,00,000}{1.1^2} = 2,47,934$; $\frac{4,00,000}{1.1^3} = 3,00,526$
> $PV = 8,21,187$
> $NPV = 8,21,187 - 8,00,000$
> **Answer = ₹21,187.08** ✓

---

### Payback Period

- **Definition:** Years to **recover initial investment** (undiscounted)
- **Rule:** Accept if payback ≤ **cutoff set by management**

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|------|------|
| **Easy** to understand and calculate | **Arbitrary cutoff** (no clear benchmark) |
| **Biased towards liquidity** | **Ignores cash flows after cutoff** (biased against long-term projects) |
| | **Ignores time value of money** (equal weight before cutoff) |

| Project (OCC 10%) | C0 | C1 | C2 | C3 | Payback | NPV |
|------|----|----|----|----|----|----|
| A | −2,000 | 500 | 500 | 5,000 | 3 | +2,624 |
| B | −2,000 | 500 | 1,800 | 0 | 2 | −58 |
| C | −2,000 | 1,800 | 500 | 0 | 2 | +50 |

- Cutoff 2 yrs: payback **rejects A** (good) and **accepts B** (bad)

> **Q:** Technique measuring the number of years required to recover the initial investment?
> - **Payback period** ✓
>
> **CLA:** Which capital budgeting method does NOT take into account time value of money?
> - **Payback Period** ✓
>
> **CLA:** Relationship between Payback Period and NPV?
> - **A project may have a short Payback Period, but its NPV can still be negative** ✓ *(payback ignores time value and cash flows after cutoff)*

**CLA:** Investment ₹10,00,000 today; inflows ₹2,00,000, ₹4,00,000, ₹4,00,000, ₹5,00,000 (Years 1–4). Payback period?

> *Why: Add undiscounted inflows until they equal the outlay.*
> $2L + 4L + 4L = 10L$ by end of Year 3
> **Answer = 3 years** ✓

**CLA:** Project X: −₹10L, +6L, +6L. Project Y: −₹10L, +1L, +1L, +15L. OCC = 10%. Which is most likely true?

> *Why: Payback ignores large later cash flows; NPV captures them.*
> X payback $= 1 + 4/6 = 1.67$ yrs; $NPV_X = -10 + 5.455 + 4.959 = 0.41L$
> Y payback = 3 yrs; $NPV_Y = -10 + 0.909 + 0.826 + 11.270 = 3.01L$
> **Answer = X has a shorter Payback Period, but Y has a higher NPV** ✓

### Discounted Payback Period

- **Definition:** Years to recover initial investment **in discounted terms**
- **Rule:** Accept if discounted payback ≤ management cutoff

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|------|------|
| **Never accepts a negative NPV project** (its DPP is **not defined**) | **Arbitrary cutoff** |
| Considers **time value of money** | **Ignores cash flows after cutoff** |
| Biased towards liquidity | |

| Project (PVs @10%) | PV C1 | PV C2 | PV C3 | DPP | NPV |

### Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

$$0 = C_0 + \sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{C_t}{(1+IRR)^t}$$

- **Rule:** Accept if **IRR > OCC** (investing/lending projects)
- **"Internal":** needs only **magnitude and timing of project cash flows**; OCC **NOT** needed to compute, only to decide
- Example: −4,000, +2,000, +4,000 → **IRR = 28.08%**

| Condition | NPV |
|------|------|
| IRR > OCC | **+** |
| IRR = OCC | **0** |
| IRR < OCC | **−** |

- NPV and IRR agree when **NPV declines continuously with discount rate**

| IRR | Opportunity Cost of Capital |
|------|------|
| **Profitability measure** | **Expected market return at same risk** |
| Depends on amount and timing of cash flows | Depends on **project risk** |

| IRR Advantages | IRR Disadvantages |
|------|------|
| Time value of money | **No IRR** may exist |
| Clear benchmark (OCC) | **Multiple IRRs** |
| **Easy to communicate** | Cannot distinguish **investing vs borrowing** |
| | Unreliable for **mutually exclusive** projects |

- **Why popular:** IRR can be computed **without knowing OCC**
- **Excel:** `=IRR(values, [guess])`; default guess **10%**; **vary the guess** to find multiple IRRs

> **Q:** Discount rate that makes a project's NPV equal to zero?
> - **Internal Rate of Return (IRR)** ✓

### Pitfalls of IRR

| Pitfall | Example | Key point |
|------|------|------|
| **No IRR** | F: −1,000, +3,000, −2,500 (NPV −339) | NPV negative (or positive) at **all** rates |
| **Multiple IRRs** | G: −30, +10 (Y1–9), −65 (Y10) | **2 sign changes** → IRRs **3.5% and 19.54%**; accept if OCC between them |
| **Borrowing** | E: +1,000, −1,500 (IRR 50%, NPV −364) | For borrowing (**+ − −**), accept if **IRR < OCC** |

- **Descartes' rule:** number of positive real roots ≤ **number of sign changes** (necessary, **NOT** sufficient)
- **Insurance sale** resembles borrowing (premium now, claims later)
- Lending: NPV **falls** as rate rises; Borrowing: NPV **rises** as rate rises

> **CLA:** Which statement about multiple IRRs is incorrect?
> - **"Multiple IRRs occur only when a project has a negative NPV" is incorrect** ✓ *(multiple IRRs come from sign changes in cash flows)*

### Mutually Exclusive Projects and Incremental IRR

| Project type | Meaning | Decision |
|------|------|------|
| **Independent** | A or B or both | Accept all meeting criteria |
| **Dependent** | B only if A | — |
| **Mutually exclusive** | A or B, **NOT** both | **Rank; choose highest NPV** |

| Case | Projects | IRR says | NPV says | Incremental IRR |
|------|------|------|------|------|
| **Scale differs** | H (−10k, +20k) vs I (−20k, +35k) | H (100% vs 75%) | **I** (11,818 vs 8,182) | I−H = **50%** > 10% → **I** |
| **Life differs** | J (3 yrs) vs K (perpetual 1,800) | J (33% vs 20%) | **K** (9,000 vs 3,592) | K−J = **15.6%** > 10% → **K** |

- Incremental flows: subtract so **first cash flow is negative**
- Valid only if projects have **same risk / same OCC**; incremental flows can still have no/multiple IRRs
- **Always prefer NPV**

#### Simple Enterprises (OCC 9%, $000)
- A: −400, 250, 300 (IRR 23%, NPV 81.86); B: −200, 140, 179 (IRR 36%, NPV 79.10)
- A−B: −200, 110, 121 → $-200x^2 + 110x + 121 = 0$, $x = 1.1$ → **IRR = 10% > 9% → choose A**

> **Q:** When mutually exclusive projects differ in scale, which cash flows adapt the IRR rule?
> - **Incremental cash flows** ✓
>
> **CLA:** A: IRR 22%, NPV ₹10L; B: IRR 18%, NPV ₹16L; mutually exclusive. Prefer?
> - **Project B, because its NPV is higher** ✓

**CLA:** Mutually exclusive projects, OCC 8%. A: −8,00,000, +4,82,000, +5,86,000 (IRR 21%). B: −4,00,000, +2,62,000, +3,44,000 (IRR 31%). Choose using IRR on incremental cash flows.

> *Why: Scale differs, so compare incremental IRR (A − B, first flow negative) with OCC.*
> $A - B$: $-4,00,000$; $+2,20,000$; $+2,42,000$
> Test 10%: $-4,00,000 + \frac{2,20,000}{1.1} + \frac{2,42,000}{1.21} = -4,00,000 + 2,00,000 + 2,00,000 = 0$
> Incremental IRR = 10% > 8%
> **Answer = Project A** ✓
> *(Higher stand-alone IRR of B is misleading for mutually exclusive projects)*

### Net Cash Flow and Operating Cash Flow

- **Rule 1:** Cash flow **≠** accounting income / PAT

| Approach | Formula |
|------|------|
| **NCF – Direct** | Cash Inflow − Cash Outflow |
| **NCF – Indirect** | OCF + CF from capital investment and disposal + CF from change in WC |
| **OCF – Bottom-up** | PAT + Depreciation |
| **OCF – Top-down** | Sales − Cash Expenses − Taxes |
| **OCF – Tax shield** | $(Sales - Cash\ Exp)(1-T) + Dep \times T$ |

- **Depreciation:** non-cash but **tax-deductible**; added back to PAT
- **Depreciation tax shield** = $Dep \times T$ = reduction in taxes due to depreciation
- **SLM depreciation** = (Cost − Salvage) / Life
- **After-tax salvage** = $SV - T(SV - BV)$
- **Negative tax** on project loss = **tax saving** (inflow) if losses offset other income

> **Q:** Term for the reduction in taxes resulting from depreciation expense?
> - **Depreciation tax shield** ✓

**CLA:** Machine ₹10,00,000; life 5 yrs; salvage ₹1,00,000 (SLM); revenue ₹6,00,000 and cash expenses ₹2,00,000 per year; tax 30%; sold at book value ₹1,00,000 in Year 5; OCC 10%; no inventory. Net cash flow at end of Year 1?

> *Why: Use the tax shield approach for OCF.*
> $Dep = \frac{10,00,000 - 1,00,000}{5} = 1,80,000$
> $OCF = (6,00,000 - 2,00,000)(1 - 0.3) + 0.3 \times 1,80,000 = 2,80,000 + 54,000$
> **Answer = ₹3,34,000** ✓

**CLA:** Same machine — NPV of the project?

> *Why: Constant OCF is an annuity; add PV of salvage (no tax as sale price = book value).*
> 📋 **Annuity Factor:** PVA(10%, 5) = 3.7908
> $PV_{OCF} = 3,34,000 \times 3.7908 = 12,66,138$
> $PV_{salvage} = \frac{1,00,000}{1.1^5} = 62,092$
> $NPV = -10,00,000 + 12,66,138 + 62,092$
> **Answer = ₹3,28,214.91** ✓

### Working Capital

$$NWC = Inventory + AR - AP \qquad CF_{\Delta NWC} = -(NWC_t - NWC_{t-1})$$

| Event | Cash flow |
|------|------|
| Initial investment in WC | **Outflow** |
| Increase in NWC | **Outflow** |
| No change in NWC | **None** |
| Decrease in NWC | **Inflow** |
| Recovery at project end | **Inflow** |

- **Cash cycle:** from cash paid to suppliers to cash collected from customers; NWC = cash tied up in operations
- Higher inventory / higher AR → **outflow**; higher AP → **inflow**

> **CLA:** Which activities give an incremental cash inflow? (I) Inventory up (II) Inventory down (III) AP up (IV) AR up
> - **II and III** ✓ *(lower inventory and higher payables release cash)*

**CLA:** Four-year project — Inventory: 80,000 / 1,00,000 / 1,20,000 / 1,10,000 / 90,000; AR: 50,000 / 60,000 / 70,000 / 65,000 / 40,000; AP: 30,000 / 40,000 / 50,000 / 45,000 / 35,000 (Years 0–4). NWC fully recovered at end of Year 4. Cash flow from change in NWC at end of Year 1?

> *Why: Cash flow = negative of change in NWC.*
> $NWC_0 = 80,000 + 50,000 - 30,000 = 1,00,000$
> $NWC_1 = 1,00,000 + 60,000 - 40,000 = 1,20,000$
> $\Delta NWC = +20,000$ → outflow
> **Answer = −₹20,000** ✓

### Incremental Cash Flow Rules

| Item | Treatment |
|------|------|
| **Capex** | Outflow |
| **Salvage (net of tax)** | Inflow |
| **Opportunity cost of existing resource** | Outflow — **market price** (perpetual) / **market rent** (finite life) |
| **Positive incidental effect** | Inflow |
| **Negative incidental effect** (cannibalisation, lost earnings from executive time) | Outflow |
| **After-sales flows** (service, spares) | Inflow |
| **Allocated existing overheads** (CEO salary, president's jet) | **NOT** a cash flow (only extra overheads count) |
| **Sunk costs** (access road built last year, design spend) | **Ignore** |
| **Depreciation itself** | **NOT** a cash flow |
| **Tax saving from depreciation / project losses** | Inflow |
| **Debt proceeds, interest, principal** | **Ignore** (separate financing) |

| Rule | Statement |
|------|------|
| **Rule 1** | Cash flow ≠ accounting income |
| **Rule 2** | Estimate cash flows **incrementally** |
| **Rule 3** | Treat **inflation consistently** |
| **Rule 4** | **Separate investment and financing** (assume all-equity) |
| **Rule 5** | Forecast cash flows **after taxes** |

> **Q:** Cost already incurred and unrecoverable whether or not the project is accepted?
> - **Sunk cost** ✓
>
> **CLA:** Building could be rented for ₹4,00,000/yr; to be used in a 5-year project; market price ₹50,00,000. Treatment?
> - **Rental income of ₹4,00,000 per year over the project life is an incremental cash outflow (opportunity cost)** ✓ *(finite-life project → market rent, NOT market price)*

### Inflation and NPV

$$\text{Nominal CF}_t = \text{Real CF}_t (1+i)^t \qquad r_{real} = \frac{1+r_{nom}}{1+i} - 1$$

| Cash flows | Discount at |
|------|------|
| Nominal | **Nominal rate** |
| Real | **Real rate** |

- Both give the **same NPV**; never mix
- Market rates are usually **nominal**
- Example: −100; real 35, 50, 30; nominal 15%; inflation 10% → $r_{real} = 4.5\%$ → **NPV ≈ ₹5.5** (both methods)

### Risk and Its Measurement

- **Risk:** Variability of returns
- **Measures:** **Variance** and **standard deviation** (σ ≥ 0, same units as variable)

$$\sigma^2 = \frac{\sum (R_i - \bar{R})^2}{N-1} \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2}$$

- $R_i$ = actual return; $\bar{R}$ = mean return; $N$ = observations (divide by **N − 1** for a sample)
- Example: returns 15, 9, 6, 12 → mean 10.5%, sum of squared deviations 45, variance 15, **σ = 3.873%**

> **Q:** Which measure is used to assess the risk of a security?
> - **Standard deviation** ✓

**CLA:** A stock earned 18%, 12%, 8%, 15%, 7% over five years. Find mean (a), variance (b), standard deviation (c).

> *Why: Historical data is a sample, so divide squared deviations by N − 1.*
> $\bar{R} = 60/5 = 12\%$
> $\text{Squared deviations} = 36 + 0 + 16 + 9 + 25 = 86$
> $\sigma^2 = 86/(5-1) = 21.5$
> $\sigma = \sqrt{21.5} = 4.64\%$
> **Answer = a: 12%, b: 21.5, c: 4.64%** ✓
> *(Dividing by N gives 17.2 — wrong for sample data)*

|---|---|---|---|---|
| **T-Bills** | 2.7% | 3.4% | 0 | < 1 yr, no default, stable prices → **risk-free** |
| **T-Bonds** | 9.3% | 5.0% | 1.6% | 1–10 yr, **interest rate risk** |
| **Common Stocks** | 19.6% | 11.5% | 8.1% | Most variable |

$$\text{Risk Premium}_i = r_i - r_f \qquad \text{Return} = r_f + \text{Risk Premium}$$

- Higher variability → **higher average return**

### Security Risk and Return (States of Economy)

$$E(R) = \sum P_i R_i \qquad \sigma = \left[\sum P_i (R_i - \bar{R})^2\right]^{1/2}$$

- $P_i$ = probability of state $i$; $R_i$ = return in state $i$; states are **mutually exclusive and exhaustive**
- Example: A (28, 15, −2) and B (10, 13, 10) with p = 0.25/0.5/0.25 → $E(R_A) = 14\%$, $\sigma_A = 10.65\%$; $E(R_B) = 11.5\%$, $\sigma_B = 1.50\%$

**CLA:** Boom (p 0.20) 20%, Normal (p 0.50) 12%, Recession (p 0.30) 4%. What is the expected return of the stock?

> *Why: Expected return is the probability-weighted sum of state returns.*
> $0.2(20) + 0.5(12) + 0.3(4) = 4 + 6 + 1.2$
> **Answer = 11.2%** ✓

**CLA:** Boom (p 0.25): A 24%, B 18%; Normal (p 0.50): A 12%, B 10%; Recession (p 0.25): A −8%, B 2%. Calculate the expected return on Security A.

> *Why: Probability-weight each state return.*
> $0.25(24) + 0.5(12) + 0.25(-8) = 6 + 6 - 2$
> **Answer = 10%** ✓

**CLA:** Same data as above. Calculate the standard deviation of Security B using the probability weights of the states.

> *Why: Use probability-weighted squared deviations from expected return.*
> $E(R_B) = 0.25(18) + 0.5(10) + 0.25(2) = 10\%$
> $\sigma^2 = 0.25(18-10)^2 + 0.5(0)^2 + 0.25(2-10)^2 = 16 + 0 + 16 = 32$
> $\sigma = \sqrt{32}$
> **Answer = 5.66%** ✓
> *(No N − 1 here: probabilities already act as weights)*

### Portfolio Return and Diversification Benefit

$$E(R_P) = \sum x_i E(R_i)$$

- $x_i$ = weight of security $i$
- Equal A/B example: $E(R_P) = 12.75\%$ = weighted average; **σ_P = 5.45% < weighted avg σ 6.075%**
- Portfolio σ **<** weighted average of σ's → **benefit of diversification**

**CLA:** 60% in Stock A (15%) and 40% in Stock B (9%). What is the expected portfolio return?

> *Why: Portfolio expected return is the weighted average of security returns.*
> $0.6(15) + 0.4(9) = 9 + 3.6$
> **Answer = 11.4%** ✓

> **CLA:** The portfolio standard deviation is usually less than the weighted average of individual security standard deviations.
> - **True** ✓

### Covariance, Correlation and Portfolio Variance

| Measure | Formula | Range | Tells |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Covariance** | $\sigma_{XY} = \frac{\sum(X_i-\bar{X})(Y_i-\bar{Y})}{N-1}$ | −∞ to +∞ | **Direction** only |
| **Correlation** | $\rho_{XY} = \frac{\sigma_{XY}}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}$ | −1 to +1 | **Direction + strength** |

- $\sigma_{XX} = \sigma_X^2$ (covariance with itself = variance); both capture **only linear** relationships

$$\sigma_P^2 = x_1^2\sigma_1^2 + x_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2x_1x_2\rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2$$

- 2×2 matrix: diagonal = variance terms, off-diagonal = covariance terms

| Case | Portfolio σ |
|---|---|
| $\rho = +1$ | $x_1\sigma_1 + x_2\sigma_2$ (= weighted average, **no** benefit) |
| $\rho = -1$ | $\lvert x_1\sigma_1 - x_2\sigma_2\rvert$ (minimum) |
| $\rho = -1$ and $x_1\sigma_1 = x_2\sigma_2$ | **0** (almost impossible in real life) |

- Example ($55/$45, σ 17.1%/20.8%, ρ 0.65): σ_P = **17.04%**, E(R_P) = 14.5%
- σ_P at ρ = 1, 0.8, 0.5, 0, −1 → 18.77%, 17.80%, 16.25%, 13.27%, 0.04%
- Adding Stock 3 (25%, σ 22%, ρ 0.5, equal weight) → return **19.75%**, σ **16.95%** (lower risk, higher return)
- **Few stocks** give most of the diversification benefit; works because **ρ < 1**

> **Q:** What happens to portfolio risk when the correlation between two stocks decreases?
> - **It decreases** ✓

**CLA:** 60% in A (σ 20%) and 40% in B (σ 10%); correlation 0.25. Calculate the portfolio standard deviation.

> *Why: Two-stock portfolio variance formula with correlation term.*
> $\sigma_P^2 = 0.36(400) + 0.16(100) + 2(0.6)(0.4)(0.25)(20)(10)$
> $= 144 + 16 + 24 = 184$
> $\sigma_P = \sqrt{184}$
> **Answer = 13.56%** ✓
> *(Less than weighted average 16% because ρ < 1)*

### Beta: Measuring Market Risk

$$\beta_i = \frac{\sigma_{im}}{\sigma_m^2} = \frac{\rho_{im}\sigma_i}{\sigma_m} \qquad \beta_P = \sum x_i\beta_i$$

- $\sigma_{im}$ = covariance with market; $\sigma_m^2$ = market variance; $\rho_{im}$ = correlation with market
- Beta = **slope of regression** of stock returns on market returns (market proxy: **SENSEX / NIFTY 50**)

| Asset / Company | Beta |
|---|---|
| **Market** | **1** |
| **Risk-free (T-Bill)** | **0** |
| **Luxury goods** (BMW, cyclical) | **> 1** |
| **Necessity goods** (FMCG, tobacco) | **0 to 1** |
| **Countercyclical** (insolvency practice) | **< 0** |

- Risk of a well-diversified portfolio is **proportional to portfolio beta**

> **CLA:** Which statement about beta is correct?
> - **Beta measures the systematic risk** ✓

> **CLA:** Which of the following companies' stock would generally have a beta of more than 1?

### CAPM and Security Market Line

$$r_i = r_f + \beta_i(r_m - r_f) \qquad r_i - r_f = \beta_i(r_m - r_f)$$

- $r_f$ = risk-free rate; $r_m$ = market return; $(r_m - r_f)$ = **market risk premium**; $\beta_i$ = security beta
- Given by **Sharpe, Lintner, Treynor**; risk premium **varies in direct proportion to beta**
- **SML:** expected return vs beta; intercept $r_f$, slope $(r_m - r_f)$; passes through (0, $r_f$) and (1, $r_m$)

> **Q:** According to CAPM, what determines a security's expected risk premium?
> - **Beta** ✓

**CLA:** T-bill yield (risk-free) is 4%; market index expected return is 11%. What is the expected market risk premium?

> *Why: Market risk premium = market return minus risk-free rate.*
> $r_m - r_f = 11\% - 4\%$
> **Answer = 7%** ✓

**CLA:** A stock has a beta of 1.5. The risk-free rate is 3% and the market risk premium is 6%. What is the stock's expected return?

> *Why: Apply CAPM directly.*
> $r = 3\% + 1.5 \times 6\% = 3\% + 9\%$
> **Answer = 12%** ✓

### Asset vs Project Cost of Capital

| Concept | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Asset cost of capital** | Expected return on all of firm's debt + equity; = $r_E$ for all-equity firm (Infosys); also **company cost of capital** |
| Use | Discount rate for **average-risk projects** (expansion of existing business) |
| **Project above SML** | Return > required → **positive NPV → Accept** |
| **Project below SML** | Return < required → **negative NPV → Reject** |
| **Asset cost of capital rule** | Accepts some **negative NPV** (high β) and rejects some **positive NPV** (low β) projects |

- Discount rate depends on **project risk**, **NOT** the company
- $r_A$ = useful **starting point**; managers know **relative** risk, not absolute — add/subtract a markup

> **Q:** Which discount rate is appropriate for a firm's average-risk projects?

### Cost of Equity, Cost of Debt and Asset Cost of Capital

$$r_A = r_D\frac{D}{V} + r_E\frac{E}{V} \qquad V = D + E$$

#### Estimating Components
$$r_E = r_f + \beta_E(r_m - r_f)$$
$$r_D = \text{YTM (if traded)} \;\;|\;\; r_f + \text{Yield Spread} \;\;|\;\; r_f + \beta_D(r_m - r_f)$$

- $r_A$ = pretax WACC; weights at **market value** (E = shares × price = market cap; **MV debt ≈ BV debt**)
- Yield spread depends on **credit rating** (higher rating → lower spread)
- **$r_E \ge r_A \ge r_D$** and $\beta_E \ge \beta_A \ge \beta_D$: debt is safer; **equity is a residual claim** standing behind debt (interest paid before dividends)
- Example: book 50/50, market equity = 2× book, $r_D$ 4%, β_E 1, $r_f$ 2%, MRP 7% → $r_E$ 9%, D/V 1/3 → **$r_A$ = 7.33%**
- Example: E ₹60m, D ₹40m, β_E 1.5, MRP 6%, $r_f$ 6%, β_D 0 → $r_E$ 15%, $r_D$ 6%, **$r_A$ = 11.4%** = discount rate for expansion

**CLA:** Market value of equity ₹80m, debt ₹20m; equity beta 1.25; market risk premium 8%; risk-free rate 4%; cost of debt 6%; no taxes. What is the required return on the company's stock?

> *Why: Cost of equity via CAPM.*
> $r_E = 4\% + 1.25 \times 8\% = 4\% + 10\%$
> **Answer = 14%** ✓

**CLA:** Same data. What is the company's asset cost of capital?

> *Why: Market-value-weighted average of cost of debt and equity.*
> $E/V = 80/100 = 0.8;\; D/V = 0.2$
> $r_A = 0.8 \times 14\% + 0.2 \times 6\% = 11.2\% + 1.2\%$
> **Answer = 12.4%** ✓
> *(r_A lies between r_D = 6% and r_E = 14%)*

## Quick Reference: Which Measure / Rate to Use

| Situation | Action/Answer |
|-----------|---------------|
| Historical sample returns | Variance with **N − 1**; σ = √variance |
| Returns by economic state | $E(R) = \sum P_iR_i$; σ with probability weights |
| Portfolio expected return | Weighted average of returns |
| Portfolio σ (two stocks) | $x_1^2\sigma_1^2 + x_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2x_1x_2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2$, then √ |
| Portfolio beta | Weighted average of betas |
| Risk for diversified investor | **Beta** |
| Risk for undiversified investor | **Standard deviation** |
| Required return / cost of equity | **CAPM** |
| Cost of debt (non-traded) | $r_f$ + yield spread (credit rating) |
| Average-risk project / expansion | **Asset cost of capital** |
| Project with different risk | Project beta on **SML** |
| Weights in $r_A$ | **Market values** (MV debt ≈ BV debt) |

## Key Decision Rules Summary

| Rule | Detail |
|------|--------|
| Risk-return | Higher σ → higher average return (T-Bills < T-Bonds < Stocks) |
| Diversification | Portfolio σ < weighted avg σ whenever ρ < 1 |
| Correlation effect | Lower ρ → lower portfolio risk; equality only at ρ = +1 |
| Diversifiable risk | Only **specific** risk; market risk stays |
| Beta benchmarks | Market = 1, risk-free = 0, luxury > 1, necessity 0–1, countercyclical < 0 |
| CAPM | $r = r_f + \beta(r_m - r_f)$; MRP = $r_m - r_f$ |
| SML decision | Above SML → accept; below SML → reject |
| Discount rate | Depends on **project risk**, **NOT** company |
| Asset cost of capital rule | Wrongly accepts negative NPV and rejects positive NPV projects |
| Cost ranking | $r_E \ge r_A \ge r_D$ |

## MODULE 6: Corporate Payout Decisions

---

### Payout Policy and Uses of Free Cash Flow

| Choice | Options |
|---|---|
| **Retain** | Invest in projects (positive / zero / negative NPV); retire debt; increase cash reserves |
| **Pay out** | Cash dividend; share repurchase |

- **Payout policy:** Choice between **retaining cash, paying dividends, or repurchasing shares**
- **Non-cash payout:** **Stock dividend**; related actions: **stock split**, **reverse split**
- **Infosys (July 2019):** payout raised from up to 70% to **85% of free cash flow** (FY2019-20)

> **Q:** The choice a firm makes between retaining cash, paying dividends, or repurchasing shares is called its ______.
> - **Payout policy** ✓

---

### Cash Dividends

| Form | Formula |
|---|---|
| **Dividend per share** | DPS |
| **Dividend payout** | $\dfrac{\text{DPS}}{\text{EPS}}$ |
| **Dividend yield** | $\dfrac{\text{DPS}}{\text{Price per share}}$ |

| Date | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Declaration** | Board **authorises** dividend (becomes a **liability**; in India, once **approved by shareholders**) |
| **Ex-dividend** | Buyers **on or after** this date get **no** dividend; before it, stock trades **cum dividend**; price falls by ~DPS |
| **Record** | Only **shareholders of record** receive dividend |
| **Payment** | Dividend **actually paid** |

- **ONGC:** ₹1.25 declared May 21, 2025; ex-dividend and record date Sep 4, 2025; paid Sep 27, 2025
- **Restrictions:** lender covenants; creditor laws; minimum payout by law in **Brazil and Chile**
- Price falls on ex-date because the **firm's market value falls** by the cash paid out

> **Q:** Buyers of a stock on or after which date are **NOT** eligible for the declared dividend?
> - **Ex-dividend date** ✓
>
> **Q:** The stock price falls on the ex-dividend date ideally by the amount of ______.
> - **Dividend per share** ✓

---

### Share Repurchases

| Method | Key Feature |
|---|---|
| **Open market repurchase** | Buys in secondary market over **several months** |
| **Tender offer** | Fixed price, stated number of shares, **~10 days**, **10%-20% premium**; offer can be **cancelled** if too few shares tendered |
| **Dutch auction** | Price **range** set; firm pays the **lowest price** that fills the desired number of shares |
| **Targeted repurchase** | Negotiates directly with a **major shareholder** |

- **Open market** and **tender offer** = most common in **India**

> **Q:** In a Dutch auction, the firm pays ______.
> - **The lowest price at which it can buy the desired number of shares** ✓
>
> **Q:** Which two repurchase routes are most commonly used in India?
> - **Open market repurchase and tender offer** ✓

---

### Stock Dividends, Splits and Reverse Splits

| Action | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Stock dividend (bonus shares)** | Extra shares to existing holders, **no cash** | 1:4 = 25%; 1:2 = 50%; **LIC 1:1 (100%)**, April 2026 |
| **Stock split** | Old share replaced by more new shares | 2-for-1; **Nvidia 10-for-1** (2024, price > $1,000) |
| **Reverse split** | Several shares merged into one higher-priced share | 1-for-10 |

- **Split rationale:** affordable for small investors; higher **demand and liquidity**; announcement effect **+2%** on average
- **Reverse split rationale:** avoid **delisting** (NYSE/Nasdaq minimum **$1**; **no such rule in India**); better **marketability** (avoid penny-stock image)
- **Typical price range:** NIFTY 500 majority **₹100-₹1,500**; NYSE majority **$10-$60**

$$P_{\text{after}} = \frac{\text{MV of Equity}}{\text{Shares after}} \qquad \text{New shares} = \text{Old shares} \times \text{stock dividend \%}$$

**Q:** Fictitious Corp has ₹20 million excess cash, no debt, 10 million shares and other assets of ₹400 million. It pays a 50% (1:2) stock dividend. Find the new share price.

> *Why: A stock dividend leaves cash and equity value unchanged; only the share count rises.*
> $\text{MV} = 20 + 400 = ₹420\text{ million}$ (price before $= 420/10 = ₹42$)
> $\text{New shares} = 10/2 = 5\text{ million} \Rightarrow 15\text{ million outstanding}$
> $P = 420/15$
> **Answer = ₹28** ✓
> *(Total market value stays ₹420 million; only the price per share falls)*

### Comparison of Payouts and Corporate Actions

| Action | Cash | MV of Equity | Shares Outstanding | Price Per Share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cash dividend** | ↓ | ↓ | − | ↓ |
| **Repurchase** | ↓ | ↓ | ↓ | − |
| **Stock dividend** | − | − | ↑ | ↓ |
| **Stock split** | − | − | ↑ | ↓ |
| **Reverse split** | − | − | ↓ | ↑ |

- ↑ increases, ↓ decreases, − unchanged; repurchase price unchanged **only at prevailing market price**

> **Q:** Which action reduces shares outstanding and leaves the share price unchanged?
> - **Share repurchase (at market price)** ✓
>
> **Q:** Which action reduces shares outstanding and raises the price per share with no change in cash or equity value?
> - **Reverse split** ✓

### Information Content of Dividends

| Observation | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Smoothing** | Changes follow **long-run sustainable earnings**; transitory changes ignored |
| **Changes matter** | Focus on **change**, not absolute level (₹5 after ₹4 is big; ₹5 after ₹5 is not) |
| **GM evidence** | Only **8** dividend changes in **23 years** (1985-2008) |
| **Increase** | Signals **confidence in future earnings**; but may signal **lack of positive NPV projects** (**Microsoft 2003**) |
| **Decrease** | Signals earnings will not rebound; but may be a response to an **external shock** (**Harley-Davidson, April 2020**: 95% cut, stock **+15%**) |

| Dividend Change | Avg. Price Reaction |
|---|---|
| **Increase** | **+0.5%** |
| **Decrease** | **−2%** (asymmetric) |

- Price sensitivity to dividend changes has **decreased over time**

> **Q:** Dividend changes have an asymmetric effect on stock prices: increases lead to ______ and decreases lead to ______.
> - **+0.5%; −2%** ✓
>
> **Q:** A dividend increase is **NOT** necessarily good news because it may signal ______.
> - **A lack of positive NPV projects** ✓

### Information Content of Share Repurchases

| Feature | Dividends | Repurchases |
|---|---|---|
| Commitment | High | **Much lower** (no commitment to repeat) |
| Smoothing | Yes | **No** |
| Signal | Sustainable earnings | Shares **undervalued** |

- Repurchase at a **premium**: senior managers and directors **hold on to their shares** → **positive** price reaction

> **Q:** Announcement of a share repurchase at a premium leads to ______.
> - **A positive stock price reaction** ✓

### Cash Dividend vs Share Repurchase (Perfect Capital Markets)

$$P_{ex} = P_{cum} - Div \qquad \text{Shares repurchased} = \frac{\text{Repurchase amount}}{\text{Repurchase price}}$$

- $P_{ex}$ = ex-dividend price; $P_{cum}$ = cum-dividend price; $Div$ = dividend per share

| (Equity value = firm value; no debt) | Before | After Dividend | After Repurchase |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash (₹ mn) | 20 | 0 | 0 |
| Other assets (₹ mn) | 400 | 400 | 400 |
| Total MV (₹ mn) | 420 | 400 | 400 |
| Shares (mn) | 10 | 10 | 9.524 |
| **Price** | ₹42 | **₹40** | **₹42** |

- Perfect markets: dividend → price drops by **DPS**; open market repurchase → **no effect** on price

**Q:** Firm has ₹20 million excess cash, no debt, 10 million shares, other assets ₹400 million. It repurchases ₹20 million of shares at the market price. Find shares repurchased and the price afterwards.

> *Why: Repurchase at market price removes cash and shares in proportion, so price is unchanged.*
> $P_0 = 420/10 = ₹42$
> $\text{Shares repurchased} = 20/42 = 0.476\text{ million}$
> $\text{Shares left} = 10 - 0.476 = 9.524\text{ million}$
> $P = 400/9.524$
> **Answer = ₹42** ✓
> *(Cash dividend of ₹2 per share would instead give ₹40)*

### Investor Preferences and Homemade Dividends

- Investor with **2,000 shares** (not participating in repurchase): total value **₹84,000** in every case

| | Before (₹42) | After Dividend (₹40) | After Repurchase (₹42) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shares | 84,000 | 80,000 | 84,000 |
| Cash | 0 | 4,000 | 0 |
| **Total** | **84,000** | **84,000** | **84,000** |

| Case | Action | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Repurchase, investor wants cash | **Sell** $4000/42 \approx 95$ shares | 1,905 shares + ₹4,000 cash |
| Dividend, investor wants shares | **Buy** $4000/40 = 100$ shares at ex-dividend price | 2,100 shares worth ₹84,000 |
| Dividend ₹2, investor wants ₹9,000 | Sell $5000/40 = 125$ shares | 1,875 shares (₹75,000) + ₹9,000 cash |

- **Miller-Modigliani payout policy irrelevance:** in perfect markets (no taxes, transaction costs, other imperfections), payout policy is **value-irrelevant**; investors replicate any payout on their own

**Q:** Firm pays ₹2 dividend per share (ex-dividend price ₹40). An investor with 2,000 shares wants a total cash payout of ₹9,000. How many shares must she sell?

> *Why: Dividend gives part of the cash; the shortfall is raised by selling shares at the ex-dividend price.*
> $\text{Dividend received} = 2 \times 2000 = ₹4000$
> $\text{Shortfall} = 9000 - 4000 = ₹5000$
> $\text{Shares sold} = 5000/40$
> **Answer = 125 shares** ✓
> *(Holdings: 1,875 shares = ₹75,000 plus ₹9,000 cash = ₹84,000; wealth unchanged)*

> **Q:** In perfect capital markets, payout policy of a firm is irrelevant to firm value.
> - **True** ✓

### Taxes

- Interest on debt is **tax-deductible**; interest earned on invested cash is **taxed** → **cash is negative debt**, holding cash has a **tax disadvantage**
- If dividends and repurchases are taxed differently, investors prefer the **lower-taxed** payout

$$\text{After-tax return} = \text{Pre-tax return} \times (1 - t)$$

**Q:** Fictonia pays 35% tax on T-bill interest (6%). A tax-free investor compares: (1) a ₹100 dividend now, invested by her in T-bills, vs (2) firm retains cash 1 year, invests in T-bills, then pays dividend. Which does she prefer?

> *Why: Compare the investor's own pre-tax-free return with the firm's after-tax return.*
> Choice 1: $100 \times 1.06 = ₹106$
> Choice 2: $6\%(1 - 0.35) = 3.90\%$; $100 \times 1.039 = ₹103.90$
> **Answer = Dividend today (₹106 > ₹103.90)** ✓
> *(The corporate tax on interest income makes retaining cash costly)*

### Agency Costs of Idle Cash

- Excess cash lets managers fund **money-losing pet projects**, pay **excessive perks**, or make **negative NPV acquisitions**
- Paying out excess cash (dividends or repurchases) **reduces agency costs** → share price typically **rises**

> **Q:** Agency costs of idle cash arise when managers ______.
> - **Misuse excess cash (pet projects, perks, negative NPV acquisitions)** ✓

### Firm Life Cycle

| Stage | Payout Behaviour |
|---|---|
| **Young growth firm** | **Retain and reinvest** FCF: avoids issuance costs and shareholders' immediate tax; investors not worried (positive NPV projects) |
| **Mature firm** | Positive NPV projects **scarcer** than cash flow; cash accumulates; investors fear **overinvestment/perks** → **payout** |

| Direct Issuance Cost | Range |
|---|---|
| **Debt** | 1%-3% |
| **Equity** | 3.5%-7% |

> **Q:** Why should a young growth firm retain free cash flow?
> - **Avoids issuance costs and immediate shareholder tax; cash goes to positive NPV projects** ✓

### Behavioural Factors

| Theory | Key Idea |
|---|---|
| **Dividend clienteles** | Investors such as **retirees** prefer **regular, stable cash dividends** (saves transaction costs and inconvenience); may pay more for dividend-paying stocks |
| **Bird-in-the-hand** | "A bird in the hand is worth two in the bush"; dividend payers may have **higher prices**; **not explained by rational theories** |
| **Industry herding** | Firms **follow competitors' dividend policies**; **fear of being left alone (FOMO)** |

- **Frontier Communications:** two share classes with identical fundamentals; **cash dividend shares** traded at a **higher price** despite lower dividends than the stock dividend class; the **premium varied over time** (preference changes)

> **Q:** Which theory says dividend-paying firms enjoy higher prices than non-payers, and cannot be explained by rational theories?
> - **Bird-in-the-hand theory** ✓
>
> **Q:** Firms copying the dividend policies of industry competitors out of fear of missing out illustrates ______.
> - **Industry herding** ✓

## Quick Reference: Which Payout / Effect to Use

| Situation | Action/Answer |
|-----------|---------------|
| Ex-dividend price in perfect markets | $P_{ex} = P_{cum} - Div$ |
| Open market repurchase at market price | Price **unchanged**; shares fall |
| Shares repurchased | Amount / repurchase price |
| Stock dividend or split | Cash and MV of equity unchanged; shares ↑, price ↓ |
| Reverse split | Shares ↓, price ↑ |
| Investor wants cash after repurchase | Sell shares (homemade dividend) |
| Investor wants shares after dividend | Reinvest dividend at ex-dividend price |
| Tax on retained cash | Compare $\text{pre-tax} \times (1 - t)$ with investor's own return |

## Key Decision Rules Summary

| Rule | Detail |
|------|--------|
| Payout irrelevance | Perfect markets → payout policy **does not** affect value (Miller-Modigliani) |
| Dividend vs repurchase price effect | Dividend lowers price by DPS; repurchase at market leaves price unchanged |
| Dividend signal | Increase **+0.5%**, decrease **−2%**; read in context of managers' likely information |
| Dividend vs repurchase commitment | Dividends smoothed and sticky; repurchases flexible, not smoothed, signal undervaluation |
| Stock split | Avg. announcement effect **+2%** |
| Reverse split | Avoids delisting (NYSE/Nasdaq $1 minimum; none in India) |
| Cash and taxes | Cash is negative debt → tax disadvantage of retaining cash |
| Idle cash | Payout reduces agency costs, raises price |
| Life cycle | Young → retain; mature → pay out |
| Behavioural | Clienteles, bird-in-the-hand, industry herding |
