## 1.1 Defining Family Businesses

### 1.1.1 What is a Family Business?

#### Key Concept: Presence of family members is not enough

- Founders being related (brothers, spouses, several relatives) does **not** by itself make a business a family business.
- **Listed or unlisted status is immaterial**; shareholding percentage alone is also not decisive.

#### Three Defining Characteristics

| Characteristic | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Ownership** | Shareholding the family/owners hold; gives **control** over direction, strategy, values and culture (more than a single percentage number) |
| **Management** | How operations are run and whose vision guides the company; run by family members (owners or non-owner family) or by outside professionals |
| **Succession (intent to succeed)** | Intent of the family to make the business last across generations |

#### Succession Intent

- A firm must be **at least into (or intend to reach) the second generation** to be called a family business.
- Intent must exist on **both sides**: the predecessor (founder) wants to pass it on **and** the successor is **willing** to enter the business.
- Founders may intend generational continuity even if survival fails (e.g. a firm may fail despite intent).
- Applied examples from the lecture:

| Firm | Status | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Zerodha, EaseMyTrip | Not (yet) a family business | Early-stage; no second generation / intent not evident; listing is irrelevant |
| TCS | Family business | Tata Trusts own ~70%; ~30% public; management fully professional |
| Reliance | Family business | Ambani family owns; next generation on board and running verticals |

### 1.1.2 Entrepreneurial Family Galaxy

#### Definition: Entrepreneurial Family Galaxy

- A view that puts the **family at the centre** and the various **vehicles as planets revolving around it**.
- Families engage in **wealth creation, wealth management and wealth preservation** through multiple vehicles, not one firm.

| Vehicles (planets) | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Companies / businesses | Wealth creation |
| Trusts | Community engagement, CSR |
| Family office | Wealth management |
| Family council, family constitution | Family governance |
| Family museum | Heritage / legacy |

### 1.1.3 Characteristics of a Family Business

#### Ownership vs Management

- **Ownership** = control and freedom to run the company in the direction, strategy, values and culture the owner wants; **not just a percentage**.
- **Management** = how the company is run; by family members (who may or may not be owners) or by outside professionals (non-owners).
- **Control and influence matter more than shareholding percentage alone.**

#### Ownership-Management Matrix (four quadrants, all possible)

| | Family managed | Professionally managed |
|---|---|---|
| **High family ownership** | Reliance, Wipro (family owns and runs) | TCS, Mahindra, Marico (family owns; professionals run) |
| **Low family ownership** | Apollo Hospitals (Reddy family below 51% but runs every arm) | Max Financial, Suzlon (fewer examples; family still exerts control via board nominees) |

- Typical benchmark mentioned for a meaningful stake: about **20%**, linked to ability to get a **board seat**.
- Low-ownership firms often arise because the family **sells stake over time** while retaining control.

#### Succession: Ownership vs Management Transition

- Each generation decides its own approach; succession need not transfer both ownership and management.
- As firms grow, needed skill sets may not exist in the family, so the **trend is towards outside professionals**.
- Families still try to retain **ownership, strategic and operational control** within themselves.

### 1.1.4 Classification by Degree of Family Control

| Control type | Focus | What it gives the family |
|---|---|---|
| **Ownership control** | Who owns the firm | Defines purpose, values, culture, direction; capital protection ("ghar ki daulat ghar ke andar"); succession of wealth and company; freedom from external accountability |
| **Strategic control** | Long-term direction and scope of the business | Decisions on growth, diversification and what to do or not do |
| **Operational control** | Day-to-day running: who runs it and how | Choice of managers, partners, suppliers, technology/automation level |

#### Ownership Control

- Owner defines **purpose, values and direction**; is answerable mainly to own vision.
- Provides **capital protection**: profits ploughed back into the business.
- Ensures **succession of wealth** over generations (reinvest or pass on).
- Embeds **values in culture** (e.g. dietary rules in the workplace, hiring norms, treating employees as family, avoiding harmful practices).

##### Ways ownership control is exercised

| Mechanism | Description |
|---|---|
| **Equity** | Percentage owned |
| **Involvement in the business** | Day-to-day role: hiring, compensation, values, customer treatment; high compliance and long-term practices so the firm lasts to the third generation |
| **Emotional ownership / identity** | Company is part of family identity (family surname; "company is my first child") |

#### Strategic Control

- Families begin **conservative, risk-averse and frugal** (survive first), then reinvest and diversify.
- Example: Reliance moved from petroleum refining (Dhirubhai Ambani; lowest-cost producer, Jamnagar) to retail, telecom and technology (Mukesh Ambani).
- Strategy is shaped jointly by **market forces, the family's goals and vision, and the family's inherent strengths** (e.g. capital, compliance and regulatory support).
- Each generation leaves its imprint and adapts to the evolved market.

#### Operational Control

- Concerns **who runs the business** (owner, family members, outside professionals) and **how**.
- Includes managing a **hybrid team** of family and professionals with a performance management system for both.
- Includes choice of partners, suppliers and automated vs manual set-up (technology-intensive or craft-based industries).

#### Key Concept: Founder's Trap

- Arises when skill sets are not in the family, there are too few family members, or the founder is unwilling to look outside.
- The company becomes **limited by the founder's imagination, capacity and vision**, so growth is limited.
- Affects the family and later generations too (successors may want a different direction or new talent).

## 1.2 Strengths and Weaknesses of Family Businesses

### 1.2.1 Strengths of Family Business

| Strength | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Long-term perspective / patient capital** | Intent to pass wealth across generations; plan 5-7+ years ahead; not driven by **QSQT (Quarter se Quarter Tak)** investor pressure; can wait for results (example: Titan watch project; Marwari "1,000-day rule" to wait before judging a new venture) |
| **Deep customer relationships** | Family-to-family, often same community; interaction beyond business; greater trust and better understanding of customer needs |
| **Values-driven culture** | Family value system shapes internal organisation and how the firm engages other stakeholders; culture of giving reflects family's cultural roots |
| **Frugality and financial discipline** | Avoids external finance accountability; prefers low debt; ploughs back profits; operates a tight ship |
| **Quick, flexible decision-making** | Centralised decisions by founder/family; no long formal planning hierarchy; gut feel and familiarity; speed to market |
| **Crisis resilience** | Stakeholders extend credit or delayed payments; family closes ranks and sacrifices; no thought of exit; 2-3 year downturn is short against a three-generation vision |
| **Employees as extended family** | Loyalty and reciprocity from non-family staff during tough times |
| **Strong ties with suppliers, customers, community** | Mutual help in crises; relationships valued over transactional exchange |

- Underlying driver: family businesses understand **family dynamics**, so they prioritise **relationships over transactions** (emotion is a key driver).

### 1.2.2 Weaknesses of Family Business

| Weakness | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Founder/owner dependence (bottleneck)** | Patriarch retains control (especially finance); delays or blocks decisions; growth, modernisation and professionalisation depend on founder's likes and dislikes |
| **Family harmony over business efficiency** | Family comes first; owner accepts efficiency/revenue loss to keep family united ("dining room in the boardroom"); shareholder value equals owner/family value |
| **Dependence on family for growth / limited professionalisation** | Trust-based preference for family members; limited number of relatives; skill mismatch causes inefficiency; succession depends on **ability and willingness** of next generation |
| **Resistance to change** | "Worked for 50 years" mindset; fails to foresee disruption (example: distributors bypassed by Udaan); successors may inherit a firm with little value left |
| **Favouritism and nepotism** | Family members appointed by relation, given liberties; lowers employee morale and efficiency |
| **Emotional decision-making and conflict** | Decisions aim to protect family and retain control; short-term and emotional rather than logical; family and business are interconnected so a weak business weakens the family and vice versa |

- Emotional decisions are typically **short-term fixes**, contradicting the family's usual long-term perspective.
- Succession nudging: next generation is steered towards relevant education (e.g. medicine for a nursing home, engineering for an engineering firm); hereditary succession occurs even in professions such as law.
- Trust-intensive sectors (banking, money lending, diamonds) and craft-based sectors (e.g. weaving; tacit skills) show strong family preference.

## 1.3 Family vs Non-family Businesses

### 1.3.1 Difference between Family and Non-family Businesses

| Dimension | Family business | Non-family business |
|---|---|---|
| **Planning horizon** | Very long-term; lasts a generation beyond the current one | Quarter to quarter (QSQT), driven by investors and market |
| **Decision-making** | Maximise shareholder value, but shareholder = family; shaped by owner thinking and **family values** | Maximise shareholder value as the market wants |
| **Risk** | Conservative; do not lose capital; conserve family wealth for next generation | Maximise output from available resources |
| **Capital allocation / raising** | Avoids equity dilution and PE; uses debt, family and friends, banks, **retained earnings**; patient capital | Equity markets (IPOs), debt, private equity; investors measure returns quarterly |
| **Transparency** | Minimal disclosure (many are partnerships or private firms); limited even to employees and non-business family | Mandated by regulator (SEBI) and investors; periodic town halls |
| **Employees** | Loyalty is foremost; relatives hired; personal, emotional, trust-based relations; long tenures (15-20 years) | Professionals on detailed contracts; professional/contractual relations |
| **Governance** | Informal; owners' group; possibly family council | Structured board with committees (audit, compensation); appointments disclosed to regulator |
| **Succession** | Family first; if none, outsider is hired while ownership control is retained and owners stay active | Merit-based; hired from outside for skills and experience |
| **Value system** | Family values guide the business (e.g. vegetarianism in canteen); employees treated like family | Vision and mission statements; shareholder value |

## 1.4 Theories and Frameworks

### 1.4.0 Role of Theories and Frameworks

- **Theory** = generalised, simplified (parsimonious) explanation that holds across settings, firms and time.
- **Framework** = scaffolding that converts theory into analysis and decision-making (like a formula with parts that fit together).
- General theories apply to all businesses but must be **adapted to recognise the family's role** (relationships, values, purpose, dual financial and non-financial objectives).

### 1.4.1 Agency and Stewardship Theory

#### Definition: Agency Theory

- Relationship between a **principal** (owner who employs) and an **agent** (who does the work).
- Three elements:

| Element | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Goal incongruence** | Principal and agent have differing interests |
| **Information asymmetry** | Information is not seamlessly shared between them |
| **Agency cost** | Cost borne by the firm because of these incongruences |

#### Agency Theory in Family Firms

- If the agent is a **family member**: agency costs are **lower** (more trust, shared values, goal congruence, free flow of information).
- If the agent is a **non-family outside professional**: agency costs can **rise**.
- Applies also in **listed family firms** and where several owners have equal stakes (e.g. brothers with different views).

| Type | Arises between | 
|---|---|
| **Primary agency cost** | Principal and agent |
| **Secondary agency cost** | Two principals (principal-principal conflict) |

#### Behavioural Agency Model

- Combines agency theory with **prospect theory**: people are **more risk-seeking in the loss domain and less risk-seeking in the gain domain** (a loss is weighted more than an equal gain).
- Assumes individuals are **self-serving** and individually rational.

#### Definition: Stewardship Theory

- Contrasting view: humans are not purely self-centred; the manager (linked to self-actualisation) acts as a **steward**.
- Steward characteristics: **long-term thinking, deep investment in the company, a mission to grow the business, high alignment with owners**.
- In family firms:
  - An outside professional acts in the interest of the family and aligns with family goals.
  - A family manager must also act in the interest of the rest of the family shareholders.
  - Both **business goals and family goals** are expected to be served.
- **Stewardship may decline over generations** (second/third generation vs first).

### 1.4.2 Stakeholder Theory

#### Definition: Stakeholder Theory

- Firms should give primacy to **all stakeholders** (suppliers, customers, employees, owners, community), not only shareholders (**shareholder primacy**).
- Shareholder = owner holding shares; stakeholder = everyone involved in the system.

#### Application to Family Businesses

- Suppliers, employees and customers are treated like family (some are actually relatives).
- Relationships are deep, last decades and pass down generations.
- Stakeholders do not abandon each other in crises; strong bonds extend to the community (shared celebrations, community investment).
- These bonds **explain resilience, continuity and long-term survival**.

### 1.4.3 Socioemotional Wealth (SEW) Theory

#### Definition: Socioemotional Wealth

- Family wealth is **not only financial**; **legacy, identity and emotional connections** are also wealth (social and emotional aspects not measured in currency).
- Explains why financially unconventional decisions can make sense from the family's perspective.
- Family firms have **twin objectives**: financial wealth and socioemotional wealth.

#### FIBER Framework (Baron and colleagues)

| Letter | Dimension | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **F** | **Family control and influence** | Extent family exerts authority over strategic decisions (board composition, management) |
| **I** | **Identification** | Psychological overlap between family identity and the company's brand image/reputation |
| **B** | **Binding social ties** | Reciprocal long-term social capital with firm, community, suppliers, workers, local government |
| **E** | **Emotional attachment** | Affective charge, shared history and emotional bonds (positive and negative) |
| **R** | **Renewal of family bonds (dynastic element)** | Desire to pass business on as a going concern to future generations |

### 1.4.4 Resource-Based View (RBV)

#### Definition: Resource-Based View

- A firm is a **bundle of resources and capabilities**.

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Tangible** | Finance, people (human capital), technology |
| **Intangible** | Patents, brand reputation, culture |

#### Definition: Familiness

- **Familiness** = unique bundle of resources created by the **interaction of business-level resources, family-level resources and individual family member resources**; a source of distinctive competitive advantage.
- Unique to each family and each family firm (each vehicle in the family galaxy can have different familiness).

| Dimension | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Structural** | Social interactions and networks within and outside the family at business level |
| **Cognitive** | Shared vision and purpose, unique language, stories, culture |
| **Relational** | Trust, norms, obligations, identity |
| **Family involvement** | Family involvement, essence and organisational identity |

### 1.4.5 Transaction Cost Economics (TCE)

#### Definition: Transaction Cost Economics

- Firm = **nexus of contracts** that can be conducted in the external market or internalised.
- **Boundary of the firm** is set by where internal cost is lower than market cost.

$$\text{Perform internally if } C_{\text{internal}} < C_{\text{market}}\text{; otherwise outsource}$$

- Family firms pursue **financial and socioemotional wealth** together, which shapes their scope.

#### Asset Specificity

- **Specific asset**: must be modified for one firm's use and cannot easily be used elsewhere (high specificity).
- **Generic asset**: transferable without modification (e.g. a Rs 100 note is fungible).
- In family firms, familiness, family social capital and reputation are specific to that family.

#### Generic Non-tradable Assets of Family Firms

| Asset | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Bonding social capital** | Social capital within the family and family firm |
| **Bridging social capital** | Links to members outside the network (bridges between networks) |
| **Reputation** | Built with community, government, employees; cannot be transferred across family businesses |
| **Tacit knowledge** | Uncodified knowledge, rules and norms understood by family and employees; explicit (codified) knowledge, in contrast, is easily moved |

### 1.4.6 Systems Theory

#### Definition: Systems Theory

- An organisation is a **unified, interconnected, purpose-driven** mechanism of parts.

| Component | Role |
|---|---|
| **Inputs** | Suppliers, raw materials |
| **Transformation** | Conversion using machinery, people, technology |
| **Outputs** | Finished goods/services to customer |
| **Feedback** | Customer feedback loops back to change inputs and processes |

#### Open vs Closed Systems

| Feature | Open system | Closed system |
|---|---|---|
| Interaction | Interacts with external environment | Interacts only within itself |
| Outcome | Dynamic, adapts using feedback | Little market linkage; may stagnate or decline |

#### Systems Theory in Family Business

- **Business = open system** (customers, suppliers, market risk).
- **Family = more closed system** (internal emotional dynamics, legacy, control).
- Three interdependent systems: **family, ownership, management**.
- Ownership system defines what owners want and the value they see (shareholder = family owner).

### 1.4.7 Adapting Theories to Family Business

- Theories apply to non-family firms too; for family firms they are **modified** to account for the family's relationships, values, purpose and **dual objectives**.

### 1.4.8 How Family Involvement Influences Family Firm Behaviour and Outcomes

#### Framework Flow (left to right)

| Stage | Content |
|---|---|
| 1. **Family involvement** | Input |
| 2. **Family behavioural disposition** | **Willingness** (favourable disposition to continue/transition across generations) and **Ability** (discretion and competence in allocating resources, capabilities, decisions) |
| 3. **Strategic drivers** | Goals, governance, resources |
| 4. **Family firm behaviour** | Professionalisation, innovation, restructuring, M&A |
| 5. **Family firm outcomes** | **Economic** and **non-economic** |

- **Feedback loops** run throughout the framework.

### 1.4.9 Goals, Governance and Resources Framework

| Element | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Goals** | Financial goals and non-economic/socioemotional goals |
| **Governance** | Structures aligning family goals with business goals; may extend across the entrepreneurial family galaxy |
| **Resources** | Familiness and generic non-tradable assets |

- Framework captures **strategic conduct and performance** as the interplay of what the family wants (goals), how decisions are made (governance) and what unique assets it leverages (resources).

### 1.4.10 Ownership Competence

#### Definition: Ownership Competence

- Ownership treated as an **active strategic asset and skill set**; ability of family shareholders to act as **informed decision-makers and responsible stewards**, creating value while maintaining **family unity and harmony**.

| Dimension | Question |
|---|---|
| **Matching competence** | **What** to own (which businesses/assets) |
| **Governance competence** | **How** to own (single owner, JV, partnership; family council, constitution; professional vs family-dominated board) |
| **Timing competence** | **When** to own |

#### Other Aspects

| Aspect | Options |
|---|---|
| **Ownership approach** | **Custodian** (hold and pass on) vs **entrepreneur** (take more risk) |
| **Ownership rights** | Non-voting vs voting rights |
| **Ownership activity** | Active vs passive role of owner |

## 1.5 Capital and Goals of a Family Business

### 1.5.1 Types of Capital

| Capital | Description |
|---|---|
| **Human capital** | Stable, highly committed workforce with experience and knowledge |
| **Social capital** | Relationships with employees, suppliers, customers, community; long-term and durable in tough times |
| **Political capital** | Social capital combined with power and influence; access to people who can make things happen |
| **Financial capital** | Internal accruals, retained earnings, family investments; **patient capital** ploughed back into the business |
| **Heritage** | Intangible: shared values, affection for the past, history, traditions, identity and pride passed across generations |

### 1.5.2 Goals of a Family Business

#### Two-dimensional Goal Matrix

| | **Economic** | **Non-economic** |
|---|---|---|
| **Family-oriented** | Maintain/protect **family control**; increase family **financial wealth** | Family **harmony**; family **reputation and social status**; positive family **identification** with the firm |
| **Non-family oriented** | **Growth of the firm** and improved firm performance | Positive environment and security within the firm; nurturing internal/external relationships (community, government); positive community impact |

## 1.6 Historical Perspective of Family Firms

### 1.6.1 Historical Perspective of Family Firms

#### Key Concept: Family firm is the original form

- Family business is the **original and still dominant form** of enterprise; the modern professionally managed, widely held corporation is the **exception** (recent and geographically narrow).
- Family-run trade predates limited-liability joint stock companies (Dutch East India Company 1602; English East India Company 1600, which pioneered tradable share capital).
- Before that, trade caravans, craft guilds, banking houses and shipping ventures were run by families, kinship networks or partnerships bound by **personal trust** rather than formal contract.

#### Early Modern Dynasties

| Family | Details |
|---|---|
| **Medici** (Florence, 15th-18th centuries) | One of Europe's first international banking networks; financed the papacy and crowns; converted wealth into political power (three popes; ruled Florence) |
| **Fugger** (Augsburg, 16th-17th centuries) | Bankers to Holy Roman Emperors (financed Charles V's 1519 election); silver, copper, textile trade and mining; early double-entry bookkeeping and an intelligence/news service; edge came from **information networks** |

#### Bank of Korea Study (2008)

- About 5,000-odd firms over 200 years old across **41 countries**.

| Country | Share |
|---|---|
| **Japan** | 56% (~3,000+ firms) |
| **Germany** | ~15% (~800) |
| **Netherlands** | ~4% (~200) |
| France | Next |
| Africa and Americas combined | Under 5% (colonial disruption, war, late start of formal enterprise) |

#### Japan: Longevity Examples

- **Kongo Gumi** (578 CE, Osaka): temple carpentry; ~1,400 years, 40+ generations; practised **Mukoyoshi** (adopting a son-in-law for competence when no capable natural heir; "adopt for competence, not just bloodline"); survived wars and the Meiji Restoration; pivoted to coffin-making post-WWII; overexpansion and debt made it a subsidiary of Takamatsu Group in 2006.
- **Nishiyama Onsen Keiunkan** (~705 CE) and **Hoshi Ryokan** (~718 CE): hot-spring inns, 1,300+ years, 40-50 generations; staff positions passed down in employee families; Nishiyama's first non-family outsider manager took charge in 2017 (professionalisation inflection point).
- Japan has roughly 20,000-30,000 firms over 100 years old and 24 of the world's 50 oldest companies.

#### India: Long-lived Family Enterprises

| Group | Details |
|---|---|
| **Tata** | Jamsetji Tata (textile trader) chose steel as nation-building; scouted ore/coal years before Tata Steel's 1907 founding; completed after his death by son Dorabji; example of decades-long horizon |
| **Godrej** (~1897), **Tata**, **Wadia** | Well past 125th anniversary |
| **Forbes and Company** (Bombay, 1767) | One of India's oldest companies; later acquired by Shapoorji Pallonji group |
| **Murugappa** | Chettiar origin; began as money lending in colonial Burma; returned post-independence; diversified into agriculture, financial services, engineering; 70,000+ employees |

### 1.6.2 Factors Behind Durable Family Firms

- Cultural norms (e.g. attitude to adoption/non-blood succession, craftsmanship identity), economic conditions (weak formal contract enforcement, scarcity of capital), state industrial policy and succession practices explain why some regions and communities produced unusually durable family firms.

### 1.6.3 Industrial-era Family Dynasties: The Global Context

| Family | Details |
|---|---|
| **Rothschild** | European banking family (18th century onward); Pan-European network; private courier/information system rivalling governments; financed wars, government debt, infrastructure in Britain, France, Austria |
| **Rockefeller** | John D. Rockefeller founded Standard Oil (1870); first great American industrial trust; broken up by the **1911 antitrust case** |
| **Ford** | Ford Motor Company founded 1903 |
| **Krupp** | German steel and armaments, founded 1811 |

- Industrial-era fortunes were entangled with **national political and military history** (including both world wars).

### 1.6.4 India's Mercantile Communities

#### Key Concept: Active adaptation

- Indian family business communities **actively reorganised** capital, alliances, product lines and internal family legal structures; they did not merely survive shocks.

#### Pre-colonial and Early Colonial Period (pre-1757 to c.1850)

- Communities: **Marwadis, Banias, Chettiars, Parsis, Bohras, Sindhis, Khatris**.
- Built indigenous banking and money lending networks financing agriculture, long-distance trade and rulers' wars.

#### Definition: Hundi

- **Hundi** = negotiable, transferable **bill of exchange**, an early **letter of credit** system; moved money across distant, politically fragmented states without physical transport; substituted for missing formal contract enforcement and central banking.

| Community | Features |
|---|---|
| **Chettiars** (Tamil Nadu) | Apprenticeship-based multinational banking; boys trained in ledger keeping and interest calculation; sent on ~3-year postings across India, Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia, Singapore, China; network punished defaults reputationally |
| **Marwadis** (Marwar/Shekhawati) | Migrated proactively along British trade routes to Bengal; early financiers and traders in Calcutta (deliberate geographic bet) |
| **Bohras** (Gujarat; Surat, Bombay) | Shia Muslim trading community; **centrally coordinated** through religious leadership; interest-free lending and financial discipline promoted; contrast to organically formed kinship/apprenticeship trust of Marwadi, Chettiar, Parsi networks |

### 1.6.5 Hindu Business Culture

| View | Argument |
|---|---|
| **Max Weber** (early 20th century) | Hindu culture (caste obligations, karma-based fatalism, ritual purity) was inhospitable to rational, innovation-oriented capitalism (as in Protestant Europe) |
| **Business historians** (Vijendra Tripathi, Claude Markovits, Thomas Timberg) | Weber conflated caste with business community; mercantile sub-castes (Marwadis, Chettiars, Parsis, Bohras) were flexible capitalist networks using community identity as **information and trust infrastructure** |
| **Gurcharan Das (saakh framing)** | **Saakh** = **reputational trust**, the central resource these communities traded on |

#### Three Ingredients of Success (Gurcharan Das)

1. High appetite for **commercial risk-taking**.
2. Central importance of **saakh (reputational trust)** in enforcing deals.
3. **Dense internal networks** for capital pooling and information sharing.

- Communities credited: Sindhis, Parsis, Marwadis, Chettiars.

### 1.6.6 The Managing Agency System

#### Definition: Managing Agency System

- Dominant Indian business organisation form for over a century (formalised from about the 1820s).
- A **managing agent** (individual, partnership or firm) is appointed on a long-term contract to manage one or more separate **joint stock companies** (procurement, sales, financing, operations).
- Remuneration: commission about **5% on sales/consignment value** or up to **10% of net profits**, plus underwriting, interest and administrative fees.
- Agent contributed only a **small fraction of risk capital**; most came from passive outside shareholders (no securities analysis industry, credit rating or strong minority protection then).
- Resulted in a **pyramid**: small family capital controlling many public companies (ancestor of pyramidal business-group structures).

#### Evolution

- Indigenous financiers moved from money lending to **brokers and agents** for British trading houses (e.g. Andrew Yule Bird and Company; trade in cotton, opium, jute, indigo), then to **direct industrial ownership**.
- Early 20th century: Marwari entrepreneurs led by the **Birla family** built their own agencies and took control of jute from British houses.
- Jamsetji Tata founded the Tata Group in 1868 (textiles) and Tata Steel in 1907, building direct industrial ownership rather than agency-fee business.

#### Governance Weakness and Abolition

- Agents were paid first via commission regardless of minority shareholders' outcomes: **profit siphoning, weak accountability** (ancestor of today's concern with related-party transactions and tunnelling).
- Restricted by the **Companies Act 1956** (Nehru government); **formally abolished in April 1970** (Indira Gandhi government).

### 1.6.7 Active Adaptation under the License Raj

- **License Raj: 1951 to 1991**: industrial licensing, import substitution, capital controls.
- Family houses lobbied for and navigated licensing, cultivated political relationships for licences and import quotas, and diversified opportunistically into accessible sectors, producing **unrelated diversified conglomerates**.
- **MRTP Act 1969**: enacted to curb economic concentration of large, mostly family-controlled houses (Tatas and Birlas were explicit targets).
- Response: groups **fragmented ownership** across many smaller family-branch-controlled entities to avoid scrutiny at asset thresholds, shaping ownership patterns still visible today.

### 1.6.8 Liberalisation Era (1991-Present): Proactive Adaptation

- 1991 reforms opened India to foreign competition and capital.
- Family houses **restructured group structures, divested non-core businesses, professionalised management**, and sometimes split joint family holdings into separately run branches (e.g. Marwari and Punjabi origin trading conglomerates in 1990s-2000s).
- **Reliance** (founded 1966, Dhirubhai Ambani) expanded into petrochemicals, telecom, retail; **Wipro** (Azim Premji) pivoted from vegetable oil to IT services from the 1980s.
- Second/third-generation leaders (often educated abroad) adopt **digital-first strategies, ESG commitments, modern governance** and court international institutional capital.
- **IPO wave (1990s-2000s)**: families traded some ownership dilution for growth capital, brand credibility and a governance-discipline signal; led to gradual decline in average promoter shareholding even as family wealth grew.

## 1.7 Performance of Family Firms

### 1.7.1 Anderson and Reeb (2003)

- Study of **S&P 500 firms over 10 years**; families held nearly **20% of equity** in the S&P 500.
- Counterintuitive finding: agency theory predicts concentrated insider control is costly to outsiders, yet **family firms performed better than non-family firms on average**.
- Performance was **even stronger when the CEO was a family member** rather than an outside professional.
- Examples: Walmart (Walton family), Ford (disproportionate voting control).

### 1.7.2 The Alignment-Entrenchment Trade-off

| Effect | Description |
|---|---|
| **Alignment effect** | Family wealth tied to long-run firm value gives strong incentive to act in the firm's interest; low monitoring costs; long-term horizon |
| **Entrenchment effect** (Villalonga and Amit, 2006) | Controlling family extracts **private benefits of control**: related-party transactions, appointing under-qualified relatives, resisting actions that dilute control (e.g. refusing acquisition or fund infusion); a conflict among shareholders rather than owner vs manager |

- On average, the **alignment effect dominates**.
- Relationship is **nonlinear**: performance is strongest at **moderate family ownership**; decline can begin at very high concentration when entrenchment dominates.

### 1.7.3 Crisis Resilience in Family Firms

| Crisis | Findings |
|---|---|
| **2008 global financial crisis** (study period 2006-2010, studied to ~2012) | Founder-led firms invested less in value-destroying activities (e.g. M&A), kept **lower capex**, had **better access to credit** (lender confidence in reputation and relationship capital) |
| **COVID-19** (2018-2021, multi-country) | Family firms showed **higher financial performance**, even after controlling for pre-pandemic conditions; stronger where family involved in management; stronger governance and connections; performance concentrated among younger, smaller, non-industrial firms |

- Crises reveal differences more than ordinary years (in normal years differences in return on capital are marginal).
- **McKinsey (2024), ~300 large Indian family firms** (revenue at least Rs 2,000 crore at least once in 5 years): about **2.3% higher revenue growth** than non-family firms; **shareholder returns about twice** non-family firms (2012-2022); more disciplined, less sentiment-driven capital allocation; willing to exit or restructure underperforming legacy businesses.

## 1.8 Economic and Social Impact of Family Firms

### 1.8.1 Economic and Social Impact of Family Firms

#### Economic Footprint

- Family firms contribute over **70% of global GDP** and about **60% of global employment** (McKinsey estimate); if combined as one economy they would be the **third largest**.
- India: family firms about 79% of GDP (expected to rise towards 85%); Spain about 70%.

#### Indian Context

- Dominance across the **entire size spectrum**: millions of small, informal enterprises (kirana stores, small units, farms; mostly sole proprietorship, HUF, partnerships; often **necessity-driven**) and large professionally managed groups (Reliance, Tata, Aditya Birla; **opportunity-driven**).
- Many firms but revenue concentrated in a few houses; reasons:
  - Historical legacy of the managing agency era and License Raj (capital concentration).
  - Prevalence of **HUF** and family partnership legal structures.
  - Underdeveloped market for corporate control (hostile takeovers and manager buyouts rare), reducing competitive pressure.

#### Social Footprint

| Area | Details |
|---|---|
| **Employment** | Jobs in tier-2 and tier-3 towns, formal and informal |
| **Philanthropy and CSR** | Tata Trusts (structured charitable endowment: education, health, rural development, arts, culture); Azim Premji Foundation; Companies Act requires **CSR spend of at least 2% of average net profit** |
| **Gender inclusion** | More women heading family firms; focus on women's employment |
| **Family offices and professionalisation** | Indian family office ecosystem has grown in the last decade |

#### Concerns about Concentrated Family Control

| Concern | Description |
|---|---|
| **Concentration of economic power / barriers to entry** | Handful of families control large private capital; network capital can bar outsiders; political-economy risk |
| **Minority shareholder risk** | Related-party transactions, expropriation |
| **Tunnelling and propping** | **Tunnelling**: channelling resources to a close circle via related-party transactions; **propping**: supporting group companies in crisis; pyramid structures can shift value away from minorities |
| **Nepotism vs meritocracy** | Family-based succession may not add value for minority shareholders |
| **Philanthropy motive** | Genuine socioemotional commitment vs reputation-driven or transactional CSR |

- Overall balance so far: gains (patient capital, long-term orientation, philanthropy, crisis resilience, employment) largely outweigh risks.

## 1.9 Longevity of Family Firms

### 1.9.1 Longevity of Family Firms

- Popular belief: family businesses do not survive beyond three generations ("shirtsleeves to shirtsleeves"); in reality many survive the long run.

#### Institutional and Country-level Factors

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Property rights and inheritance laws** | Predictable laws raise survival (Western world, Japan); nationalisation or unpredictable inheritance tax lowers it (Wendel family's 250-year-old French steel business collapsed with nationalisation in 1974) |
| **Cultural practices** | Japan's capable son-in-law practice decouples competence from lineage, widening the leader pool |
| **Capital market depth / market for corporate control** | Deep markets (PE buyouts, hostile takeovers) force professionalisation or acquisition; shallow markets (India) allow even weaker family firms to survive longer |
| **Political stability** | Sudden shocks (License Raj, 1991 liberalisation) strain firms; Indian family firms survived nonetheless |
| **Industry characteristics** | Craft, heritage and hospitality sectors with slow-changing skills and production methods last; capital-intensive, rapidly disrupted industries (e.g. telecom) shorten longevity |

#### Firm-level Drivers

| Driver | Contribution |
|---|---|
| **Governance** | Family constitution, council, regular meetings and mechanisms |
| **Succession planning** | Careful, early planning avoids succession trouble |
| **Ownership structure** | HUF in India; consolidating ownership via trust or holding company preserves control and capital |
| **Professionalisation of management** | Separating who runs from who owns; non-family executives in operating roles while family retains ownership and oversight through the board |

## 2.1 Module Introduction and Learning Outcomes

### 2.1.1 Introduction to the Module

#### 2.1.1.1 Core Idea
- A family business operates through **two systems** that are **distinct in priorities yet closely connected in practice**
  - What happens in the business affects **family relationships, expectations and harmony**
  - Decisions within the family shape how the business is **owned, governed and managed**
- Interdependence becomes **more complex as ownership spreads across generations** and members take on different roles
- Module flow
  - Family and business dimensions
  - How owners exercise power, define value and shape ownership and decision-making
  - Integration through the **three-circle model**
  - How family businesses **evolve** as families, ownership structures and businesses grow
- **Central question:** not simply who owns the business, but **how family, ownership and business interests interact over time**

### 2.1.2 Learning Outcomes

#### 2.1.2.1 Outcomes
- Analyse the relationship between the **family system** and the **business system**, including their different expectations and priorities
- Distinguish between **family-first, business-first and family enterprise spirit** philosophies and examine how they influence business decisions
- Apply the **three-circle model** to identify stakeholder positions and interpret the family, ownership and business dimensions
- Examine the **powers and five rights of family owners** and assess how ownership choices influence business direction, value and continuity
- Compare the **stages of family-business evolution** and identify the changing ownership, management and relationship issues that emerge as the family and business grow

## 2.2 Family and Business Systems

### 2.2.1 The Family–Business System

#### 2.2.1.1 Two Systems, One Larger System
- A family business brings together **two powerful systems**: the **family system** and the **business system**
- They are **very distinct** but **interconnected**; they cannot function separately
- A family business is a **larger interconnected system** = family system + business system + the interconnection between them

#### 2.2.1.2 Blurred Boundaries
| Aspect | Non-family business | Family business |
|---|---|---|
| Boundary between family and business | Clear; nothing moves from the dinner table to the boardroom or back | **Not clear**; a lot of interconnection and "haze" |
| Relationship between the systems | Simple | **Complex and multidimensional** |
| Impact of decisions | Confined to business | Business decisions affect the family **at multiple levels**; multiple family players are part of the business |

### 2.2.2 Introduction to the Family System

#### 2.2.2.1 Nature of the Family System
- A **self-contained unit** with its own **rules, values, culture, policies and way of functioning**
- Defines acceptable and non-acceptable behaviour and what is tolerated
- Has a **history**: rules followed, traditions, celebrations, important functions, pilgrimages from earlier generations
- Is both an **open and a closed system**
  - Closed: its own rules and values
  - Open: bound by market vagaries; family expectations grow when the business grows
- Not purely self-contained: new members enter through marriage and **bring new value systems**; values evolve over generations
- Deeply **embedded in the broader community**
  - Communities share practices (e.g. a community rule of sticking with a business for **1,000 days**)
  - The community pools resources to support a new entrepreneur
- Families from the **same community can have very different value systems**, shaped by each family's unique journey and the stage of its business

#### 2.2.2.2 Expectations of the Family from the Business
| Expectation | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Stable lifestyle support** | Family wants a stable lifestyle (house, area, city, what is allowable and not); **income from the business supports it** |
| **Emotional needs** | Business decisions should consider the **emotional aspects** of the family; family thinks emotionally, business thinks logically and rationally |
| **Identity** | The business gives the family a **sense of identity**; it is an extension of the family's identity |
- These three are **common across all family businesses**
- Expectations **vary from generation to generation**; they keep pace as the family and business grow, and can change even within the same generation

#### 2.2.2.3 Education and Employment Expectations
- **Education** is not only school and college; it includes **induction into the family business**
  - **Informal apprenticeship:** a child sits in the father's cabin or mans the shop, listening to commercial discussions ("fly on the wall")
  - Business conversations flow into the dining room and decisions are taken inside the family residence
  - Over time, scions increasingly **work outside before returning**
- **Employment** expectation is **unconditional and two-sided**
  - The default is that the next generation will be employed in the family business
  - The parent also states "I am building this business for all of you" and expects them to join and continue it

#### 2.2.2.4 Titles, Pay and Harmony
- The family pays extra attention to **family harmony**; anything that disturbs it is avoided
- Therefore **equal pay, titles and perks** for family members
  - Skills, merit and extra qualification are not the basis; a differently qualified person gets a **different role but the same title and pay**
  - Equivalent perks (e.g. an equivalent car) are provided
- The family system is **inclusive, protective and emotional**
- **Why harmony:** the intent is that the business **lasts two or three generations**; a fractured family leads to a **split of the business**
- **Dichotomy:** the business can be **capitalist** while the family is **socialist**; the two must **coexist** and create a constant tug of war

### 2.2.3 Family Values and Hierarchy

#### 2.2.3.1 What the Family Values
- **Support, extreme loyalty and trust**; trust is the **most important currency** on the family side (and matters in the business too)
- **Skills, education and talent** are not given much importance
- Core expectation: **"accept me for who I am"** in spite of shortcomings
- Loyalty, trust and support are expected from **every family member across branches**, whether or not they have a business role

#### 2.2.3.2 Family Hierarchy
| Basis of hierarchy | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Generation** | A member of an earlier generation has a bigger say, regardless of talent or skills |
| **Age** | Within a generation, the elder is listened to **with no questions asked** |
- Example given: the youngest of seven second-generation brothers is a day older than the eldest brother's son, yet the nephew must listen to the uncle, and **wealth also goes by hierarchy** (uncle gets disproportionate wealth)

#### 2.2.3.3 Informality and Bonding
- Rules, hierarchy and policies are **unwritten**, passed person to person and generation to generation
- **Informality is a plus**: trust lets things be done on word; avoids bureaucracy
- Bonding events (e.g. festival seasons, with the business shutting down) keep the family together
- Earlier generations lived in one building with one kitchen and one dining room; today **nuclearity** is increasing, so families have to find ways for members to know each other

### 2.2.4 The Business System

#### 2.2.4.1 What the Business System Looks For
- Rules, policies and procedures needed to run the business; aims to be **successful, profitable and growing**
- **Customer first:** what problem is solved, what needs are met, and evolving with the customer
- **Shareholder value:** optimise growth and performance
- Performance measured by **revenue, profits, number of places present, amount of investments, efficiency**
- Seeks **talent** that meets market requirements and growth; emphasises **performance management, merit and qualifications** (conventional education matters more than learning at the shop)
- **Incentives** are structured to drive results, so business performance and people performance go hand in hand
- Clear **hierarchy of authority** (senior/junior), with pay grades and bonuses varying accordingly
- The business also **has to meet the requirements of the family**

#### 2.2.4.2 Family System vs Business System
| Dimension | Family system | Business system |
|---|---|---|
| Orientation | Emotional; "socialist" | Logical, rational; "capitalist" |
| Key currency | Trust, loyalty, support | Competence, merit |
| Role of talent | Not important | Important |
| Pay and titles | Equal | Differ with performance and role |
| Performance link | Not linked to business performance | People performance linked to business performance |
| Hierarchy | Generation and age | Authority structure and capability |
| Employment | Default entitlement | Based on talent and need |
| Rules | Unwritten, informal | Formal policies and procedures |

### 2.2.5 Balancing Family and Business Priorities

#### 2.2.5.1 Three Philosophies
| Philosophy | Primary emphasis |
|---|---|
| **Family First** | Family relationships, harmony, stability and family needs |
| **Business First** | Performance, competence, customers, profitability and growth |
| **Family Enterprise Spirit** (balanced view) | Bringing family and business considerations together |
- Family businesses vary along a **spectrum** of which system they prioritise

#### 2.2.5.2 Family-First Characteristics
- Family requirements (stability, lifestyle support) come first; **income and dividends are sacrosanct** and the business should not touch them
- Relationships and hierarchy are not to be interfered with
- All family members are employed; **performance appraisal is of little importance**
- Growth is linked to **age and hierarchy, not talent**; cousins of the same age are promoted together on the same pay and tenure matters
- **Non-family employees** are expected to be loyal and to do work beyond their job description (employee of the family as well); no one-size job description
- The **patriarch's discretion and wishes** are paramount; decisions are **never questioned**
- Risk: can become **detrimental to the business**

#### 2.2.5.3 Business-First Characteristics
- Performance, talent, skills, customer requirements, profitability, revenue and growth drive decisions
- Policies, procedures and norms resemble a **non-family business** and industry standards rather than the family's "familiness"
- Importance to **merit, performance and results** rather than input metrics
- Risk: leads to **family conflict**
  - Example: in a bad year, no salary hike and profits reinvested lowers family lifestyle support and raises perceptions of favouritism and disharmony

#### 2.2.5.4 Finding the Balance
- Effective family businesses need an **optimum balance**; finding it is not easy and takes time
- **No-gos are clearer than do's** (e.g. never create disharmony, because it takes away succession and longevity)
- Systems and practices evolve over time; the business system is strengthened while harmony is maintained
- **Growth ("top line") solves many family issues**: when the business grows, basic and social security needs are met and higher-order needs can be managed

### 2.2.6 Family Business Philosophies and Business Decisions

#### 2.2.6.1 Impact on Key Decisions
| Decision area | Business First | Family First | Balanced View / Family Enterprise Spirit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Entry rules** | Only those with adequate skills and qualification for the specific role | Being a relative is enough; closer the relative, easier the entry | Opportunities for family members based on business needs and, to some extent, skills |
| **Compensation** | Based on job description and market demand and supply | Equal within a generation; varies by generation and age (elder may get slightly more) | Family agrees an acceptable standard and rules by level; skills considered |
| **Stock ownership** | Shared by contribution and performance; can go to non-family | Equal by branch of the family; performance and skills do not matter | Equal values for all, delivered partly as stock and partly as other opportunities or investments |
| **Titles and authority** | Based on merit; one boss, clear hierarchy | Same titles within a generation; indirectly everyone reports to the patriarch who has the final say | Role and hierarchy based on competence, balancing family and business |
| **Governance** | Board of directors, chairman, independent directors | Patriarch decides; at best broad family consensus | More than one forum; a **family council** discusses before final decision; owners have a say |
| **Community role** | Leadership role in the community | Voluntary participation | Active where family, community and patriarch align |

### 2.2.7 Levels of Analysis in Family Businesses

#### 2.2.7.1 Three Levels
| Level | Focus |
|---|---|
| **Individuals** | Family and non-family members; personal values, character, priorities, ambitions, capabilities, expectations |
| **Relationships** | Family, business and ownership relationships |
| **System dynamics** | Interconnection of the family and business systems |

#### 2.2.7.2 Individuals
- Includes family members who may **never be in the business** (not on the org chart) but have an **outsized influence**
- Personal values and dreams shape the business
  - A lavish-spending owner affects the business through personal priorities, not business performance
  - An individual satisfied with 5% growth sets the tone for business and family expenses
- Two people in the same business may approach the same decision differently

#### 2.2.7.3 Relationships
| Relationship | What is traded | What shapes it |
|---|---|---|
| **Family** | **Emotions** (harmony, trust, loyalty, support, protection from anxiety) | Emotion and personal history |
| **Business** | **Competence** | Merit, competence, hierarchy, performance |
| **Ownership** | **Power and influence** | A few owners can chart the direction of the business and the family; probably the most important relationship |

#### 2.2.7.4 System Dynamics
| Dynamic | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Sameness (similarity) and uniqueness** | Common desires across contexts (hold control, pass on wealth across generations); differences driven by **institutional context** (legal structures, cultural values), e.g. India vs Italy, Germany, US |
| **Mutuality** | Members either win together or go down together; families know when to come together and make sacrifices |
| **Competition** | Internal competition is inevitable among cousins, brothers, even husband and wife; the **fundamental attribution error** (ascribing a decision to personality or character) fuels conflict; putting oneself in the other's shoes and evaluating the decision helps maintain harmony |
| **Unintended consequences** | Any action has ripple effects (**butterfly effect**); a harsh word in the office may surface months later in another family context; conflict may trace back years (e.g. a childhood gift); family trades in emotions |

## 2.3 Three-Circle Model

### 2.3.1 Introduction to Three-Circle Model

#### 2.3.1.1 The Model
- A popular framework of **three intertwined circles**: **Family, Business and Ownership**
- Every stakeholder sits in **one, two or three circles**, depending on role
- **Seven stakeholders**; each has own goals, ambitions and expectations; circles also have expectations of each other

#### 2.3.1.2 The Seven Stakeholder Positions
| No. | Position | Circles | Description |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Family owner working in the business** | Family + Ownership + Business (centre) | Typically the patriarch/founder; runs the business and is the largest or sole shareholder |
| 2 | **Family owner not working in the business** | Family + Ownership | E.g. spouse-partner with shareholding but no active business role |
| 3 | **Non-family owner working in the business** | Ownership + Business | Invested or co-founded; usually minority stake; active role |
| 4 | **Family member working in the business, not an owner** | Family + Business | Usually next generation before succeeding; aspires to become an owner |
| 5 | **Non-family owner not working in the business** | Ownership only | Outside investor, not on board or in management; trusts the patriarch; rare, except in listed companies or when forced |
| 6 | **Family member, neither owner nor employee** | Family only | Too young to join yet (later moves to business, then ownership circle); or those married into the family, who remain here (they may now move to the business circle but not to ownership) |
| 7 | **Non-family employee, not an owner** | Business only | Professionals recruited for skills; limit work to business needs |

## 2.4 Ownership Dimension and Powers of Owners

### 2.4.1 Ownership Dimension

#### 2.4.1.1 The Hidden Inverted Pyramid
- The visible pyramid: CEO at top, mid-level managers, lower-level employees
- The **hidden inverted pyramid** above it: **CEO reports to the board; the board is answerable to the owners**
- Layers in a family business: firm-level governance (board) → holding companies through which the family owns the firm → broader ownership (family and non-family owners) → family-level influences (entrepreneurial family, **family assembly, family council, family constitution**)

#### 2.4.1.2 Meaning of Ownership
- Ownership is about **power and influence** and equally about **rights and responsibility** ("with great power comes great responsibility")
- Owners control shareholding and so shape growth, expansion, direction and, indirectly, the family
- Family firms prefer to **retain ownership within the family** and avoid dilution
- Family ownership is predominant worldwide: **65% and above, in some countries up to 90%** of businesses
- Owners are typically founders and descendants

#### 2.4.1.3 Types of Ownership
| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Legal ownership** | Shareholding |
| **Financial ownership** | Ability to determine financial allocations and capital allocation |
| **Emotional ownership** | Shared family name and identity; owner's reputation is at stake; can be more powerful than shareholding |

#### 2.4.1.4 Whose Interests Owners Serve
- The **family** (most important stakeholder), including non-owning and extended family
- **Employees** (treated as family)
- Other stakeholders such as early suppliers and first customers

#### 2.4.1.5 Dilution of Owner Power Over Time
- Number of family owners rises; **voting rights percentages drop** with each generation
- Founder may have been a 100% owner (or partner with spouse); shareholding dilutes
- Individual influence falls, so **cohesiveness and family bonding become more important** and agreement on strategic decisions is expected
- Problems tend to appear once the business reaches a certain size ("when the money is too much")

#### 2.4.1.6 Owner Roles and Investor Types
| Distinction | Types |
|---|---|
| **Approach to role** | **Custodian:** ensures longevity and stability. **Entrepreneur:** drives the journey and growth of the company (e.g. owners behaving entrepreneurially vs custodially) |
| **Level of involvement** | **Passive investor** ("sleeping partner"): owner but no active role. **Active investor:** involved; business is identity ("I am the company"), also possible across later generations |
- Family offices managing mutual funds and not creating in the business are described as **passive investors**
- Every owner needs a **deep understanding of the family's history, legacy and values**; next generation learns early (sitting in the shop, watching treatment of customers, suppliers, employees)

#### 2.4.1.7 Classes of Shares
| Share type | Feature |
|---|---|
| **Class A / Class B shares** | Differ in voting rights and rights over dividends |
| **Separate voting from cash flow rights** | Example: each branch gets **one vote** irrespective of shareholding percentage or number of members |
| **Promoter / founder shares** | Recognise the founder's historical contribution |
| **Special / decision-based voting shares** | Higher voting power on specific decisions (e.g. a marketing decision) |
- Why it matters: with 50%/25%/25% branch holdings, the 50% holder can claim the CEO role through voting potential without being the most qualified

#### 2.4.1.8 The Responsible Owner
- Huge rights and privileges: can **create wealth or send the business down a path of ruin**
- Owners may have to invest, adapt or sacrifice in a bad year, balancing family demands for lifestyle stability with business needs
- The patriarch is seen as singularly responsible for both business and family; the role can be **lonely**; risk and reward are his
- This explains why a patriarch trusts a long-serving early employee over the son

### 2.4.2 Power to Destroy

#### 2.4.2.1 Nature of the Power
- Owners **set the rules, so they can break them**; often a single owner or two (majority plus partner) who need **no approval from anyone**
- Decide direction, vision, strategy, hiring; decisions have long-term bearing
- At the founding stage conflict is usually absent; **conflict deepens in subsequent generations**
- Conflict can arise from **any of the three circles**; family matters can spill over and force detrimental business decisions
- A split intended to preserve a "good enough pie" may leave **both businesses and partners worse off**

#### 2.4.2.2 Sources of Destruction
| Source | Illustration from the module |
|---|---|
| Unclear succession / skill differences between brothers | **Ambani** brothers: the founder died intestate, skill sets varied, mother had to intervene |
| Power used wrongly | **Ranbaxy**: both brothers talented, but power was used in the wrong direction, leading to decline |
| Exclusion in succession without foresight | **Murugappa Group**: a family member excluded from succession pursued a claim to a say and board seat; led to decline |
| Refusal to let go / professionalise | Desire to retain ownership and control and assert authority can destroy the business |
| Wrong choice of successor | Choice between elder vs more capable; son-in-law may be more competent; criteria may not suit every generation |

#### 2.4.2.3 Three-Generation Rule
- Popular view: first generation builds, second stabilises, third causes wealth to disappear
- Research shows it is **not universal**, but it indicates where destruction can start
- Reasoning: first generation builds brick by brick and is frugal; second sees some of the grind and some stability; **third arrives when things are stable, takes lifestyle for granted and cannot relate to the struggle**

### 2.4.3 Power to Create

#### 2.4.3.1 Nature of the Power
- Owners can create **in every generation**, not only the founder; subsequent generations can behave as **entrepreneurs rather than only custodians**
- **Founder:** sees a vision, solves a customer problem, creates a business (still a struggle phase)
- **Second generation:** stability exists, so they seek to **leave their own legacy** (the earlier success is their father's legacy); they have stability and wealth to create
- Inherited wealth left idle (e.g. managed through family offices and mutual funds) is regarded as failing to use the power to create

#### 2.4.3.2 Strategic Redirection
| Example | Change of direction |
|---|---|
| **Reliance** | From oil and petrochemicals to consumer tech and retail tech; data as the new oil |
| **Adani Group** | From trading roots to infrastructure |
| **Bajaj** | Moving away from Chetak to the executive segment and motorcycles; success in two-wheelers |
| **Ola (Bhavish Aggarwal)** | From Ola to e-scooters and AI; outcome still to be judged, but shows the power and wealth to create |
- Creation does not guarantee success; it is the exercise of power

### 2.4.4 Power to Sustain

#### 2.4.4.1 Nature of the Power
- Sustaining both **family and business** over time requires a **competent successor** who understands the dynamics of business and family and has **long-term orientation**
- Requires successors who **fully imbibe the vision and purpose** set by founders; founders must identify next-generation members in sync with the philosophy
- Purpose, vision, succession and long-term orientation are central

#### 2.4.4.2 Illustrations
| Group | Sustaining factor |
|---|---|
| **Tata** | Purpose of being **nation builders**; entered steel, auto, mining as building blocks; culture passed on |
| **Godrej** | Four generations; consistent communication of purpose and vision; broke the three-generation rule, stable despite a recent split |
| **TVS** | Managed transitions across generations with a clear succession; created many businesses (creation role); long-term vision led to being among the first Indian companies to win the **Deming Award** for quality |

#### 2.4.4.3 The Three Powers Together
- **Destroy, create and sustain** span both the ownership and business dimensions and move across **all three circles** (family, owners, business)

## 2.5 Rights of Family Owners

### 2.5.1 Right of Family Owners

#### 2.5.1.1 The Five Rights
| Right | Question it addresses |
|---|---|
| **Design** | How should ownership be structured? |
| **Decide** | Who gets to make important decisions, and how? |
| **Define Value** | What do owners actually want from ownership? |
| **Inform / Be Informed** | What information should owners have and share? |
| **Transfer** | How should ownership move between people or generations? |
- Ownership is a source of **influence**, not just a financial arrangement
- The rights become more important as ownership gets more complex: with concentrated ownership, information and decisions are relatively straightforward; with many branches, information, voting, decision rights and transfer matter much more

### 2.5.2 Right to Design

#### 2.5.2.1 Ownership Structure Matrix
- Two dimensions
  - **Decision-making control:** unified vs shared
  - **Ownership criteria:** exclusive vs inclusive
- The structure may have to change as the family, business and external environment change

| Structure | Control / criteria | Who can own | Works best when | Challenges |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sole owner** | Unified + exclusive | One member per generation holds all ownership | Business needs a **decisive leader**; wealth concentrated in one large asset and other assets can be devolved to the rest | Identifying the one owner each generation; what to give others; successor may be set by age/hierarchy rather than skill; document how wealth devolves |
| **Partnership** | Shared + exclusive | Only some family members who actively participate | Multiple family owners add value; other assets or liquidity exist for non-owners | Rules for entry and exit of ownership group; treatment of non-active members; limiting shared assets to operating companies; buying shares from the previous generation requires **recapitalisation** each generation and reduces investment; may block estate planning vehicles |
| **Distributed** | Shared + inclusive | **Any descendant is eligible** to be an owner | Most wealth is in one family asset; ownership shared across all branches | **Dilution**; no absolute majority; consensus nearly impossible; aligning many interests; active owners may seem distant or lose control |
| **Concentrated** | Unified + inclusive | Any descendant can be an owner, but a **subset holds control** | Business requires decisive action even with multiple owners | Who exercises control each generation; passing control within the branch or to the most qualified (and criteria); keeping non-voting owners engaged |

#### 2.5.2.2 Movement Between Structures
- Typically starts as **sole owner** (a sole proprietorship), often moving to partnership with a spouse, brother or relative (a friend is unlikely)
- **No predetermined path:** can start as a partnership; sole owner or partnership can move directly to distributed or concentrated; distributed can move directly to concentrated
- Drivers: new family members each generation, outside investors in later generations, market forces (e.g. external investor needed for growth)

#### 2.5.2.3 Illustrations of Redesign
| Example | Redesign |
|---|---|
| **Godrej** | Family split the group into two parts: Godrej Enterprises and Godrej Industries Group |
| **TVS** | Moving from cross-holding to more individual family-branch-level ownership |
| **Wipro (Azim Premji)** | 65% of ownership devoted to philanthropy; rest with owners and public shareholders |
- These reflect changes over time, changing family requirements across generations, and the family's purpose and vision

### 2.5.3 Right to Decide

#### 2.5.3.1 Scope
- Covers decisions about the business and **how much decision power is delegated** to other family members and to non-family members
- Two parts: **who decides** and **how decisions are made** (majority, board intervention, single person)
- Clear decision structures are essential to balance financial performance and family harmony

#### 2.5.3.2 Illustrations
| Example | How the right is exercised |
|---|---|
| **Marico (Harsh Mariwala)** | Professionally run; decisions devolved to CEO and management; owner retains strategic board-level oversight |
| **Tata Sons** | Each business managed professionally; TCS run by its CEO who reports to the board; Tata Sons holds a stake and a nominee on each board; only certain decisions come to Tata Sons board |
| **Dr. Reddy's Labs** | **Family council** sets and codifies rules for decisions and governance |
| **Dabur (Burman family)** | Clear rules on family's non-engagement in day-to-day management; professionally run, family owned |
| **GMR Group** | **Family constitution** covering who decides, who comes into the business, qualifications needed, and how inter-family disputes are resolved |
| **Mahindra Group** | Professionally run; owner provides strategic leadership and oversight |

### 2.5.4 Right to Define Value

#### 2.5.4.1 Concept
- Value is created when the business becomes successful, but **success can be financial and non-financial** (nation building, community development, family enrichment, business enrichment)
- Value is about **what owners most want** from the business and must be defined as measurable trade-offs

#### 2.5.4.2 The Value Triangle
- Owners define value in terms of **growth, liquidity and control** (three vertices of a triangle)

| Vertex | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Growth** | Growth of the company and so of the family |
| **Liquidity** | Money owners can take out of the business to maintain the family's lifestyle; money taken out leaves less capital for growth |
| **Control** | Control over the business, chiefly shareholding, which owners cherish most |

- Each side combines two vertices; **owners can effectively choose only two sides**; trying for all three gives a **suboptimal solution**

| Choice (side of triangle) | What is given up | Reasoning | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Growth + Control** | **Liquidity** | Growth needs capital; control rules out external investors; so profits are ploughed back and family lives frugally; typical of founder stage | **Asian Paints**: expansion and capital-intensive plants, founding families kept voting rights; low dividend payout |
| **Growth + Liquidity** | **Control** | Growth plus cash out needs external funding; investors dilute control, owners become answerable, unanimity lost; painful for owners | **DLF**: debt burden led to selling stake and losing control of one company to raise money |
| **Liquidity + Control** | **Growth** | Accept growth at market rate or in a limited geography or segment; reinvestment need is low so cash can be taken out without outside investors | Cement companies (limited reach due to transport cost); **Pidilite** (market leader growing about 9–10% CAGR) |

#### 2.5.4.3 Steps in Exercising the Right
1. Define value on the triangle (where the business is positioned)
2. Articulate a **compelling purpose** reflecting the choices on the three vertices
3. Translate purpose into **specific goals** and create **guardrails**
4. Communicate in an **owner strategy document**
- Purpose acts as the **glue** for family and business and is communicated down generations

### 2.5.5 Right to Inform

#### 2.5.5.1 Scope
- Right to **inform and to be informed**: what, whom, whom not, when and how to inform; some disclosure is forced (regulator)
- Extends across the other four rights (design, decide, value, transfer)
- Applies to family members (owners and non-owners), non-family managers, and disclosure of family-level agreements with business implications
- **Going public** trades privacy for regulatory disclosure (effectively loss of control, a trade-off of liquidity/growth against control)

#### 2.5.5.2 Why and How to Inform
| Aspect | Points |
|---|---|
| **Why owners need information** | To understand where the business is moving and alignment with family vision; to make better decisions; addresses the **agency problem** |
| **Why inform** | Prevents negative emotions, distrust, conflict escalation and breakdown of cohesion; new family and professional entrants must understand the owner's purpose and trade-offs |
| **How** | **Structured** flows (family constitution, family council) build trust; **informal** channels can produce "I was not told, but I learnt about it", harming emotions |
| **Transfer information** | Telling early sets expectations (e.g. unequal branch sizes when one brother has one child and the other three) and gives time to prepare |

#### 2.5.5.3 Illustrations
- **Asian Paints (late 1990s):** one founding-family CEO decided to sell his entire stake to a competitor's parent and did not inform the other three families; FDI regulatory constraints let the others block it; exercised right to decide and right not to inform
- **Mukesh Ambani:** publicly disclosed which child will take care of which business, forestalling disputes and setting expectations
- **Listed family firms (e.g. Dabur):** public disclosure norms are met, but how the family manages its own conflict is not public; firms with a family structure are relatively more stable

### 2.5.6 Right to Transfer

#### 2.5.6.1 Scope
- Covers **how and when to exit ownership** and pass it across generations: who gets it, when, how, the whole process and structure
- **When** is the stickiest point: the current generation prefers to stay as long as possible

#### 2.5.6.2 Reasons for Delay
- Whether the next generation is **ready**
- Whether enough authority can be devolved
- Whether successors have **earned respect** (respect cannot be bought by ownership)
- Loss of stability and identity for the exiting owner

#### 2.5.6.3 Choices in Transfer
- **Inform:** not whether but **when, how and whom**; to owner-group members, non-owner family members, professionals, and non-family or listed-company shareholders
  - Failure to inform or plan caused turmoil (Dhirubhai Ambani died intestate; Sanjay Kapoor's family assets in turmoil)
  - Planned transfers: **Max Group** (timeline announced and repeated), **Cipla (Hameed family)** structured transition of leadership roles and equity control
- **Whom:** one sibling may inherit the business and another **equivalent assets**
- Assets are **tangible and intangible**; disputes can be over the **family name** (e.g. Hero Group, Krishna Sweets)
- Even on a sale to outsiders, families may protect legacy by retaining the family name as the company name (Italian design families)

## 2.6 Family Dimension

### 2.6.1 Family Dimension: Introduction

#### 2.6.1.1 Scope of the Family Circle
- Family is about **relationships and emotions**; primary aspiration is **harmony and cohesion**
- Extends beyond owners and employees: non-owners, those who married in, the next generation not yet owners, relatives influencing through relationships
- Shown through a **genogram** (family tree); some members become owners, some stay family only
- Indirect influence through marriage (the spouse's relatives)

#### 2.6.1.2 Types of Family
| Family type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Family of orientation** | The family one is born into; shapes values and culture |
| **Family of procreation** | Created after marriage (self, spouse and children); couple sets the rules |
- A person belongs to both simultaneously; norms differ, but both must maintain **harmony and cohesion**
- Membership can be withdrawn if harmony is not maintained
- Small differences are papered over, unpleasant matters avoided at gatherings, forums meet less often, and **cohesiveness decreases**; the drift may take a generation or two to become apparent (sometimes triggered by the marriage of a second child)

#### 2.6.1.3 Family Science
- Explains behaviour of individuals in a family setup
  - Elder vs younger sibling (younger must fight for resources; harder for the middle child)
  - Gender expectations about passing on the business; changing, with daughters now leading (Apollo Hospitals, Kinetic, Parle, Bisleri)
- Reinforces family as a system of interrelated actors influencing the business

#### 2.6.1.4 Four Stages of the Family Dimension
| Stage | Features and key issues |
|---|---|
| **1. Young business family (young owner)** | Sole proprietor or partnership; focus on **building the business and its survival**; young family; building capital and **social capital**; time for family; children may not identify with legacy if parents give little time |
| **2. Entering the business** | Parents about **35 to 55**; children in teens to late teens; business more stable; midlife challenges; children **craft their own identity**; elder child expected to join, younger may pursue own path (with capital available) |
| **3. Working together** | Elder generation about **50s to 65**; younger about **20 to 40/45**; siblings, parents, possibly sons-in-law or daughters-in-law work together; issues of **trust, openness, reliability, communication mechanisms**; different work ethic and skills; "seeds of conflict" sown; possible three generations competing for identity and space |
| **4. Passing the baton** | Founding generation typically **above 60**; preparing to exit; how to manage transition and **whom to pass leadership to** (eldest, most skilled, who will make it last and grow); implications for ownership and business circles |

## 2.7 Business Dimension

### 2.7.1 Business Dimension: Introduction

#### 2.7.1.1 Overview
- A family business is still a business: customers, resources, people and markets; as it grows, operation must change (professional management and governance)
- Business life cycle has **three stages**: **startup, expansion, maturity**

#### 2.7.1.2 Three Stages of the Business Dimension
| Feature | Startup | Expansion | Maturity |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Structure** | Decision-making around **one individual**; very informal | Formal structure needed as complexity rises; **owner-manager role evolves** | Stable organisation structure; senior management; divisional or functional |
| **Products / markets** | Typically one product, one market | More than one product, more regions | Stable customer base |
| **Resources / money** | Uncertain; funding needed; works frugally; money not taken out | Growth pangs; need resources to keep pace with demand | Growth tapered; well-established routines; low uncertainty and risk |
| **Key issues** | **Survival of the firm**; balance aspirations with reality | **Professionalisation**: delegation (family or outside professionals), financial planning, budgeting, strategic management, systems and processes; avoid dependence on one individual | Governance systems; refocus (resources spread thin), choose directions; commitment of owners and managers; reinvestment; maturity of individuals; do not forget original purpose |

#### 2.7.1.3 Startup Specifics
- Family business does not aim to become a "unicorn" quickly; aims to survive (e.g. the 1,000-day milestone) with **patient capital** and building for the long run
- Extended family and community often supply the **first customer, employee, investor and supplier**, giving stability and a networked value chain

#### 2.7.1.4 Linking Business and Family Stages
| Business stage | Comparable family-side position |
|---|---|
| Startup | Sole owner / early partnership; young business family |
| Expansion | Next generation settling in; may include outsiders |
| Maturity | Working-together stage; many family members attracted to the business ("magnet" effect) |
- **Passing the baton** is more likely in the third (maturity) stage, sometimes the second
- The ownership circle explains powers and rights; the family circle explains the family system; the business circle explains the business stage

## 2.8 Stages of Family-Business Evolution

### 2.8.0 Overview of the Four Stages

#### 2.8.0.1 The Four Stages
| Stage | Who is in control |
|---|---|
| **Controlling owner** | One founder (sometimes a couple), control with one person |
| **Sibling partnership** | The founder's children (siblings) |
| **Cousin consortium** | Cousins across branches (about one generation later, approx. 30 years apart per generation) |
| **Family federation** | A very large number of more distant family members |
- At each stage: who drives the business, issues, frictions, how resolved, changes needed
- **Not every family passes through all four stages**; usually a family starts in stage 1 or 2 (e.g. two brothers start together); starting at the cousin stage is rare
- These are ways of understanding evolution, not compulsory stages in a fixed order

### 2.8.1 The Controlling Owner Stage

#### 2.8.1.1 Features
- Ownership usually with **one person** (could be a couple with a ratio); control with one
- Founder engrossed in **building from scratch**: finding customers, value proposition, raising funds, building a team and structure, raising a family
- Concerns: growing the business to a **meaningful size** for successors and family support; getting the successor in; and **how to exit**
- Margins between family and business are small

#### 2.8.1.2 Characteristics and Risks
| Aspect | Description |
|---|---|
| **Decision-making** | **Autocratic**, by whims and fancies; **no governance mechanism**, no board; works in family and business |
| **Growth** | Limitations built in unless the founder is open to external investment |
| **Succession** | Unclear: needs enough scale to be meaningful; founder reluctant to let go because **identity is linked to the business** |
| **Dependence risk** | Extremely high dependence on one person; **no contingency plans**; founder health an issue |
| **Boundaries** | Very blurred; **capital flows freely** between family and business with no distinction of spending |
| **People** | Extremely loyal, informal; employees aware growth will be low for them unless the company grows fast |

### 2.8.2 The Sibling Partnership Stage

#### 2.8.2.1 Ownership and Management Control
- Ownership usually **divided equally** between two or more siblings
- Management control rests with some or all; not always equal due to different age, caliber, skills
- Over time, usually **one sibling controls**, in most families the **elder brother**; appointing the younger brother needs strong reasons

#### 2.8.2.2 Business Issues
- A process for **shared control** (who does what; discuss before communicating to employees; otherwise employees can play siblings against each other)
- Intended model is "first among equals", but typically the eldest calls the shots
- Skill differences cause friction; the eldest may lack the right skills
- Siblings who see no room may exit: roles of owners in business vs owners outside business must be clarified
- Capital retention: those in ownership and management should not take capital out
- Controlling **family branches** (spouses pressure over spending, roles, preservation)

#### 2.8.2.3 Emotional Undercurrents
| Undercurrent | Description |
|---|---|
| **Childhood roles** | Elder brother's directive tone carries into the office; younger sibling may become a **rebel** |
| **Favouritism** | Patriarch partial to one child; plum projects and roles go to the favourite, not necessarily the most skilled |
| **In-laws and spouses** | Influence at the **dinner table**, not the boardroom; requests (e.g. employment for a relative) are hard to resist; costs the business bears |
| **Ownership vs pay** | Ownership equal, pay may not be; differences become contentious after years in business |
| **Rivalry** | Unspoken; questions of competence, contribution, next CEO, financial benefits, who leads next generation; managed well, rivalry is **good for the business** if it does not get out of hand |

#### 2.8.2.4 Patriarch's Role
- Make things **equal for all children** (e.g. a car or house for each); no competence argument applies to assets
- Make the **future clear**: which sibling gets which business or role; absence of a will leads to disputes (Dhirubhai Ambani died intestate; Sanjay Kapoor)
- Unclear succession affects business, employees, dependents and the family

### 2.8.3 The Cousin Consortium Stage

#### 2.8.3.1 Shareholding and Authority
- Many cousins from multiple branches; not all in the business
- **Shareholding fragmented**: no single branch can control or chart direction; buy-in needed from other branches
- Many opinions (cousins and their spouses); consensus is hard
- To reduce friction, families **split into smaller businesses** (e.g. real estate into residential, commercial, retirement homes), each with a single head, plus a **holding company** controlling finances and assets
- Some cousins work outside and remain small owners
- **Founder's authority weakens** considerably (may still come to office daily)

#### 2.8.3.2 Weakening of Common Ground
- Siblings grew up in the same household with the same culture; cousins grew up in **different households** with different rules and lifestyles
- Households become more nuclear; expectations of what to get from the business differ
- **Loyalty to the family name declines** as relationships fragment along with shareholding

#### 2.8.3.3 Frictions
| Friction | Description |
|---|---|
| **Active vs passive ownership** | Active cousins want profits **reinvested** for growth; passive owners (path outside) expect **higher dividends and payouts** |
| **Rivalry between family branches** | Finite number of roles; branches compete for roles regardless of skill, for **board seats and investment** from the holding company (HUF karta decides; an excluded woman successfully claimed a say in the Murugappa case) |
| **Competency gap** | Not all cousins are equally skilled but expectations stay high; harmony prioritised, so **everyone is compensated equally** irrespective of skill gaps |

#### 2.8.3.4 Positions Within the Consortium
| Position | Wants | Biggest fear |
|---|---|---|
| **Active CEO (cousin)** | Reinvest profits, reduce dividends, grow the company | **Interference** from other branches; board gang-up blocking decisions |
| **Active cousin (non-CEO, often younger)** | Meaningful role in a division or function | **Being undermined** by other branches; performance questioned; role handed to another branch |
| **Passive owner** | Extra dividends to reinvest in his own outside venture | Capital that could be reinvested being taken out and not used well (concern of others) |
| **Younger cousin** | Recognition, due importance despite skills | **Short shrift**: not taken seriously (the "Chotu"); left with outside investment as only option |
| **Senior cousin** | Stability of family and business | **Loss of legacy**; more wedded to the family name |
| **Minority branch** | A voice | **Not being heard**; being dominated despite useful inputs |
| **Professional CEO (non-family, non-owner)** | **Autonomy** to run the business | Good proposals **shut down by the board** because the family cannot agree; must forge consensus |

### 2.8.4 Family Federation Stage

#### 2.8.4.1 Features
- Extension of the cousin consortium: next generation, more distant cousins, **very large number**
- Individual shareholdings **very small and negligible**; no one can influence alone
- Business typically **hived off** into multiple companies or areas, each handled by a branch or family member
  - **TVS Group** as an example: about 10 areas (tyres, motors, fasteners, insurance), **cross-holding** with the main company plus significant share in own company

#### 2.8.4.2 Governance Mechanisms
- Requires **governance of a very high order**; informal relationships and a board alone are not enough
- Families build a **family forum**, write a **family constitution** (what can be done, who can join, skills required), and set up a **family council** that decides more, alongside the board
- The shift is from relying on relationships and informal understanding to **structures** supporting a large, complex system

## 2.9 Module Summary

### 2.9.1 Module Summary

#### 2.9.1.1 Integrating Ideas
- **Same decision, two consequences:** decisions on who joins, who receives ownership or who leads can carry both a business and a family consequence (hierarchy, fairness, loyalty, expectations)
- **Family vs business hierarchy differ:** age or generation matters in the family; capability and performance matter in the business
- Family may treat members equally; business may need to differentiate; **neither system can be ignored**
- **Life cycles do not move together:** a family may handle succession while the business is expanding; a mature business may have a family still working through ownership and participation; understanding both cycles explains why a structure works at one point and becomes difficult later
- As ownership disperses, decision-making must evolve: founder decides directly; siblings can discuss together; cousin groups and federations need deliberate mechanisms for **communication, decision-making, ownership alignment, roles, succession and governance**

## Quick Revision Summary

### Key Definitions
- **Family–business system:** family system + business system + their interconnection; boundaries are blurred
- **Family system:** emotional, trust and loyalty based, equal treatment, generation/age hierarchy, unwritten rules
- **Business system:** customer, performance, talent, merit, formal hierarchy
- **Family First / Business First / Family Enterprise Spirit:** emphasis on family needs / performance and competence / balancing both
- **Levels of analysis:** individuals, relationships (family: emotions; business: competence; ownership: power and influence), system dynamics (sameness, mutuality, competition, unintended consequences)
- **Three-circle model:** family, business, ownership; **seven** stakeholder positions
- **Hidden inverted pyramid:** CEO reports to board; board answerable to owners
- **Ownership types:** legal, financial, emotional
- **Owner roles:** custodian vs entrepreneur; passive (sleeping partner) vs active investor
- **Three powers:** destroy, create, sustain
- **Five rights:** design, decide, define value, inform/be informed, transfer
- **Family of orientation** (born into) vs **family of procreation** (created by marriage)

### Important Formulas / Frameworks
- No numerical formulas in this module
- **Value triangle:** vertices = **growth, liquidity, control**; owners can choose **only two sides**
  - Growth + Control → give up **Liquidity**
  - Growth + Liquidity → give up **Control**
  - Liquidity + Control → give up **Growth**
- **Ownership structure matrix:** unified vs shared control x exclusive vs inclusive ownership → sole owner, partnership, concentrated, distributed
- **Family stages:** young business family → entering the business → working together → passing the baton
- **Business stages:** startup → expansion → maturity
- **Evolution stages:** controlling owner → sibling partnership → cousin consortium → family federation

### Must-Remember Points
- Family trades in **emotions**, business in **competence**, ownership in **power**
- Family harmony supports longevity; family is "socialist", business "capitalist"; "top line solves many problems"
- Family-first: equal pay/titles, patriarch final say, entry by being a relative; business-first: merit and market pay; balanced: family council and agreed rules
- Power of owner dilutes with each generation, so cohesion and governance become more important
- Three-generation rule is not universally supported by research
- Controlling owner stage: autocratic, no governance, blurred family-business capital, unclear succession, high dependence on founder
- Sibling stage: equal ownership, unequal management/pay; favouritism, in-laws, rivalry; patriarch must keep things equal and make the future clear
- Cousin stage: fragmented shareholding; frictions are active vs passive owners, branch rivalry, competency gap; founder's authority weakens
- Federation stage: tiny holdings, multiple businesses, cross-holdings, family forum, family constitution and family council
- Not every family passes through all four stages; usually starts at stage 1 or 2
- Family and business life cycles do not move together

# Module 3: Succession in Family Firms

## 3.0 Module Overview

### 3.0.1 Welcome to the Module

#### Scope of the Module
- **Succession** is a critical transition in a family business: it involves much more than handing over a leadership role.
- It can include transfer of **management responsibility, ownership, authority, relationships and family expectations** across generations.
- The challenge is balancing **business continuity** with **family interests**.

#### Three Perspectives Covered
| Perspective | Focus |
|---|---|
| **Fundamentals** | What succession is, why it is complex, actors, factors and challenges |
| **Incumbent's perspective** | Emotional and financial investment, timing of handover, exit pathways, gradual shift of responsibility, why letting go is hard even when a successor is identified |
| **Successor's perspective** | Willingness and ability to join, entry paths, qualities expected, earning credibility, stakeholder responses |

### 3.0.2 Learning Objectives
- Learning objectives are provided in the module as an image; the topics below cover the full module content.

---

## 3.1 Fundamentals of Succession

### 3.1.1 What is Succession

#### Key Actors
- **Incumbent:** the person at the top who currently manages the family business and is expected to give up the position.
- **Successor:** a **family member or a professional** who takes over the family firm, its management and day-to-day operations.

#### Definition
- **Succession** is a **gradual process** (not a single event) through which **control, decision-making authority, management responsibility and often ownership** move from an incumbent to a successor.
- It can take **decades**; it starts very early (grooming) and the "handover day" is itself vague.
- A formal event may mark or celebrate the succession, but the real process begins well in advance.

#### Two Dimensions of Transfer
| Dimension | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Management transfer** | Who runs the business and takes day-to-day decisions |
| **Ownership transfer** | Who holds ultimate rights, shareholding, voting power and influence |

- A **complete succession** = transfer of **both ownership control and management control**.
- If only one is transferred, it is a **partial transfer**.
- Ownership transfer is usually the **second** transfer, not the first.

#### Why Process, Not Event
- Control and influence shift gradually so that business and family stay stable and decision-making continues smoothly.
- The incumbent must also prepare: give up control and decision authority and take a back seat without disturbing the business.
- Succession must also occur in the **family circle**: the successor becomes the **de facto head of the family** and the family must back this person; trust, cohesiveness and harmony with a new person take time to build.
- Succession is seen as **taboo** because it is read negatively (incumbent is "too old", "frail", "can't manage"), instead of positively (successor has come of age, knows new technology and markets).

### 3.1.2 Ownership vs Management Transfer: Example

#### Core Idea
- Complete succession involves both transfers, but they **do not happen at the same time**.
- An incumbent may hand over day-to-day management but keep **ownership and voting rights**, or ownership may shift while management authority stays with the incumbent.
- **Real power transfers with ownership**; management control is given partially and slowly, but the final say rests with the owner (voting rights).

#### Illustrative Cases
| Firm | Management | Ownership |
|---|---|---|
| **Wipro** | Rishad Premji (chairman) handles day-to-day operations without seeking approval | Azim Premji still holds far more voting power; bulk of his shares moved to the foundation; Rishad holds roughly 10% of what his father holds |
| **Walmart** | Professionals took over after Sam Walton | Walton family retains majority stake |
| **Tata Group** | Professionals run all group companies (e.g., TCS, Tata Motors) | Tata Sons holds ownership control; Tata Sons is owned by Tata Trusts; family presence is at Tata Sons |
| **Mars** | Professionals plus family members as CEOs of divisions | 100% privately held by the family; family board and family council |
| **BMW** | Professionals manage everyday operations | Family holds in excess of 40% and has voting rights; management follows owners' guidelines |
| **Marico** | Harsh Mariwala stepped down from executive role in 2014; Saugata Gupta became CEO | Mariwala family retains close to 60% |
| **Dabur** | Professionals manage; no Burman on the executive committee or at CXO level | Burmans hold in excess of 60% and influence direction |

#### Link to the Three Circles
- Succession spans the **ownership, family and business circles**; both family members and professionals play roles.
- Family may hand day-to-day business to professionals while retaining ownership, with a policy on family involvement (working alongside professionals at top level, or taking a back seat while staying engaged).
- Both incumbent and successor need clarity on how **each** transition is happening.

### 3.1.3 The Three-Generation Rule

#### Definition
- **Three-generation rule:** popular idea that family businesses do not survive beyond three generations ("shirtsleeves to shirtsleeves in three generations").
- Quoted statistics: about **30%** move into the second generation, **13%** into the third, and only about **2-3%** survive beyond.
- In the original survey, success meant the **family controls the business and it is still running** beyond three generations.

#### Reasons Offered
| Reason | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Ownership fragmentation** | $100\%$ with the founder, then $50\%$ each to 2 children, then $25\%$ each to their 2 children each; no one has a majority and decision-making suffers |
| **Behavioural factor** | Elder sibling joins earlier; third generation arrives when the business is already well-established, with nothing left to build and its own aspirations |

#### Why the Rule Needs Caution
- Statistics treat a family as **failed** if it no longer runs the business, has sold it, or is not significantly involved; this definition of survival **ignores changes in ownership, management or business structure**.
- The rule can influence behaviour: third-generation members may worry and choose to **sell/exit "when going is good"** unnecessarily.
- It is a heuristic that needs more research.

#### Counter-Examples
| Firm | Continuity |
|---|---|
| **Hermes** | Founded 1837; now sixth generation (executive chairman and CEO Axel Dumas); resisted takeover attempts including the LVMH attempt |
| **Cargill** | Survived 5-6 generations and is still private |
| **Ford** | Fourth and fifth generation; family holds around 40% ownership but, through **Class B shares** (differential voting rights), has disproportionately larger voting control |
| **Tata, Godrej** | Indian groups lasting more than a century |
| **Others** | Shapoorji Pallonji, RP Goenka Group, Lalbhai Group, Arvind Group; Japanese firms lasting 1,000 years |

- Over generations a family may have diversified and a different business may have become the core, or the original core may remain.

### 3.1.4 Types of Succession

#### Overview
- Three models differ in whether succession goes to **one or several** successors.
- **One successor:** primogeniture or ultimogeniture. **Multiple successors:** coparcenary.

| Type | Meaning | Key features | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Primogeniture** | **Eldest** (male) child becomes successor | Groomed early; siblings expected to follow the eldest; widely practised; Japan allows the son-in-law to take over if there is a daughter | Shapoorji Pallonji Group; News Corp (Murdoch family, Lachlan as sole chairman); Ford (Henry Ford I to Edsel Ford to Henry Ford II) |
| **Coparcenary** | **Ownership shared** among multiple family members | Ownership is joint but management needs role clarity (individuals must hold specific roles); Indian form is the **Hindu Undivided Family (HUF)** | Rothschild, Hinduja brothers, RP Goenka Group, Henkel |
| **Ultimogeniture** | **Youngest** child chosen as successor (broadly: moving beyond the first child) | Elder siblings are superseded; reasons: favourite child, elder siblings unwilling or lacking ability; protects family wealth | JCB (Lord Bamford appointed the younger son as heir apparent) |

#### Coparcenary in the Indian Context (HUF)
- Legal form of coparcenary; the law lays out who can be a coparcener.
- Traditionally male descendants are coparceners; daughters-in-law are not; the father is typically the **karta**.
- All coparceners have **equal rights**; in India "equal" is clearly defined, while abroad splits can be **unequal** (incumbent defines shareholding) yet everyone remains an owner.
- The **2005 Succession Act** brought women within the law; this reflects women taking active roles (e.g., Apollo Hospitals, Pratap Reddy to his daughters) and questioning of patriarchy. The Murugappa Group case (Valli Arunachalam taking the group to court) is a landmark for women's ownership and management rights.
- **Hinduja motto:** everything belongs to everyone and nothing belongs to anyone.
- **Henkel:** controlling equity held by more than 150 family members who must behave as a **unified voting pool**.
- Core challenge: getting all coparceners to agree.

#### Distinguishing Ultimogeniture from Division of Businesses
- Giving each sibling a separate company in a group (Rahul Bajaj: Bajaj Auto to Rajiv, financial business to Sanjiv; Adani Group divisions) is **not** a pure case of primogeniture or ultimogeniture.

#### Implications for Longevity
- **Primogeniture / ultimogeniture** conserve capital within the family and support capital formation; **coparcenary divides** and fragments it.
- Coparcenary fragments individual/branch influence and dilutes each person's share of wealth, unless family governance retains family influence.
- An incumbent may pass the business to the eldest son and give equivalent assets to the younger son, protecting both business and family.
- The real challenge is **replicating the transition across generations**; whichever model is chosen, a **broader family governance structure** is needed to keep influence, control and wealth (to beat the three-generation rule).
- The model can change across generations (e.g., coparcenary at one stage, primogeniture in a branch later).

### 3.1.5 Factors Influencing Succession

#### Overview
- Factors operate at **four levels**, viewed from the successor's perspective: **individual, family, family firm, society/community**.

| Level | Factors |
|---|---|
| **Individual** | Opportunity to **extend legacy**; entrepreneurial ambition; **early exposure** and emotional bond (sitting in the shop/office as a child); parents' values, culture and relationship with the child; **self-efficacy/confidence**; education and prior work outside; independent entrepreneurial ambition |
| **Family** | Family culture and values; how other branches and earlier members joined; grooming from early age; identification with family purpose; **number of siblings** and birth order (eldest often expected to join, youngest has choice); **gender** expectations; **family unity** (fights among brothers may deter the successor) |
| **Family firm** | **Size** and growth headroom; **number of businesses** (can give business-level control to different siblings, offers stability and legacy opportunity) |
| **Society and community** | **Uncertainty avoidance** (high-avoidance cultures push successors toward the stable family business); entrepreneurial orientation of the culture; community business orientation; caste/community craftsmanship passed only within the community; national-level influences on identity and role |

#### Illustrations of Individual Entrepreneurial Pull
- **Hacco ice cream:** family sold to the Lotte Group; the successor set up a new business after the non-compete period ended.
- **Puneet Dalmia:** started an online portal (jobsite.com) before returning to the Dalmia family business.
- Entrepreneurial ambition can take the focal firm in new directions, create a new brand in the same industry, or take the successor down a wholly different path; these factors change over time.

#### Note on Scope
- In a professionally managed or loosely held firm only **management succession** matters; in a family firm the family level affects individual-level factors too, because all **three circles** are involved.

### 3.1.6 Succession as a Process

#### Stages of the Process
| Stage | Successor role | Incumbent role |
|---|---|---|
| **Early induction** | Listener and observer (childhood visits to shop/office, dinner-table discussions); imbibes purpose, values, commitments, discipline | Sole operator |
| **Helper** | Odd jobs the incumbent dislikes (meeting distributors/dealers, collections); learns ground-level data; "earns stripes" | Behaves like a monarch; delegates grunt work |
| **Manager** | Oversees others but has little real authority; employees may bypass to the incumbent ("big boss") | Oversight over the new manager and his team |
| **Leader** | Takes decisions, shows command through pivotal decisions | **Consultant/advisor**; fewer decisions |

- The incumbent's role **diminishes** while the successor's role **increases**; the successor's line **lags** because the incumbent does not let go easily (emotional and behavioural reasons).

#### What Gets Delegated First and Last
- Incumbent delegates departments he dislikes or does not understand first (e.g., sales, digital marketing).
- **Finance, accounting and legal** are retained longest: "money is the final control".
- Investments (e.g., moving from manual books to Tally or an ERP) still need the incumbent's approval, so the successor must convince him; this is a chance to earn respect.

#### Building Credibility
- Employees may play a "cat and mouse" game, checking with the incumbent on expenses and investments.
- Working **outside** (employment or own venture) and returning earns greater respect from employees (not seen as thrust by bloodline), though the incumbent may still hold back finance.
- **Pivotal decisions** act as evidence of command (example: Bajaj Auto shutting down the Chetak to focus on motorcycles), and make it easier for the incumbent to move on.

### 3.1.7 Succession and Commitment

#### Importance
- The successor's commitment shapes how smoothly succession unfolds: it makes handover easier because the incumbent sees the business is in safer hands.
- Commitment must cover the **family's purpose and values**, not just the successor's own plans (the successor is a custodian).

#### Four Forms of Commitment
| Type | Meaning | Key phrase | Typical situation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Affective** | Genuine desire to contribute; identifies with purpose and goals | "I want to" | Desire plus requisite skills; also called effective commitment, the strongest bond |
| **Normative** | Felt obligation/family expectation to join | "I ought to" | Eldest son in primogeniture; norms reinforced at family functions and supplier meetings |
| **Calculative** | Weighs pros, cons, alternatives and trade-offs | "What is in it for me?" | Numbers exercise: family business versus outside job |
| **Imperative** | Family business is the only or safest option | "I have no alternative" | Doubts about own skills; risk-averse; business is ready-made and stable |

- **Any combination** can strengthen the successor's buy-in; the aim is to move motives over time toward **affective** commitment.

### 3.1.8 Impact and Pathways in a Transfer

#### Impact of Transfer
- A transfer is **irreversible**, and its effect spans multiple generations.
- It affects **leadership, wealth, governance and family relationships**; all three circles are impacted.

#### Three Pathways
| Pathway | Description | When used / implications |
|---|---|---|
| **Sale to an outsider** | Outside company takes over and decides what is best | Usually the **last choice**. Reasons: next generation uninterested or pursuing own path; competitive situation the family cannot handle; owner tired; amazing offer; conflict among incumbent partners (e.g., brothers choose family harmony over the business) |
| **Divide among next generation** | Split by division, branch, territory, or split family wealth/assets | Common in large business houses (e.g., Haldiram's divided by territory); gives each member clarity, **autonomy** and reduces conflict |
| **Transfer intact** | Whole business passed on undivided; run jointly without ownership split | Works while harmony lasts; **less autonomy**, conflict taken as a matter of time; **formal governance** far more important |

#### After a Sale
- **Buyer:** commercial purpose; may transform culture and values, resell or integrate it.
- **Seller:** must find a **new identity** (can no longer be "former owner"); seller can screen buyers for track record on values, culture and employee care, but has little say after the sale.

#### Key Elements of a Transfer
| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Assets** | Shareholding is one asset; also land, buildings, property, movable financial wealth; classified as business/non-business and financial/non-financial; design of who gets what shares |
| **Roles** | Defines next-generation autonomy and freedom to act without stepping on others |
| **Incumbent's glide path** | Succession is a journey; incumbent plans how to let go and picks up activities outside the business (many retain a small office) |
| **Preparing the organisation and stakeholders** | Customers, suppliers and others; choosing the right successor (competence, character, personality) is the incumbent's responsibility |
| **Building next-generation capability** | Knowledge of ownership structure, financials, books, laws, tax and, first of all, family purpose, values and culture; handover of responsibilities that are monitored; pathways into governance roles (council, board) to observe and learn; lifelong-learning mindset |

#### Planning the Transfer
- Treat it as a **joint process**, not the incumbent's own agenda and timeline.
- Fix an **end date** by which a significant portion of responsibilities are handed over, then **work backwards**; set clear benchmarks.
- Incumbent gives up least-comfortable responsibilities first; **finance, books and auditor relationship** go last.
- Transfer is a three-circle transfer: the successor must balance and nurture all three dimensions.

#### Reasons Transitions Fail
| Reason | Description |
|---|---|
| **Incumbent will not let go** | Stays in the background; employees still treat him as the boss |
| **Rigid expectations** | Next generation must run the business by the incumbent's rules; theoretical but no functional autonomy |
| **Wrong choice of successor** | Hierarchy, loyalty and trust placed above skills; owners have power to destroy the firm |
| **No transfer at all** | "Last man standing" runs the business after the owner passes away |

### 3.1.9 Why is Succession Difficult

#### Three Elements of the Process
1. **Define and communicate** the guidelines, principles and mechanisms.
2. **Plan and manage** the process.
3. **Coach, mentor and train** the successor.

#### Common Challenges
| Challenge | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Clone syndrome** | Incumbent wants the successor to be a literal clone: same competencies, skills, way of working and values; a clone is very hard to find |
| **Letting-go syndrome** | Incumbent holds on strongly; identity and emotional investment tied to the business; sees no path for self after transition (especially founders) |
| **Founder's shadow** | Founder's influence lingers after handover; expectation to run it as if the founder were present, also from employees, vendors, suppliers and clients who want continuity |
| **Successor's cage** | Successor feels trapped in the family business and wants other paths (e.g., singer, movie star); family funding of ambitions may not be available |
| **Stakeholder communication** | Vendors and clients are like an "external family"; they must be told what is transferring, when, and how relationships will be affected |
| **Emotion and identity** | Letting go carries emotion and an identity issue; founder must also coach and mentor |
| **Culture and motivation gaps** | Gaps in both incumbent and successor across the three systems |

- Succession is an **irreversible decision with long-term implications** for all systems, which makes these challenges more serious.

### 3.1.10 Key Dimensions of Succession Stories

#### Measuring Success
- Judged through the impact on the **family, firm and key stakeholders** (three-circle view).

| Dimension | Indicators |
|---|---|
| **Family** | Greater unity, stronger bonding, rising engagement, no conflicts; **harmony** is highly valued |
| **Stakeholders** | Turnover of employees (especially long-term), clients and suppliers; engagement with the social and wider community |
| **Firm** | Performance, growth, return on capital, profit margin, profitability, market position, sustainable growth |
| **Entrepreneurial development** | New initiatives, who is engaged, ability to raise external funding, engaging external stakeholders in new opportunities (entrepreneurial family galaxy view) |

### 3.1.11 Framework of Best Practices for Managing Succession

#### Four Stages
| Stage | Content |
|---|---|
| **1. Plan for succession** | Communicate plans; define entry and selection criteria |
| **2. Identify and groom potential successors** | Train early; assess regularly; involve appropriate outsiders in evaluation |
| **3. Select the successor** | One of the groomed candidates is chosen; roles formalised |
| **4. Complete the handover** | Close the process; ownership transfer completed alongside leadership transfer |

#### Cross-Cutting Dimension
- **Relationship management** runs across all four stages: within the family and with external (family and non-family) stakeholders.

### 3.1.12 Ten Key Tensions

#### Definition
- Successful succession requires balancing **competing priorities** rather than choosing one side; the balance changes as business and family evolve. Managing them supports **transgenerational prosperity**.

| # | Tension | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Ability vs willingness** | Willingness is the drive to use resources better; ability is competence to invest them well |
| 2 | **Family vs business** | Family has emotional needs; business has rational needs |
| 3 | **Togetherness vs separateness** | Togetherness stresses collectivism, shared vision and values; separateness requires separating family role from business role for rational decisions |
| 4 | **Tradition vs innovation** | Keeping close to origins and past methods versus new ways and new business lines |
| 5 | **Transparency vs secrecy** | How much to share with family and outsiders; founders tend to keep information secret |
| 6 | **Equity vs equality** | Family expects equal treatment and assets; business logic gives resources to where needed |
| 7 | **Control vs autonomy** | How much control leader/incumbent retains versus autonomy for family and non-family members |
| 8 | **Continuity vs new growth (roots vs wings)** | Staying rooted in family and founder values and vision versus freedom to find new growth avenues |
| 9 | **Commitment vs competence** | Next generation expected to be committed, but competence is also needed; committed people expect higher rewards |
| 10 | **Liquidity vs growth** | Money taken out of the business (owner's right to value) leaves less capital for growth; prioritising growth means a more frugal life |

## 3.2 The Incumbent's Perspective: Passing the Baton

### 3.2.1 Letting Go of the Baton: The Incumbent's Dilemma

#### Why It Is Difficult
- Incumbent built the business and is **emotionally, financially and psychologically invested**; identity and company are closely linked.
- **Social status**, networks, power and respect come from the leadership position.
- Fear of "what do I do with my time?" after a lifetime of work.
- Even when the market has changed, the successor is better suited, or health demands it, letting go remains an **emotional** challenge, more than the state of the business.
- Many firms keep a **founder's office/corner office** (e.g., sits two hours a day, charity or foundation matters, meeting old employees) without taking part in daily decisions.

#### Types of Incumbent Exit
| Type | Behaviour | Impact |
|---|---|---|
| **Emperor** (refuses to leave) | Stays until the end, holds the last word, overrules the successor even in front of employees; exits only through force (health) or death | Successor not groomed or planned for; decisions slow; capable successor cannot take the final call; firm and successor weakened |
| **General** | Exits but watches closely, keeps internal connections, waits for (or engineers) the first failure to return; overturns the successor's decisions on return | Operational chaos, undermined successor, lowered respect for the family, employee uncertainty |
| **Ambassador** | Plans the exit well in advance; after formal exit serves as **mentor or advisor** without taking control | Organisation stronger: gains incumbent's wisdom plus successor's energy |
| **Governor** | Mentors during the last few months in office, then makes a **clean break** and takes up other roles (civic bodies, NGOs, school, trust) | Smooth handover with a clean break |

- Difference between ambassador and governor: ambassador continues as mentor after formally stepping out; governor mentors while still in office and then leaves fully.

### 3.2.2 Right Time for Handover

#### Principle
- Timing depends on the **readiness of both** incumbent and successor.

| Scenario | Incumbent / Successor | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Too early** | Incumbent in 40s at peak energy; successor in teens or early 20s, just out of education, no experience | Successor is a helper at best, identity torn between peers and business, undisciplined; incumbent has years to return and revisit decisions; setup for failure and instability |
| **Ideal** | Incumbent in 50s; successor late 20s to early 30s, 7-8 years in business, settled identity, possibly married | Incumbent's struggles are over, business stable, identity clear and less competitive; successor understands market and technology; devolve a division, new geography, new product or factory first (finance retained) |
| **Too late** | Incumbent in late 60s; successor around 40 with 10-15 years in business, eager to take over | Tension if no retirement plan or significant handover has begun; both want to be "firmly in the saddle"; refused successor may temper approach; harmony and cohesiveness hit |

- Absence of retirement plans makes giving up control harder.
- The right time is when **both generations are prepared** for a gradual and well-planned transfer.

### 3.2.3 The Process of Handing Over

#### Four Steps for the Incumbent
| Step | Actions |
|---|---|
| **1. Prepare self and business** | Prepare emotionally, financially and on governance; understand requirements across the **ownership, management and family circles**; assess gaps and fill them. Prepare the incumbent, successor, family and business together |
| **2. Assess successors** | Decide whether management goes to a **professional or family member**; plan ownership transfer (co-partner arrangement or full transfer); choose among children by **willingness and ability**; check readiness of family, business and governance to support the choice |
| **3. Ready the successor** | Groom in advance across the three circles; evaluate over time; incumbent starts **letting go** (tests the waters, not everything); give **independent decision-making rights** so the successor owns consequences; look for **early wins** |
| **4. Transition across three circles** | Coordinate transfer of authority across business, family and ownership (pace may differ); make the successor the **public face** to external stakeholders (events, family functions, key conversations) |

#### Role of Early Wins
- Early wins are **evidence** that the business is in safe hands and give the incumbent confidence to move out.
- The incumbent can set the successor up for a win, but the successor must not usurp decisions from an effective professional team set up to support them; doing so weakens the organisation.

## 3.3 The Successor's Perspective: Ascension

### 3.3.1 Qualities and Attributes Required of a Successor

#### Succession as Ascension
- For the successor, succession is an **ascension**: becoming the leader across the **family, business and ownership** systems; the successor lives in all three.
- Poor succession erodes family wealth and penalises successors without proper credentials; succession is about **earning the role, not inheriting it**.

| System | What the successor must handle |
|---|---|
| **Family** | Belonging, fairness to the family, shared history |
| **Ownership** | Shareholding, dividends, steward of the organisation |
| **Business** | Performance, direction, professional structure, competitiveness |

- Navigating three systems creates conflicts of **logics, loyalties and emotions**.

#### Five Stages of the Ascender's Journey
1. **Intention:** "Do I want to do this?" (incumbent to hand over and successor to take over).
2. **Preparing:** "Am I ready?"
3. **Proving:** do family, employees and external stakeholders trust and believe in my credibility?
4. **Accepting the transfer:** accepts handover and gains control.
5. **Stewardship:** becomes the steward who builds the next generation of leaders and the next-generation version of the company.

- Throughout, the incumbent's role shrinks from active decision-maker to advisor/consultant.

#### Attributes Incumbents Look For
| Importance | Attributes |
|---|---|
| **Most critical** | **Integrity** and **commitment** to the business |
| **Very important** | Experience in the family business; respect from employees; intelligence to make decisions |
| **Also considered** | Birth order and gender (impact changing over time) |
| **Softer aspects** | Relationship with incumbent; standing in the family; relationships with family and employees; competence/skills; personality traits |

- Blood guarantees a foot in the door; **qualities** are needed for the doors to open wider and gain respect.

#### Five-Part Profile of the Ascender
| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| **Character** | **Humility** (accept starting at the bottom rung; learn from longer-serving staff; ask more than tell); **respect** (earned, not automatic; respect hierarchy and founder's legacy; go bottoms-up); **integrity and accountability** (accept mistakes, do not blame others, accept feedback) |
| **Curiosity** | Visit customers, shop floor, suppliers; study competitors; ask "why do we do it this way?" before transforming; understand unit economics, profitability and every part of the business; be open to feedback |
| **Commitment** | Shaped by whether selected or volunteered; **staying power/resilience**; aim to shift motive toward affective commitment |
| **Competence** | Skill in financials and business metrics; ability to manage people older and longer in the business |
| **Literacy across three systems** | Family (relationships, networks, roles, fair treatment of branches); ownership (dividend, liquidity, capital needs, other shareholders' expectations, responsible owner); business dynamics |

#### Tensions Faced by the Ascender
| Tension | Pair |
|---|---|
| Humility vs decisiveness | When to be humble, when to take bold decisions |
| Tradition vs innovation | Honour legacy but adapt; moving too fast labels the successor a rebel |
| Curiosity vs focus | Over-curiosity spreads effort; focus on elements needing real investment |
| Patience vs urgency | Expected to learn before changing, yet pressure to change quickly |
| Family loyalty vs professionalism | Balance between family and business |

- Balance lies in between; **failure lies at either extreme**.

### 3.3.2 Willingness-Ability Matrix

#### Definitions
| Dimension | Meaning | Nature |
|---|---|---|
| **Willingness** | Drive to accept responsibility; sense of purpose for the firm; readiness to take important but unpopular decisions; strong desire to lead and continue the legacy; a psychological state | Comes from within; cannot be brought in from outside ("heart") |
| **Ability** | Competence: proven skills, performance, experience, track record; decision-making and judgement under pressure; functional and industry expertise; record in family business or outside | Can be built and taught over time ("head") |

#### The 2 x 2 Matrix
| | Low ability | High ability |
|---|---|---|
| **High willingness** | **Eager apprentice** | **Ready leader** |
| **Low willingness** | **Not (yet) a fit** | **Reluctant talent** |

#### Profile of Each Quadrant
| Quadrant | Characteristics | Recommended handling | Risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ready leader** | High commitment and skills; track record (inside or outside) | Stretch assignments; little coaching; give authority and let run; monitor with metrics and evidence | Tension if the incumbent will not let go while the successor wants more authority |
| **Reluctant talent** | Ability displayed outside (challenging roles) but ambivalent about entering the family firm | Surface motives and expectations; **redesign the role** that fits own values, culture and family purpose (win-win) | Forcing entry leads to loss of motivation, disengagement, resentment and toll on family harmony |
| **Eager apprentice** | Willing, gaps in competence | **Development plan**: leadership programmes, MBA, outside experience, right mentor, stretched roles; **small wins**; slow, measured increase of authority | Sheltering an heir, loyalty over competence, visible early failure costs employee respect and can set back the succession |
| **Not (yet) a fit** | Pressured by expectations; skill gaps; sense of entitlement | Bring in a **temporary professional CEO**, hope ability or willingness improves; consider an **ownership-only (passive/active owner)** role; or look outside | Employee morale falls; other branches or members may stage a coup; business must not be risked |

#### Dynamics
- The matrix is **not static**: succession is a process, so successors move between quadrants through development, experience, stronger commitment and support.
- An **eager apprentice** is a better fit than a reluctant talent: ability can be bought/built, willingness cannot; stakeholders see ability improve more easily than commitment; a reluctant talent sees small stumbles as blocks and withdraws faster.
- Overdoing the process (micromanaging, constant evaluation) can turn a **ready leader into a reluctant leader**.

#### Head vs Heart (Family Logic vs Business Logic)
| Logic | Drives | Preference |
|---|---|---|
| **Family logic** | Commitment (willingness) | More family members in the business for trust and family-oriented culture |
| **Corporate/business logic** | Competence (ability) | Education, proven work experience; common today for next generation to work elsewhere first |

- Outcome depends on circumstances, which branch is powerful, the firm's position and the family's **dominant logic** (e.g., large families with many shareholders still choosing professional management).

#### Applies to Multiple Stakeholders
| Stakeholder | Willingness | Ability |
|---|---|---|
| **Successor** | Do I want this, for the right reasons? | Skill and credibility brought in |
| **Incumbent** | Willing to let go? | Can manage the transition, coach and mentor |
| **Family** | Wants one leader who ticks all boxes (conflict if several do) | Can govern, avoid conflict, keep ownership safe |
| **Non-family employees, clients, suppliers** | Willing to follow and stay on | Can complement the successor for the next phase |

### 3.3.3 How Succession Moves from Operation to Ownership

#### Three Layers of Control
| Layer | Scope | Handover |
|---|---|---|
| **Operational control** | Running the business day to day: execution, dealing with employees, one division/brand/territory, P&L, delivery, hiring | **First**; acts as a test; mistakes are forgivable and reversible |
| **Strategic control** | Long-term vision, board involvement, governance, investments and capital allocation; judgement | **Second**; successor takes part in decisions, but the incumbent may still take the final call |
| **Ownership control** | Shareholding, voting rights, dividend rights, inheritance decisions, rights and responsibilities of an owner | **Last**: irreversible, affects family, business and ecosystem; mistakes cannot be reversed |

- Progress depends on **earned trust and credibility**, not just time or age.

#### Traps in Each Layer
| Layer | Trap |
|---|---|
| **Operational** | Responsibility without authority |
| **Strategic** | Successor can only propose; incumbent keeps final signing authority ("shadow over the chair") |
| **Ownership** | Transfer too late (dependent on the founder) or too early (leverage lost) |

#### Control in Practice
- **Operational:** measured on brand/territory P&L as de facto CEO, **consistency and trend** of results, team confidence, attrition and morale.
- **Strategic:** successor-led strategy first; then split between chairperson and CEO; work with independent directors; finally CEO/MD and chairperson roles. The board watches quality and data backing of proposals, openness to dissent and feedback, and reporting.
- **Ownership instruments:** voting versus **non-voting shares** (non-voting often first in structured families); **shareholder agreement** when there is more than one successor; family consensus; training for the owner role; estate planning; family office; redrafted constitution; external advisors and estate planners; country regulations and tax structures.

#### Implications and Planning
- Successor may get responsibility before authority; **proven credibility** gives negotiating power over decision rights.
- Start with a title and hands-on role; master each layer before the next; prepare for the ownership role before becoming an owner; keep a personal chart of milestones and timelines.
- Plan by **milestones rather than birthdays**; leadership on **merit**, ownership governed by **fairness**.
- Protocols should be **documented**, not verbal, so contingencies are covered; define the incumbent's role after ownership transfer.
- Incumbent may lose identity after full transfer; an **independent director or advisor** can act as a referee.

#### When the Sequence Is Disturbed
| Situation | Effect |
|---|---|
| Estate planning gives ownership before the heir has the role | Ownership and leadership thrust on an unprepared successor |
| Sudden vacancy (death of patriarch/matriarch, possibly without a will) | Plan goes haywire; need a mediator and interim protocols; unsettles suppliers, customers, employees |
| Owner consciously retains ownership until death | Avoidable; owner should work toward transfer actively |
| Younger sibling catapulted into ownership through a split | Ownership without operating experience |

- Strong advisor and team support (all three systems) matters; **Birla Group** after Aditya Vikram Birla's sudden demise (Kumar Mangalam Birla and supporting team) is a successful unplanned transition; absent advisors lead to turmoil.

### 3.3.4 Earning the Right of Succession

#### Inheritance vs Earned Mindset
| Aspect | Inheritance mindset | Earned succession |
|---|---|---|
| **Basis of authority** | Belongs to the family; respect for title | Competence and character first |
| **Path** | Fast-tracked, sheltered roles | Real roles, demonstrated competence and accountability |
| **Trust** | Assumed and demanded | Based on predictable performance, accumulated over time |
| **Stakeholder stance** | Compliance and watchfulness | Voluntary endorsement of stakeholder responsibility |

#### Types of Legitimacy
| Type | Question | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| **Pragmatic** | Does this leader serve my interests? | Visible results and deliverables |
| **Moral** | Is this person fair and principled? | Integrity, fairness, sacrifice |
| **Cognitive** | Is this simply how things are? | Taken-for-grantedness; takes time and consistency to be accepted as "one of us" |

- Legitimacy must be **conferred by validating stakeholders** who watch the successor's narrative (vision, interaction) and actions (strategic action, visibility).

#### Credibility Architecture
| Element | Question |
|---|---|
| **Competence** | Track record, functional depth, results delivered |
| **Character** | Humility, fairness, accountability for mistakes |
| **Relationships** | Endorsement beyond the incumbent: employees, family |
| **Contribution** | Contribution to each of the three circles |
| **Base: predictability** | Words and actions aligned repeatedly across all four |

#### The Ladder of Credibility
| Step | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **1. Listening** | Learn names, numbers, customers, suppliers |
| **2. Contribution** | Small visible wins build confidence |
| **3. Owning** | Take ownership and accountability for a unit or project |
| **4. Leadership** | Set direction at board/strategic level |
| **5. Stewardship** | Last and slowest step; build the next set of leaders |

#### Principles and Pitfalls
- Underpromise and over-deliver; expand the scope of decisions gradually; avoid starting with a big transformative decision; the incumbent should allow freedom to succeed or fail and adapt (how the successor responds to failure is observed).
- Employees watch: accountability, listening first, consistency, same standards applied to self, investment in hard skills (finance, technology), willingness to hold **difficult conversations** (including when the founder does not back the successor, and in crises).
- Pitfalls:

| Pitfall | Description |
|---|---|
| **Too conservative** | Replicates founder's legacy, resists needed change |
| **Rebellious** | Sets the past aside and builds an entirely new system; discards proven practices and alienates veterans |
| **Wavering** | Awkward, inconsistent mix of old and new |

- Do not change what works well; where change is required, explain why and the fallout if it does not happen, and implement it to earn credibility.

### 3.3.5 Paths of Succession

#### Framework
- Axis 1: strategic objective: **renew or create new value** versus **continue** the existing business.
- Axis 2: where it happens: **core business**, **entrepreneurial family galaxy**, or **outside**.
- Seven paths are described below.

| Path | Description | Strengths | Weaknesses | Fits when | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Parachute to the top** | Little exposure, appointed straight to the top (heir without credentials) | Clear structure; no uncertainty about the boss | No credibility or legitimacy; stakeholders watch every test, knowledge gaps, relationships | Sudden vacancy; works with a strong board and advisors | **Kumar Mangalam Birla** after a death in the family |
| **2. Bottom-up** | Entry-level role, climb the hierarchy | Deep knowledge of operations and relationships; peer respect | Takes longer; stuck with "owner's son/daughter" label; never seen as a peer | Business with a lot of **tacit knowledge**; long horizons | **Brian Roberts at Comcast** (summer job supervisor made clear he would be judged on merit) |
| **3. Job rotation** | Rotate through functions or businesses | Integrated view; understands teams' ambitions and constraints | Too shallow; disrupts teams (now report to him); results not attributable; in-depth knowledge questioned | Multi-unit, multi-division businesses | Family federations |
| **4. Work outside, then return** | Education, work in another professional organisation, return to a defined role, progress to ownership | Proven track record and fair peer comparison; new skills, benchmarks, networks; return seen as by choice | Re-entry culture shock; outsider label; loss of tacit knowledge if late; reduced commitment | Independent test of capabilities, or new skills help the firm | **Rishad Premji**: Harvard MBA, 2 years at Bain, 4 years at GE Capital, joined Wipro in 2007, chairman in 2019 |
| **5. Own venture outside, then return** | Start a venture outside, return later | Learn fundraising, P&L, people, risk, resource hunting; in tune with today's customer needs; decision-making matters more than the venture's success | Seen negatively if return is due to the venture's failure | Family business at a crossroads needing entrepreneurial talent; **return by strength** is best | Not specified |
| **6. New venture within the family galaxy** | Lead a new venture using family brand, capital and network, then feed it to the core | A "sandbox" with family resources; credibility without displacing incumbents; creates leadership seeds for multiple heirs | Competes with core for capital and attention; soft treatment ("pet project"); governance complexity | Diversified or large family with multiple potential successors | **Reliance**: Akash (Jio platforms), Isha (Reliance Retail), Anant (new energy) |
| **7. Forge own path within the family business** | Create and scale new ventures inside the family business (renewal) | Skills of scaling from zero to one and one to ten; finance available; own P&L gives legitimacy | Failure is highly visible; resistance from old-timers; needs **ring-fencing** | Core needs renewal or recharging | **Anand Mahindra** (hospitality, real estate) |

### 3.3.6 Tests for the Successor

#### Who Tests
- Family members, employees, customers, suppliers and other stakeholders continuously evaluate the successor.

#### Types of Tests
| Test | Description | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Qualifying** | Age, work experience, education, outside experience, skills | Basic, explicit and checkable |
| **Circumstantial** | Unplanned challenges or crises | Reveal **character and judgement**; cannot be scheduled or prepared; outweigh birthright |
| **Self-imposed** | Ambitious goals set by the successor (e.g., doubling sales in a year) | Watched against the stated goals; changing goalposts withdraws legitimacy |
| **Political** | Challengers undermine the leader's influence in a power contest across three systems | Informal; respond by communicating the vision honestly, building alliances/coalitions and setting clear governance mechanisms |
| **Invisible/soft** | Fairness (favouritism), loyalty to legacy and values, market reliability, employees' sense of security | Judged by employees, suppliers, customers, the market |

#### Mapping the Tests
| | **Within successor's control** | **Beyond successor's control** |
|---|---|---|
| **Explicit/designed** | **Qualifying tests** | **Formal reviews** (by external audiences, e.g., board evaluation, family-council milestones) |
| **Implicit/informal** | **Self-imposed** and **political** tests | **Circumstantial tests** |

- A **self-diagnostic** for ascenders helps identify strengths, weaknesses and what to strengthen.
- Formal review mechanisms add structure so the successor can learn and receive credible feedback; the goal is not to eliminate every test.

### 3.3.7 How Stakeholders Respond to the Successor

#### Four Major Responses
| Response | Who | Behaviour | Advantage / risk | Successor action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Protect, guide, mentor** | Loyal veterans, close allies of the incumbent, elders in the family (less often siblings) | Guidelines, guidance, more chances | Faster learning; **overprotection** questions legitimacy | Use air cover with caution; stretch yourself |
| **Enable change** | Younger family peers, younger managers, outside directors, professionals | Support renewal | Mandate to renew and strengthen the core | Decide what to change, **pilot**, build quick wins, scale gradually; communicate what will **not** change |
| **Wait for stumble** | Rival cousins, managers who wanted a professional, non-family members, investors | Appear to comply but fall short, blame external factors; "we told you so" if you fail | Many return to back a winner after early wins | Create **early wins**, communicate, build relationships |
| **Leave the family firm** | Relatives, high-potential non-family professionals, incumbent's confidants who see no role | Choose to exit | Loss of knowledge and relationships | Retain by design: **role clarity, incentives, ownership access**; if exit occurs, capture knowledge and treat it with **dignity** |

#### Other Responses
| Response | Successor action |
|---|---|
| **Actively rebel or resist** (coalitions, open disagreement, boardroom battle, e.g., Tata Sons going public) | Address the issue, do not take it personal, build alliances |
| **Hedge** (open line to incumbent while appearing to support successor) | Clarify roles and reward where due |
| **Go directly to the incumbent** | Actively talk with the incumbent to define roles and flow of decisions |
| **Probe** (small deliberate challenges) | Respond consistently; do not overreact |

#### Stakeholder Heat Map
- Maps important stakeholders against the likely responses; focus on the **likely** responses and those from key stakeholders, not rare ones.

### 3.3.8 Equipping the Successor for the Role

#### Knowledge
| Type | Content |
|---|---|
| **Knowledge to leverage** (already in the firm and family) | Tacit business know-how; relational knowledge (suppliers, customers); history, origin stories, founding principles and reasons for past decisions; family system knowledge (personalities, alliances, unwritten rules) |
| **Knowledge to acquire (gaps)** | Management, finance, industry and technology, people leadership, governance and decision-making, wise use of ownership (shareholder agreements, estates), family-enterprise programmes |

#### Networks
| Ring | Content |
|---|---|
| **Inner circle (inherited)** | Incumbent, family, close confidants |
| **Business networks (inherited, then rebuilt)** | Customers, bankers, suppliers, managers |
| **Outer ring (self-built)** | Peer, mentor, advisor, alumni networks, industry bodies (example: Ram Charan as mentor to Harsh Mariwala) |

- Networks can be mapped on a 2 x 2: **family vs non-family** by **internal vs external**.

#### Three Hats
| Hat | What must be learned |
|---|---|
| **Manager** | Strategy, competitive position (USP), markets and customers, finance, operations, digital transformation, technology, leading people |
| **Owner** | Decision-making and governance, working with the board, capital allocation, risk management, liquidity, regulatory matters (tax, estate), stewardship and legacy |
| **Family leader** | Intergenerational conversation, avoiding conflict while maintaining fairness and equity, abiding by family constitution and council, developing the next generation |

- **Trap:** over-investing in the manager hat and hoping to repair the other two later. Each hat needs different competencies; specialised family-business programmes help because a family-business leader differs from a professional manager.

#### Working with the Incumbent's Departure Style
| Incumbent style | Successor's response |
|---|---|
| **Monarch/emperor** (will not leave; exit only by overthrow or death) | Negotiate authority and **put it in writing**; obtain board or governance safeguards |
| **General** (leaves reluctantly, plots a comeback) | Work with the board on clear guardrails on any return; give a clear **advisory role** and office so the incumbent cannot encroach |
| **Ambassador** (strong ties, mentor) | Leverage ties as advisory input while retaining operational and strategic decision rights |
| **Governor** (set timeline, clean break) | Honour the exit, value contributions, communicate the legacy, invite advice when needed |

#### Mutual Role Adjustment
| Incumbent | Successor |
|---|---|
| Monarch to overseer to delegator to consultant/advisor | Helper to manager to leader |

- One climbs while the other descends. Rules: a basic agreement on how the process and decisions will work during the limited tenure; written, structured dialogues for each stage; credit the incumbent and agree on the next role as advisor; over time, the successor asserts rights.

#### Confidants
- Long-serving employees in the incumbent's inner circle hold knowledge of workings, relationships, power and money, and see themselves as automatic confidants of the successor.
- Dilemma for both: are they a threat or opportunity; for the confidant, align with the outgoing incumbent or switch loyalty.
- Confidants act as **gatekeeper/bridge**; the successor needs a set of dos and don'ts when navigating them.

#### Establishing Leadership Identity
- Leader, not "son/daughter of the leader": the ascender stakes a claim through narrative and action, and stakeholders grant recognition.
- Phases: **intention** (identify with family and firm), **nurturing** (internalise the role through support and learning), **transfer** (manage conflict among the roles of manager, family leader and owner, and move from child to leader).

#### Support Systems
| Support | Function |
|---|---|
| **Family constitution** | Rules of entry and conduct for the family |
| **Family council** | Threadbare discussion; equitable decisions that keep business in mind |
| **Independent directors** | Advice and mentoring |
| **Shareholders' agreement (SHA)** | Protects successor's ownership rights; covers exits and liquidity |
| **Development plan** | Own milestones from operational to strategic to ownership readiness |
| **Feedback loop** | Feedback from stakeholders and course correction |

- Successor should identify gaps in each of the three hats, how to fill them and what evidence will show they are filled.

#### The Journey in Summary
1. **Prepare:** build willingness and ability.
2. **Entry paths and tests:** be ready for tests under control and prepare for those that are not.
3. **Earn respect and credibility** through demonstrated performance.
4. **Lead:** sequence from operational to ownership control, moving from the incumbent's generation to the next.
5. **Steward:** as owner and family leader, prepare the business for the next phase of transition.

- It is a continuous process, working in tandem with the incumbent; the successor is judged by **many judges in three courts** (family, business, ownership) over many years.

## Quick Revision Summary

### Key Definitions
- **Succession:** gradual process of transferring control, decision authority, management responsibility and often ownership from incumbent to successor (family member or professional).
- **Complete succession:** transfer of both **management** and **ownership** control; ownership is usually the second and last transfer.
- **Incumbent / Successor (ascender):** current leader expected to hand over / person who takes over.
- **Three-generation rule:** belief that family firms rarely survive beyond the third generation (about 30% reach gen 2, 13% gen 3, 2-3% beyond); to be treated with caution because "survival" is narrowly defined.
- **Primogeniture:** eldest child; **Coparcenary:** shared ownership (HUF in India, karta as head); **Ultimogeniture:** youngest child.
- **Commitment types:** affective (want), normative (ought), calculative (weigh), imperative (no alternative).
- **Transfer pathways:** sell to outsider, divide among next generation, transfer intact.
- **Incumbent exit types:** emperor (refuses to leave), general (leaves but plots return), ambassador (mentor after exit), governor (mentors, then clean break).
- **Syndromes/challenges:** clone syndrome, letting-go syndrome, founder's shadow, successor's cage.
- **Willingness** (from within, heart) and **Ability** (can be built, head); matrix: ready leader, eager apprentice, reluctant talent, not (yet) a fit.
- **Control layers:** operational, strategic, ownership.
- **Legitimacy:** pragmatic, moral, cognitive.
- **Tests:** qualifying, circumstantial, self-imposed, political (plus invisible tests).
- **Three hats:** manager, owner, family leader.

### Important Formulas
- No numerical formulas in this module. Ownership dilution illustration with 2 children per generation: share per heir after $n$ generations $= 100\% \times \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{n}$ (100% to 50% to 25%).

### Must-Remember Points
- Succession is a **process (often decades)**, not an event; the incumbent's role shrinks while the successor's grows, with the successor lagging.
- Finance, accounting and legal are the last functions the incumbent releases; ownership is the last control layer released.
- Ideal handover: incumbent about 50s, successor late 20s to early 30s with experience; too early and too late both create tension.
- Four levels of factors: individual, family, family firm, society/community.
- Four-stage best-practice framework: plan, identify and groom, select, hand over; relationship management throughout.
- Ten tensions: ability/willingness, family/business, togetherness/separateness, tradition/innovation, transparency/secrecy, equity/equality, control/autonomy, continuity/new growth (roots/wings), commitment/competence, liquidity/growth.
- Transfers fail when the incumbent will not let go, expectations are rigid, the wrong successor is chosen, or no transfer is made.
- Success is judged on family satisfaction, stakeholder satisfaction, entrepreneurial development and firm performance.
- Seven successor paths: parachute, bottom-up, job rotation, work outside then return, own venture outside, new venture in family galaxy, forge own path within the family business.
- Four stakeholder responses: protect/mentor, enable change, wait for stumble, leave; plus rebel, hedge, go to incumbent, probe.
- Ladder of credibility: listening, contribution, owning, leadership, stewardship.
- Support systems: family constitution, family council, independent directors, shareholders' agreement, development plan, feedback loop.

## 4.1 Part 1: Working Together

### 4.1.1 Family Relationships at Work

#### Why Relationships Compound
- Five family members share **10** one-to-one relationships; fourteen share **91**.
- Relationships multiply as generations pass and as siblings and cousins enter the business (one-to-many, many-to-one).
- A single pair may be sibling, co-owner and board member at once (e.g., a brother who is also a co-owner, a father who is chairman).
- Each relationship is critical and valued; they fall into the three circles: **family, business, ownership**.

#### Three Systems: Currency and Question

| System | Trades in | Question asked |
|--------|-----------|----------------|
| **Family** | **Emotion** | "Do we belong?" |
| **Business** | **Competence and skill** | "Can you do the job? Can you get it done?" |
| **Ownership** | **Power** | "Who decides, and who benefits?" |

- Logic, membership rules (how one enters or exits) and questions differ across the three.
- Every major decision touches all three: the family expresses emotion, the business expresses logic, ownership holds the power to decide.

#### Strength or Liability
- A family firm has "one firm and two rough edges": the same feature can be a **strength or a liability**.

| Feature | As strength | As liability |
|---------|-------------|--------------|
| **Shared history** | Develops deep trust | Old grievances keep resurfacing |
| **Emotional bonds / shared identity** | Loyalty, commitment | Reluctance to change or exit |
| **Long horizon** | **Patient capital** | Resistance to change |

#### Research on Relationships and Performance
- **Harmony norms and fairness** curb the **Fredo effect**.
- More sons lower performance after the founder's death; equal heirs need **governance** around them.
- Where relationship conflict lowers performance, **invest in goodwill**.

### 4.1.2 Roles, Hats and Branches

#### Overlapping Hats
- Family members wear different hats: **owner, manager, family member**; there are **seven roles** in the three-circle model, and several can collide depending on where one sits.
- Relationship questions change with stage of the family business:

| Stage | Key question |
|-------|--------------|
| Young business family | Who counts as family in the firm? |
| Next generation entering | Is there a fair employment policy? |
| Working together | How do siblings, in-laws and outsiders cooperate? |
| Passing the baton | How does anyone exit with dignity? |

- In the **entrepreneurial family galaxy**, more entities mean more relationships and more hats (manager, owner with fiduciary duty to firm and family, family leader).
- **Example:** a brother and sister can be siblings, fellow board members, chairperson vs. husband-of-sister CEO, trustee, beneficiary and foundation co-director. **Firing a brother-in-law touches all the roles he plays.**

#### Genogram and Branches
- A **genogram** maps family branches and shows who owns, who works in the business, or neither.
- G1 -> G2 -> G3: the second generation creates different **branches**.

| Branch design | Effect |
|---------------|--------|
| Shares split by branch first, then among branch heirs | **Branch size drives control** |
| **Equal seats** per branch on council/board | Small branches over-represented |
| **Proportional seats** (follow ownership) | Large branches dominate; small ones disengage |
| **Branch holding company** (each branch votes through one vehicle) | Extra layer; politics move upstairs |
| **Separate assets/businesses by branch** | Loss of scale; need non-compete and brand rules |

#### Position Shapes Stance (Dividend Cut for New Plant)

| Person | Likely stance | Reason |
|--------|---------------|--------|
| **Family owner-manager** | Supports | Sees growth, legacy, control |
| **Non-working family owner** | Hesitates or opposes | Depends on dividends for income |
| **Non-family manager** | Supports (or quietly supports) | Growth brings jobs, promotions, budgets |
| **Spouse of owner** | Worried | Household cash flow, children's future |

- Avoid **attribution error**: instead of "he is stubborn", ask **which circle the person sits in**.

#### The Four Rooms

| Room | Cares about | Role |
|------|-------------|------|
| **Owner room** | Power and influence | Elects the board; sets who may own the firm |
| **Boardroom** | Wisdom and judgment | Hires the CEO; approves strategy |
| **Management room** | Competence and execution | Runs the business day to day |
| **Family room** | Love, harmony, unity, development | Descendants, spouses, next generation |

- Family room options: a **council of four to seven members** elected by the family; a **family assembly** every year or two; its own mandate (unity, education, family policies, development).
- The family room runs alongside the other rooms but **they do not report to the family**.

### 4.1.3 Fairness and Justice in the Family Firm

#### Four Kinds of Fairness
- In a family firm each judgment is made **twice**: by kith and kin, and by non-relations.

| Fairness | Asks | Covers |
|----------|------|--------|
| **Distributive** | Are outcomes fair? | Pay, dividends, shares, jobs, titles |
| **Procedural** | Was the process fair? | Voice, clear rules, same rules for all |
| **Interactional** | Was I treated with respect? | Tone, honest explanation, dignity |
| **Retributive** | Are consequences fair? | Same discipline for all, family and non-family |

#### Distributive Rules: Equality, Equity, Need

| Rule | Basis | Strength | Weakness |
|------|-------|----------|----------|
| **Equality** | Same for all | Builds unity | Ignores effort |
| **Equity** | Contribution | Rewards effort | May split the family |
| **Need** | Requirement | Kind | Can reward dependency |

- Decide **which rule applies to which resource before the conflict**.
- Common pattern: **ownership follows equality; pay follows equity; hardship is handled by need via a family fund.**

#### Procedural Fairness: Six Rules
1. **Consistency**
2. **Lack of bias**
3. **Accuracy** (good information)
4. **Correctability** (a way to appeal)
5. **Representation and voice**
6. **Ethics** (fits shared values)

#### Interactional Fairness: Four Parts
1. **Truthfulness**
2. **Respect**
3. **Propriety** (no insults or irrelevant hints)
4. **Justification** (give reasons)

- Family test: "**Would you say it in this way to a non-family manager?**"
- Publish the process before the decision; deliver the decision with reasons.

#### Fair Process
- Three steps: **engage, explain, set expectations**.
- Applied in **five domains**:

| Domain | Question |
|--------|----------|
| **Ownership** | Who may own; how shares pass on |
| **Family membership** | Who counts as a member (family of procreation/orientation, co-opted in-laws) |
| **Recruitment and careers** | Entry, pay, promotion |
| **Management transition** | Who leads next; how decided |
| **Family participation** | Does the family meet, separate conflict from the issue, give everyone a voice, share information at adequate intervals |

- For each domain check: were people **engaged, told why, and told what happens next**?

#### When Fairness Fails
- Disturbed harmony norms + unfair distribution + role ambiguity lead to **relationship conflict**; a mediator should focus on fairness and clarity.
- **Fredo effect:** (from The Godfather) incompetence, opportunism or dubious ethics of a relative harms the firm.
- Lever: **clear rules and visible fairness before conflict starts.**

### 4.1.4 Family Employment Policy

#### Why a Written Policy
- A policy **pre-decides contentious issues** and sets fair rules for every relative; it protects the individual, the family and watching non-family colleagues; generational transitions become easier.
- **Omar's story** (no decisions made): nobody attracted him, no entry rules, no career path, no feedback (patriarch gives none; employees cannot give feedback to the boss), pay is "take what you want"; four years without a review, little pay progression, exit in frustration. Worst for someone who worked outside in a structured company.
- Questions a policy should answer: Is every relative entitled to a job? Qualifications? Who supervises, nurtures and monitors? Are spouses welcome? What if it does not work out?
- **Most families write down only the entry rule.**

#### Six Decisions

| Decision | Content |
|----------|---------|
| **1. Attract** | Handpick talent; risks: discouragement, disengaged future owners; avoid bias |
| **2. Entry** | Entry rules |
| **3. Career path** | Roles and sequence |
| **4. Feedback** | Development and review |
| **5. Pay** | Salary, board fees, dividends |
| **6. Exit** | Agreed in advance |

#### Entry Rules
- **None in:** relatives are passive owners with no say but enjoy ownership benefits (a glass ceiling may cap power).
- **All in:** every family member is entitled to enter; then rules on paycheck and roles are needed to avoid resentment.
- Other questions: level playing field and no favouritism; higher or lower bar than outsiders; where in-laws fit; role of gender (make it explicit).

#### Career Path (Support x Earned)

| | Low support | High support |
|---|-------------|--------------|
| **Favour-based** | **Undefined path**: random outcomes | |
| **Earned on performance** | | **Custom path** (best): support to succeed, promotion earned, not entitled |

- Common mistake: "he is family so he can **parachute** into any role".

#### Feedback and Development
- Typically no feedback and no development attention; extra coaching often via an **outside director or coach**.
- Best practice: supplement company systems with **anonymous 360-degree feedback** (boss, peers, direct reports).
- **Coddling** is the most destructive system: little feedback and lack of merit is tolerated. A person who has worked only in the family firm and answers yes to many coddling indicators is likely coddled.
- Remedy (from parent, individual and non-family colleague): **merit** gives acceptance from the wider ecosystem.

#### Pay

| Pay level | Effect |
|-----------|--------|
| "Take what you want" | No market anchor |
| **Below market** | Owners gain but frustration sets in |
| **Above market** | Other family members dislike it; leaves less capital for growth |
| **Equal pay for all** | Mixes ownership, merit and pay |
| **Ideal** | Pay by merit, market and role |

#### Three Flows of Money

| Flow | Pays for | Basis |
|------|----------|-------|
| **Salary / bonus / incentive** | The job | Performance |
| **Board fees (sitting fees)** | Governing | Paid each board sitting (usually quarterly) |
| **Dividends** | Capital invested | Owner room only; linked to profits |

#### Exit
- Decide upfront; written measurable targets; if not performing, exit should be communicated beforehand.
- "Job for life" raises questions on merit and performance; top roles held by family can block non-family talent and limit skills.
- **Each room stands for something different** (family room: values and hopes of next generation; management room: what is workable and a signal to non-family staff); decide the policy in the **Owner Room**; emotions cannot be decided in the management room.

#### Research on Employing Relatives
- Largely positive but varies by area; profitability falls after a family CEO succession (policy issue: define entry and career rules).
- **Entitlement via reciprocal nepotism** leads to loss of business; policy response: **people earn their place**.

### 4.1.5 Related-Party Transactions

#### Definition and Forms
- **Related-party transaction:** money or value moving between family and firm (and across multiple firms and branches).
- Forms: **loans** (no guarantee, no interest, no repayment date), **property rentals**, **fees**, **family employment** (pay above fair market value, no linked responsibilities), **equity dealings / share issues**, cross-selling between family firms (can inflate valuations if listed), use of company assets as personal assets.
- Not wrong in themselves if **at arm's length, disclosed, independently approved**; legitimacy depends on structure and governance.

#### Tunnelling and Propping

| | Direction | Effect |
|---|-----------|--------|
| **Tunnelling** | Value taken **out of** the firm to controllers/other parties, not at arm's length | **Minority shareholders** (and lenders) bear the loss |
| **Propping** | Value put **into** a struggling firm by other family firms, at varied valuations and risks | Minority shareholders at risk; can hide losses |

- Tunnelling is most likely where a family's control far exceeds its share of cash-flow rights.

#### Legal Guardrails (India)
- Income Tax Act has an entire section on related-party transactions; the Companies Act 2013 and SEBI's LODR Regulations set a legal floor.
- Section **188 with Rule 15**: a relative's appointment to an office of profit above a certain pay needs **shareholder approval**.
- Law defines "relative" widely (about 27 categories); related parties can include trusts and firms of in-laws.

#### Five Safeguards

| Step | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **1. Define** | Who is a related party |
| **2. Disclose** | Interests and transactions; record and approve formally |
| **3. Approve independently** | Independent approval |
| **4. Arm's-length pricing** | Same price as to an independent third party |
| **5. Monitor** | Ongoing, including loans and pay to relatives and personal use of company assets |

### 4.1.6 Working Relationships

#### Who Works with Whom

| Group | Examples | Runs on |
|-------|----------|---------|
| **Family inside the firm** | Parents, siblings, spouses | Kinship plus role |
| **Family outside the firm** | Non-working owners, retired elders, trustees, relatives in other firms | Ownership and emotion |
| **Non-family inside** | Executives, employees, independent directors | Competence and careers |
| **Non-family outside** | Advisers, consultants, lenders, customers, suppliers | Reputation and contracts |

#### Relationship Risks and Mitigation

| Relationship | Risk | Mitigation |
|--------------|------|------------|
| **Parent-child** | Expectations not spelled out | Write down the rules; outside experience builds competence |
| **Sibling-sibling** | Unequal effort; rivalry from childhood | Fair process |
| **In-laws** | Neither family nor outsider; stake in their relative being looked after | Clear boundaries |
| **Family-non-family manager** | Unclear authority | Clear mandate |

#### What External Parties Want

| Party | Wants | Built by |
|-------|-------|----------|
| **Banker / lender** | Predictability | Regular reports, no surprises |
| **Community** | Good neighbour and employer; leader | Stewardship |
| **Adviser** | A clear client | Contract / engagement letter |

- The family is judged through the lens of the firm and vice versa.

#### Trust
- Trust is the **base currency / working capital**. Four dimensions: **competence, openness, concern, reliability** (promises kept, even across a generation).
- Three research drivers: **ability, benevolence, integrity**.
- Trust breaks easily with one act; rebuilding takes much longer but is not irreversible.

#### Transparency and Privacy
- Who needs to know what, to what extent, and when; **owners need to know everything**.
- Non-family managers/employees need only a summary of results (growth, profitability); not debt, individual pay, liquidity or family drawings; valuation and dividends need not be shared at all.
- Board needs full information; family staff need a summary of succession plans and disputes; disputes can be kept from non-family managers.

#### Before Joining the Family Business
- **Ask the owners** (five owner rights: design, decide, value, inform, transfer): What will I be told? Is there a shot at the top and a progression path? Written 5-10 year goals? Who sets my pay?
- **Ask yourself:** Why join? Anything to learn? Future on par with outside? Am I all in or hedging? Is outside experience first better? How are relationships inside? Does the role match my skills and let me grow?
- Many "no" answers are red flags.

#### Traps for Joiners
1. **Promotion without workload** (title/role without feedback or skills).
2. **Special treatment outside the chain of command.**
3. **Home and work blurred.** Address the parent by title (e.g., "MD Sir", "CEO Sir", "Seth Ji") rather than "Papa" or surname.
4. **Identity beyond the firm:** the family name is on the firm; create something different and outside it.

#### Six Habits for Steady Working Relationships
**Name your hat; write things down; meet on schedule; keep family drama out of work; use outsiders for feedback; agree a conflict route early.**

### 4.1.7 The Role of Family Science

#### Three Lenses
- A firm can have a perfect governance chart and still fail because of how the family behaves as a system.

| Lens | Idea |
|------|------|
| **Family systems** | The family is an emotional unit; anxiety travels through triangles and down generations |
| **Circumplex** | Cohesion, flexibility and communication decide how well a family copes with change |
| **Generations** | Lineage and age cohorts are different things; both shape behaviour |

#### Family Systems Concepts (Bowen)

| Concept | Meaning | Family-firm example |
|---------|---------|---------------------|
| **Differentiation** | Thinking apart from group emotions | Disagree with a parent without obeying or cutting off |
| **Triangles** | A third party absorbs tension | Non-family CEO drawn into father-son fight |
| **Projection** | Parents transfer anxiety to a child | Worried parent overprotects an heir |
| **Transmission** | Patterns pass across generations | Feuds and favouritism repeat |
| **Cutoff** | Distance replaces resolution | A branch stops talking and sells out |

- Aim: stay **connected without being controlled**.

#### Circumplex Model
- **Cohesion:** disengaged, separated, connected, enmeshed. **Flexibility:** rigid, structured, flexible, chaotic.
- **Enmeshed and rigid** = founder control; **disengaged and chaotic** = drifting branches.
- **Communication** is the facilitating dimension that moves a family towards the balanced centre.

#### Birth Order
- Popular claim: eldest responsible/conventional, middle diplomatic peacemaker, youngest rebel/risk-taker.
- Evidence: large samples show **no lasting effect on personality**, and a very small effect on self-rated intellect. Birth order bites through **rules** (e.g., primogeniture) and **expectations** (scripts, not traits).

| Position | Common script | Typical hat | Risk |
|----------|---------------|-------------|------|
| **Eldest** | Carries on the business | Owner-manager in core firm | Sibling resentment; pressure on eldest |
| **Middle** | Keeps the peace | Owner; chair/counsel in family assembly/council | Overlooked; may quit or disengage |
| **Youngest** | Protected yet free | Owner; new ventures, foundation | May not take business seriously |
| **Only child** | Sole heir | All three hats early | No peers to evaluate; isolation |

- Fix: a **written, merit-based path** regardless of birth order; competence over stereotype.

#### Paternal and Maternal Lines
- Descent rules (property, name, succession) often follow the **paternal line**; law is changing and **daughters have equal rights by birth under Hindu law**.
- Cousins may identify with the father's or mother's line; in-laws add a second family; the mother is often the mediator (Kokilaben Ambani brokered the 2005 split).

### 4.1.8 Generations and Sibling Dynamics

#### Four Meanings of Generation

| Meaning | Basis |
|---------|-------|
| **Descent** | Rank in the line (G1, G2) |
| **Cohort** | Born in the same period |
| **Life stage** | Same age phase as peers |
| **Historical period** | Shaped by the same events |

- **Example:** founder born 1935 (Silent generation); eldest child A born 1958 (Boomer); youngest D born 1975 (Gen X); A's eldest child E born 1981. A and D are same generation by descent but 17 years apart and different cohorts; D and E are uncle and nephew, six years apart, adjacent cohorts.
- Design the firm for **rank by descent and affinity by cohort**.
- **Implications:** succession (uncle and nephew may compete; skipping a generation may fit); governance (they represent different branches and age bands); communication (agree channels and pace for mixed-age meetings).

#### Sibling Rivalry
- **Drivers:** childhood roles that never changed; unequal effort or pay; unclear authority; perceived parental favouritism.
- **Coolers:** fair processes and transparent pay; separate spheres of responsibility and clear roles; a purpose larger than any sibling; an outside adviser or board.
- Rivalry is **structural**, so address it with structure.

#### Fairness Among Siblings

| Rule | In siblings | Risk |
|------|-------------|------|
| **Equality** | Equal shares | Ignores unequal effort |
| **Equity** | Same rules/results for pay | Splits active and passive siblings |
| **Need** | Support a sibling in difficulty | Breeds dependency |

#### Parent's Role

| Role | What it involves | Limitation |
|------|------------------|------------|
| **Referee** | Decides each dispute; parent central | Siblings stay in the lobby |
| **Mediator** | Helps siblings agree | Works only while parent lives and is respected |
| **Architect** | Sets rules, forums and exit options in advance | Outlasts the parent; **preferred** |

- **Fragility test:** if peace depends on one person being in the room, it is not yet good governance.

#### Ladder for Siblings Working Together
1. **Prevent** (shared understanding, in writing)
2. **Talk** (family meetings, councils)
3. **Decide** (board with delegated authority)
4. **Resolve** (mediate before formal steps such as lawyers)
5. **Exit** (pre-agreed mechanism to sell stake)

- **Unanimity trap:** requiring every sibling to agree gives everyone a veto; solve with **reserved matters and delegation**.

### 4.1.9 Foundations for Working Siblings

#### Eight Foundations
Siblings become partners by choice, not co-owners by accident, through: **purpose, board, code of conduct, decision process, transparency, written agreements, working relationships, exit plan.**

| # | Foundation | Content |
|---|------------|---------|
| 1 | **Shared purpose** | Why co-own rather than divide and exit? Income, growth or legacy? What will each give up (freedom, dividends, control)? Test: "Would each of us buy in today as an outsider on the same terms?" Write a short statement; review every few years |
| 2 | **Neutral forum (board)** | Board with outsiders; independent chair rather than a sibling (or ensure voice for others if a sibling chairs) |
| 3 | **Code of conduct** | **Respect** (disagree in private, united front in public, no side deals); **disclosure** (declare conflicts and related-party interests); **merit** (published path); **confidence** (do not use firm information against another owner); **resolution** (mediate before litigation) |
| 4 | **Decision process** | Clear decision rights (who decides what), employment, dividend and compensation policies; rule, not yearly fight |
| 5 | **Transparency** | Open policies on pay, dividends, money; publish pay bands and basis of placement and promotion; policies replace yearly negotiations |
| 6 | **Written agreements** | Family constitution, shareholders' agreement, policies, transfer documents; a **map, not a template**; involve legal counsel and advisers |
| 7 | **Working relationships** | Each sibling owns a sphere with clear metrics; consider no sibling as boss of another; regular feedback (outsiders for candour); agreed disagreement (private forum, limits); a tiebreaker. **Separate spheres beat shared responsibility for everything** |
| 8 | **Exit / buyout plan** | See below |

#### Policy Mechanisms (Foundations 2, 4, 5)

| Mechanism | Problem solved |
|-----------|----------------|
| **Board with outsiders** | Absence of a neutral forum |
| **Decision rights** | Who decides what |
| **Employment policy** | Entry, pay, promotion decided by same rules for every sibling |
| **Dividend policy** | Trade-off between liquidity and growth |
| **Compensation policy** | Suspicion of unequal or favoured pay |

#### Exit Plan Design
- **Right of first refusal:** family can match an outside offer before a stake leaves.
- **Who can force a sale** and under what conditions; **put and call options; shotgun clauses**.
- **Price formula** agreed in advance (not fought out when emotions peak); **pay out in stages** so the buyout does not drain the firm.
- "Staying is a choice, and exit is a strategic choice": an agreed way to leave can make staying stronger.

### 4.1.10 Styles of Working Together, Across Branches

#### Five Styles of Sibling Teams (Goals x Ownership)

| | Ownership concentrated | Ownership shared |
|---|------------------------|------------------|
| **Goals divergent** | **Dominant leader**: one decides, others follow | **Divergent interests**: equal power, different aims |
| **Goals aligned** | **First among equals**: leadership with team consent | **Complete equals**: equal power, shared aims |

- Fifth style, outside the matrix: **Dysfunctional**.

| Style | What makes it work |
|-------|--------------------|
| **Dominant leader** | Independent board with a term; succession plan |
| **First among equals** | Clear mandate, review cycle, real consultation |
| **Complete equals** | Clear decision rules, delegated authority, a **tiebreaker** (sometimes prune the tree) |
| **Divergent interests** | Agree a shared purpose, or split into separate businesses |
| **Dysfunctional** | Outside help, then restructure or separate |

#### Pruning the Family Tree

| Level | Action |
|-------|--------|
| **Ownership** | Buyout or consolidation of branches; limit who may hold shares |
| **Governance** | Limit seats; represent branches, not every individual |
| **Management** | Fewer family employees, more professionals |

- Research: **simplicity helps both family harmony and business performance**; the aim is a viable firm and an intact family, not exclusion for its own sake.
- Inequality between branches is inevitable: manage it through forums, shared purpose and agreed processes.

#### Contrasting Splits

| | Ambani (Reliance), 2005 | Godrej, 2024 |
|---|-------------------------|--------------|
| **Who split** | Two brothers (sons of founder, who died in 2002 without a will) | Two branches (Adi-Nariman and Jamsetji-Smita) |
| **What split** | Oil and petrochemicals to one; telecom, power and finance to the other | Listed companies vs Godrej and Boyce and land |
| **Manner** | After a very public feud; mother reportedly brokered | Announced jointly as amicable |

- Lesson: absent rules, especially on an unanticipated founder exit, separation is messy; **planned pruning can strengthen both branches**.

### 4.1.11 Non-Family Executives and In-Laws

#### Non-Family Executives: Two-Way Bargain

| What they need | What the family needs |
|----------------|-----------------------|
| Clear mandate, responsibilities and authority | Skills the family lacks |
| Market-linked pay | Loyalty to the firm, not one person |
| Career path and honesty about the ceiling (top role is reserved for family) | Honest advice |
| Clear JDs and contracts | Discretion; continuity |

- They play in the **management room**; must know which decisions are theirs; keep confidences; **must not play one family member or branch against another**; respect the family's lineage, history and values; build trust for a long stay.

#### In-Laws
- An in-law belongs to two families of orientation; the couple and children form the family of procreation; the business family and the other family ("shadow family").
- In-laws help raise the next owners.
- **Positives:** bring skills the family lacks (son-in-law is CEO in about **10%** of cases); a measure of fairness; comparison with their own family business.
- **Negatives:** can hear and amplify grievance; as pseudo-owners, shares may leak outside the family through inheritance.

#### Five-Stage Ladder of In-Law Involvement
1. **Excluded** (still get information)
2. **Informed**, no role
3. **Included in the family room** (gatherings, assemblies)
4. **Employed** (role, consultant or adviser)
5. **Owner**

- Most follow the first four stages; ownership is rare.
- Successor CEO: opportunity (outside talent, skills); give a clear mandate and a plan for ownership. Employee/adviser: it is a job, not a path to ownership.
- Define who is family, protect assets, set clear boundaries, and define who can enter the owner room.
- To thrive, an in-law brings skills (best honed outside), knows the family structure, is patient, finds a confidant, and has review points and a path to exit.

### 4.1.12 Allocating Roles and Control

#### Role vs Control

| Decision | Question | Decided by |
|----------|----------|-----------|
| **Role allocation** | Who does what? ("the job") | Competence and interest |
| **Control allocation** | Who decides, at which level? ("the decision") | Shares, votes, board seats (owner room) |

- A **role without control, or control without a role**, is a first stepping stone to conflict.

#### Three Layers of Control Across Structures

| Structure | Operational | Strategic | Ownership |
|-----------|-------------|-----------|-----------|
| **Founder-led** | Family | Family | Family |
| **Sibling team** | Split (e.g., one finance/HR, other marketing/sales) | Mixed | Usually equal |
| **Family owns, others run** | Non-family | Mixed (family and non-family) | Family |
| **Branch-based** | Each branch | Each branch | Each branch |

#### Control Tools
- **Voting structures** and **dual-class shares** (some shares carry dividends, others voting control).
- **Trusts** (trustees and beneficiaries, majority rules) and **shareholders' agreements** (no selling, tag-along/sell together, first right of refusal).
- **Veto rights** in constitutions.
- Balance control against cash-flow rights to avoid tunnelling.

#### Five Principles
1. **Separate the layers** (ownership, strategy, operations; use the four rooms).
2. **Match the person to the role** by competence, skills and interest, not birth order.
3. **Fair process:** written rules in advance, voice for everyone, documented and reviewed.
4. **Write it down and review it.**
5. **Plan the exit** for everyone.

## 4.2 Part 2: Women in Family Business

### 4.2.1 Why Women, Why Now: The Evidence

#### Six Lenses

| Lens | Argument |
|------|----------|
| **Justice and rights** | Equal treatment is owed, whatever the business case |
| **Talent** | Half the family is half the successor pool |
| **Continuity** | In a small pool each missing candidate is costly |
| **Governance** | Diverse boards see more risks |
| **Demography** | Daughters and widows increasingly hold shares |
| **System** | Kinship sets roles before business logic |

- Audience framing: a founder hears **continuity**; a board hears **governance**; a family hears **fairness**.

#### Global Evidence
- Women's presence in senior and mid management and CEO roles has risen; board seats improved thanks to quotas (in India mostly listed firms; most family firms are unlisted and outside these numbers).
- Inside family firms women hold roughly **a fifth** of top roles.
- Pipeline narrows at every rung: US corporate data 2025: about **49%** of entry roles vs about **29%** of C-suite.
- Plateau in C-suite presence since 2024.

#### Business Case
- Studies (23 countries McKinsey; EY and PIIE across 91 countries; Italian and French firm studies): top-quartile gender diversity on executive teams is **39%** more likely to outperform on profit.
- Caveat: **correlations, not causation**. Family firms give women a better climate as **chair than as CEO**; women CEOs seem to do better in non-family firms.
- **Never let the ethical case depend on the financial case.**

#### India
- Women own **1 in 5** enterprises but only about **a tenth of turnover** (most are micro; a size gap, not a count gap).
- **397 of 573** women executive directors (about **69%**) are promoter family members.
- Women start fewer firms and close more for family reasons.
- Law: India lags on enforcement of women's legal rights while supportive frameworks do somewhat better.

#### What Is Not Counted
- Global family-firm series; daughter successions in India; heirs among women CEOs; **unpaid family work**.

#### Five Phases and Family vs Non-Family Firms

| Phase | Note |
|-------|------|
| **Worker** | About 49% of entry-level in large US firms |
| **Entrepreneur** | About 1 in 10 women starts a business a year |
| **Owner/investor** | Typically by inheritance or marriage into family firms |
| **Leader/director** | About a third of senior management roles |
| **Household manager** | Most unpaid care; links family and firm |

| Dimension | Non-family firm | Family firm |
|-----------|-----------------|-------------|
| **Entry** | Application and selection | Kinship, birth, marriage, widowhood |
| **Promotion** | Formal criteria, HR processes | Informal; decided by relatives; may skew against women |
| **Route to ownership** | Purchase or stock plans | Gifts, inheritance, marriage |

### 4.2.2 Family Roles Across Generations

#### Women in the Three-Circle Model
- Women can occupy all **seven** roles, including the centre where family, ownership and management overlap; the system is entered through a "door" (family, ownership, business or a mix).

| Role | What she brings | Hidden cost |
|------|-----------------|-------------|
| **Mother** | Values, family story, mediation; knows moods and rituals; manages people | Unpaid and unseen; over-visibility can invite triangulation |
| **Daughter** | Talent, loyalty, fresh view | Groomed to help, not lead |
| **Daughter-in-law** | Outside skills and perspective | Split loyalty; influence without a formal role |
| **Mother-in-law** | Culture, values, memory, continuity | Loss of role; may block change |

- Examples: **Roshni Nadar Malhotra** (groomed to lead; chairman of HCLTech); **Anu Aga** then Mehr Padamsey at Thermax (mother as manager of people); **Nisaba Godrej**.
- One woman wears many hats over a lifetime (daughter, wife, mother, widow). After a patriarch's unexpected death the widow may lead or hold the firm steady until the son is ready (Kokilaben Ambani mediating after Dhirubhai died intestate; Sanjay Kapoor's case).
- With several daughters-in-law, ensure both equity and equality in title and pay.

#### Daughter: Heir or Helper
- **Heir:** told early she will take over; title and ownership; part of all three circles.
- **Helper/supporter:** talented but authority is **borrowed** ("help your father or brother"), not used fully.
- **Outsider:** no plan within family; assumed to marry into another business family; given assets, not ownership.
- Research: many fathers saw daughters as non-viable successors; changing. Branches may be created so son and daughter each have a business (e.g., TVS: TAFE headed by Mallika Srinivasan passing to Lakshmi; son Sudarshan Venu in TVS Motors).

#### Daughters' Roles: Father's Agenda vs Daughter's Own Agenda

| Role | Pattern | Way forward |
|------|---------|-------------|
| **Daddy's little girl / Snow White** | High protection; father's agenda; avoids tough management | Give P&L responsibility; honest feedback; groom |
| **Silent voice / invisible successor** | Father's agenda, low visibility; decisions communicated through father | Ask for her view |
| **Caretaker of king's gold** | Can lead, known identity | Transition leadership to her |
| **Usurper** | Own ambition, wants control, may displace brother | Separate role from person; split out a branch |
| **Legitimate leader** | Own agenda, qualified, fully in control | Protection not required |

### 4.2.3 Equality, Equity and Difference

#### Three Ideas

| Idea | Meaning | Test |
|------|---------|------|
| **Equality** | Equal enjoyment by women and men of valued goods, opportunities, resources, rewards | Are outcomes and access equal? |
| **Equity** | Fairness in distributing benefits and responsibilities; route to equality | Is the process fair given starting points? |
| **Difference** | People differ in circumstances, constraints, preferences; some differences are imposed | Is the difference chosen or imposed? |

- Equity language can mask discrimination. **Gender-blind is not gender-neutral.**

#### Four Lenses

| Lens | Idea |
|------|------|
| **Gendered organisations** | Ideal worker imagined male; neutrality hides a gendered substructure |
| **Tokens** | Under about **15%** representation: visible, polarised, pushed to assimilate |
| **Role congruity theory** | Communal roles vs agentic leadership create prejudice against women leaders |
| **Care and justice** | Two ethics; influential but critiqued as essentialist and thinly evidenced |

- One daughter among sons is a **token by construction**.

#### Guardrails
- **Not deficiency:** difference is not inferiority. **Not essentialist:** style is not destiny. **Not a job filter:** "good with people" must not become HR-only.
- Test: "**Would we say the same for a son?**" Rule: **roles follow capability and preference, not category.**
- Research: women described as more conciliatory, attentive, collaborative; must prove competence before respect; variation within each sex is larger than the average gap.
- Posture: equitable process, same criteria for all, plus active steps to widen the pool.

#### Five-Test Fair Process for the Daughter Question

| Test | Check |
|------|-------|
| **1. Criteria** | Written rules before names or gender are discussed |
| **2. Information** | Every child sees same numbers, plans, people |
| **3. Development** | Same budget, mentorship, rotation |
| **4. Decision body** | Includes an independent director, not only relatives |
| **5. Feedback** | Written reasons and a route to appeal for daughter and son alike |

#### What a Family Firm Offers Women and Costs

| Offers | Matching cost |
|--------|---------------|
| Security, assets, protection in crisis | Potential minority holder; unclean exit; crises arrive unprepared |
| Standing and founder's trust | Must prove competence first |
| Flexibility, work-life fit | Family-firm boundary blurs; cost greater for women |
| Early responsibility, mentorship | Better climate as chair than as CEO |
| Ownership route | Voting power often tied to rule |

- Exchange: woman gains security, assets, flexibility, standing, access to family capital and patience; firm gains talent (selected daughters cross a higher bar), a wider successor pool, diverse board perspectives, loyalty, continuity of knowledge, networks.
- It holds **only if the woman is seen, paid and given a voice.** A written policy with criteria, equal access to information, development and independent assessment makes decisions about daughters fairer.

### 4.2.4 Women as the Invisible Force

#### Five Forms of Invisibility
- Invisibility is **structural, not personal**.

| Form | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Legal** | No shareholding or board seat; law does not recognise contribution |
| **Economic** | Unpaid or unrecorded labour; or paid below market |
| **Organisational** | No title; not on the org chart; no appointment order or paycheck |
| **Ownership** | Small shareholder on paper (e.g., 90-10 or 80-20) but no control; vote matters for nothing |
| **Narrative** | Absent from the founding story |

- Examples: Sudha Murty (reportedly a Rs 10,000 loan to her husband, later written off, which started Infosys); Meherbai Tata (pledged her diamonds to raise funds when Tata Steel was in a downturn); Bertha Benz.

#### The Cycle and Its Costs
- Unpaid help -> no title/pay/record -> no voice or standing; daughters watch mothers and assume the same, so the cycle repeats.
- **Break it:** name the role and title; pay and record the work; record ownership accurately; give a formal channel and seat.
- Costs: woman loses pension, shares, standing on exit, monthly pay; family loses when succession ignores her insight; business suffers key-person risk, with undocumented guarantees.

| Room | Formal channel |
|------|----------------|
| **Family room** | Seat on family council |
| **Owner room** (most powerful) | Seat as owner |
| **Boardroom** | Board seat if director |
| **Management room** | Clear role, JD, pay |

### 4.2.5 Roles Women Play and Why They Change

#### Five Roles (Authority x Influence)

| Role | Authority | Influence | Notes |
|------|-----------|-----------|-------|
| **Invisible** | Low | High | "Whispers into the CEO's ear"; off the chart, unpaid; fix by formal recognition |
| **Figurehead** | High (title) | Low | Fix by adding real power to the title |
| **Manager** | | | e.g., Shobhana Bhartia (HT) |
| **Entrepreneurial leader** | | | e.g., Priya Paul (Apeejay Park Hotels) |
| **Strategic leader** (top right) | High | High | e.g., Mallika Srinivasan (TAFE), Nisaba Godrej, Roshni Nadar Malhotra; strong manager, owner and family leader |

- Hats: invisible woman = informal manager hat, no owner hat, family-leader hat often unrecognised; strategic leader = all three hats very high. Identify the hat currently worn and the hats still to be acquired.

#### Drivers of Role Change

| Driver | Effect |
|--------|--------|
| **Growth and specialisation** | Informal role becomes a qualified job |
| **Family commitments** (children, elder care) | Can reduce a manager to invisible, and return later (both ways) |
| **Sudden events** | Unplanned succession into top job |
| **Conflict** | Woman redrawn as mediator or conciliator |
| **Financial or legal** (tax, inheritance) | Invisible to figurehead or owner, sometimes unwittingly |
| **Personal factors** | Ambition, qualifications, sense of identity |

- Drivers can be grouped as structural, shock, personal and institutional.
- Sudden events often push women into the top job unprepared (a pattern known as the **glass cliff**).

### 4.2.6 Barriers Women Face and Success Guilt

#### Typology of Barriers (not exhaustive)
- **Occupational separation** (Spain: 81% of female-led vs 47% of male-led family service firms), few promotion chances, little strategic voice, overlooked as successor, gender bias despite home support.
- Women often hold roles without formal title and are paid below men for the same role; must prove competence more.
- About **81%** of firms choose a male successor when both sexes are available; segregation narrows in second-generation firms.

#### Double Bind and Legitimacy Gap
- **Double bind:** expected to be warm and consultative, yet decisive; when decisive, judged unlikeable and not leader material. The same behaviour is "assertive" in a son and "aggressive" in a daughter.
- **Legitimacy gap:** home support (father, mother, siblings) gives family legitimacy; staff loyal to the father see "the boss's daughter"; **role incongruity**.
- What helps: **founder's public sponsorship**, clear mandate (possibly successor), early measurable results, bias-aware HR policy.
- Test: is her authority her own (from role and JD) rather than lent by father or brother?

#### Three-Level Map of Barriers

| Level | Barriers |
|-------|----------|
| **Individual** | Blindness to differences, imposter syndrome, assumed glass ceiling |
| **Family and firm** | Separation, fewer promotions, no title, overlooked, bias |
| **Institutional** | Inheritance laws, property rights, quotas, culture |

#### Policy Responses

| Issue | Response |
|-------|----------|
| Occupational separation | Rotation through core line function |
| Fewer promotions | Clear criteria; independent review |
| Little strategic voice | Seats on family council, board; voice at retreats |
| Overlooked as successor | List all candidates with unbiased criteria |
| Gender bias from employees | Public sponsorship; 360-degree feedback; grievance/appeal route |

- Build levers across all four rooms; strongest when decided in the **owner room**.

#### Success Guilt and Imposter Phenomenon
- Achievement coded masculine causes role conflict; **success guilt** (guilt and love coexist, e.g., a mother-daughter team in an Egyptian firm).

| Level | What helps |
|-------|-----------|
| **The woman** | Name the feeling; separate work from self-worth; log results; peer support |
| **The family** | Praise the work, not the name; drop sibling labels; specific feedback; frame the win as a family strength |
| **The firm** | Written criteria; independent assessment; 360-degree feedback |

- Peer networks (women leadership fora, alumni) help acknowledge self-worth.

### 4.2.7 The CEO's Spouse: Six Roles and Readiness for Widowhood

#### Six Roles

| Role | Helps when | Harms when | Succession step |
|------|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Jealous spouse** | Her voice is heard early | Sees firm as rival; undermines successor | Give her a life after succession |
| **Chief trust officer** | Closest confidant; carries messages, interprets decisions | No accountability | Involve actively in strengthening trust channel |
| **Business partner** | Co-owner sharing risk; roles explicit | Roles unclear; home/firm blur | Plan joint exit and finances |
| **Back-office (VP HR/finance/admin)** | Runs smoothly | Overdependence; person-centric organisation | Document knowledge; formal role and equitable pay |
| **Senior adviser** | Wise counsel | Control without formal role | Agree advice vs decision; formal channel |
| **Free agent** | Healthy boundary; own career | Crisis, with no information shared | Brief fully and in good time |

- Mapped by sense of ownership vs continuity: **partner** in strongest quadrant; trust officers and senior advisers strengthen continuity; free agent partly. **Aim: move the spouse up and to the right.**

#### Readiness for Widowhood: Five Questions
1. Does she know the **advisers** by name?
2. Does she have full and fair access to **accounts and documents**?
3. Does she know the **will and agreements** (shares, votes, chair)?
4. Who holds **authority for the immediate 90 days**?
5. What is the **emergency succession plan**?

- Readiness is not necessarily involvement; the free agent must know as much as the partner.

### 4.2.8 Daughters as Successors and the Rule of Primogeniture

#### Primogeniture in Practice
- **Primogeniture:** eldest-born succeeds. Gender-neutral in words, but in practice the **eldest-born son** (Spanish family data: firstborn of either gender, yet successors are overwhelmingly male).
- Helping a daughter step up: **name the successor early**, formal role on the chart, market pay, outside experience and credentials, same criteria for all, build a network.

#### Rules That Decide Who Inherits Control

| Rule | What it does | Where |
|------|--------------|-------|
| **Male-preference primogeniture** | Eldest son first; daughters only if no son | UK hereditary peerages |
| **Fixed shares by law** | Son's share twice a daughter's | Islamic inheritance law |
| **Adopted son-in-law (mukoyoshi)** | Husband takes family name and succeeds | Japan |
| **Coparcenary by birth** | Daughter equal to son in ancestral property and management control | India, since the 2005 amendment |

- India: partition risk if unplanned (HUF). Valli Arunachalam (Murugappa group) case: court ruled in her favour; settlement followed.
- Keep two questions apart: **who gets the shares, and who gets the chair?**
- Research on firstborn gender and post-succession performance is inconclusive as it ignores the **willingness-ability matrix**.

#### Benefits and Costs of Primogeniture

| Benefits | Costs |
|----------|-------|
| Clarity: everyone knows the heir from day one | Selected by birth/gender, not ability or willingness |
| Early grooming of the heir | Others not groomed |
| Control stays undivided; stability for stakeholders | Excluded siblings hold shares without role; resentment |

- Clarity, grooming and unity do not depend on birth order or gender.

#### Fairer Alternative
- **Ownership fair for all children** (sons and daughters); **leadership chosen by written criteria** applied equally, by a family council, not one person; non-leaders get a buyout or dividend.
- Equal in the ownership circle; **merit in the business circle**.

### 4.2.9 Heiresses Inside and Reasons to Join

#### Three Heiress Paths (Study of 12 Heiresses)

| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Invisible force** | Power without a formal title |
| **Professional** | Title, function, visible responsibility |
| **Anchor** | Stabilising support holding family/firm together, often without title |

- Movements: professional -> invisible (personal event, e.g., childbirth); invisible -> anchor (unexpected family crisis); anchor -> professional (organisational event such as formalising a role). **Entrepreneur** path: new venture inside or outside the family galaxy (Ananya Birla, Swatantra Microfinance).
- Ask **where the heiress is now, not who she is**.
- Helps advancement: parental mentoring, public family sponsorship, outside credentials. Styles (conciliatory, supportive, collaborative, flexible) are choices, not stereotypes.

#### Transparent vs Mirrored Ceiling

| Ceiling | Meaning |
|---------|---------|
| **Transparent (glass) ceiling** | Top position visible but blocked by policies, bias, closed networks |
| **Mirrored ceiling** | Heiress herself chooses not to pursue the top position |

- Before calling it mirrored, check that a competing **male candidate was offered the same options**; if she was not offered, it is a glass ceiling.

#### Push and Pull

| | Source | Risk if unmanaged | Question to ask |
|---|--------|-------------------|-----------------|
| **Push** | Crisis; parent's request; no brother available | Stopgap, no negotiated role, blurred boundaries, weak authority | What role, title and pay does she want; why now? |
| **Pull** | Opportunity to lead; flexibility; access to family capital, trust, resources; supported by outside experience | Expectations exceed what the firm can offer | What evidence backs the firm's promise? |

#### Five Questions for the Heiress
1. **Why:** pushed or pulled; would I choose this if no one asked?
2. **Role:** title, authority, pay, reporting line in writing.
3. **Proof:** experience and credentials.
4. **Path:** am I on the successor list; what criteria?
5. **Exit path:** buyout, notice, how and when can I exercise it?

### 4.2.10 Law and Regulation: What Reaches a Family Firm

#### Reach of Rules

| Type of rule | Who it reaches |
|--------------|----------------|
| **Board quotas** | Mostly large or listed companies (often **comply-or-explain**) |
| **Pay and leave rules** | Every employer (pay transparency in developed contexts for large employers; family leave varies) |
| **Inheritance and property law** | Every family (varies by national law and personal law, e.g., Islamic, Hindu) |

- Law is mandated on paper; practice may vary.

#### India
- **Companies Act:** at least one woman director for listed firms and those meeting size criteria (independent director).
- **Hindu Succession Act, amended 2005:** daughters are coparceners; Muslim personal law also has protective provisions.
- Workplace: **maternity leave, labour codes, POSH Act**.
- Enterprise support: **PM Mudra**, **3% MSE procurement sub-target**, **90% CGTMSE cover** for women-owned units; not law per se.
- Moving target: corporate laws bill, Shariat Act challenge and labour code rules pending.

#### What the Family Business Should Do

| Area | Action |
|------|--------|
| **Scope** | Understand which rules reach the firm (size, listing aspirations, jurisdictions) |
| **Pay and leave** | Plan for pay-gap reporting and labour code compliance |
| **Inheritance** | Audit each member's personal law; use wills, gifts, trusts; put understandings in writing |
| **HR floor** | Maternity and safety provisions and a POSH committee |
| **Family constitution** | Write equal treatment of daughters and sons into it |

- **Law sets the floor; policy sets the practice (criteria, pay bands, review); the family constitution sets the promise.**

### 4.2.11 Working Couples

#### Three Models

| Model | Pattern | Question |
|-------|---------|----------|
| **Primary-secondary** | One career always wins conflict (e.g., his firm, she runs back office) | Is secondary a choice or designed? |
| **Turn-taking** | Priority alternates on a schedule | Is the turn ever honoured? |
| **Double-primary** | Neither yields; both ambitious | Who arbitrates? |

#### Whose Firm and Where Does the Spouse Work

| | Spouse works outside | Spouse works inside |
|---|----------------------|---------------------|
| **His family firm** | Free agent case; both free | Spouse keeps trust; coparcener under Indian law |
| **Her family firm** | She is clearly the successor; no turn-taking needed; possible double-primary if he is ambitious outside | Son-in-law joins for skills and harmony; in-laws may not question him enough, so nepotism can creep in |

#### Five Conversations
1. **Priority:** whose career first, for how long, can it change.
2. **Money:** who earns, who is paid, who is unpaid for a role.
3. **Boundaries:** what is discussed at home vs office.
4. **Title:** role, pay and credit commensurate; roles need not be at the same level.
5. **Review:** as family stage changes (children, elders), renegotiate and plan succession roadmap.

- Distinguish a **deliberate choice** from an arrangement that emerged without discussion.

## 4.3 Part 3: Conflict Management

### 4.3.1 Introduction to Conflict Management

#### Why Family Conflict Is Different
- Disagreements are inevitable, but decision-makers are also parents, siblings and spouses; a dispute over money, responsibilities or leadership affects both business and relationships.
- Conflict is not necessarily harmful if handled constructively; avoidance builds resentment; unmanaged confrontation damages relationships and business.
- Owners make the rules, so there is **no referee until they agree to create one**; an independent referee helps.

#### Sailboat Case
- Two brothers disputed a company-paid sailboat (racing vs customer entertainment). The real issue was **deep-seated resentment over roles and contribution**. The firm declined, sold at a discount and the family lost its identity. "It was never about a sailboat."

#### Three Zones of Conflict

| Zone | Features | Outcome |
|------|----------|---------|
| **Too hot** | Public, in court, warfare in public domain | Growth stalls; best to exit, split or sell; mediation likely too late |
| **Goldilocks zone** | Difficult issues raised and addressed in reasonable time; intent never in doubt; trust remains | Resolvable |
| **Too cold** | Outward unity is fake; the "elephant in the room" never named; silence | Sudden collapse or sale |

#### Three-Question Diagnostic
1. **Direction:** are critical issues addressed; nothing left unsaid, nothing carried back after the meeting?
2. **Decisions:** are decisions made honestly and transparently?
3. **Relationships:** can members still work and celebrate together; is there enough **trust** (intent not questioned)?

### 4.3.2 Nature and Sources of Conflict

#### Divergent Interests Are a Feature
- Family circle: harmony, fairness. Ownership circle: return of capital, liquidity. Business circle: resources and time to execute. Differences are by design, yet all aim at the longevity of the family firm.

#### Position vs Interest
- **Position:** stated demand ("I want a 40% dividend"). **Interest:** underlying need ("I need predictable income").
- Bargaining on positions (40% vs 20%) escalates conflict; discussing interests can avoid it.

#### Eight Sources of Conflict
- Eight sources are named; usually more than one operates together, and they build over time. Those described:

| Source | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **Role overlap** | Multiple hats; others compare roles |
| **Resource allocation** | Starting point of many conflicts; equality vs equity (family members often prefer equality) |
| **Succession ambiguity** | No agreed plan; vying; favouritism; those who expect to lose may leave and break the firm |
| **Life-cycle shocks** | Sudden events; plan what to do and document it |
| **Communication gap** | No shared forum; ad hoc decisions in wrong room; the unspoken "elephant" |
| **Fairness** | Favouritism, birth-order assumptions about roles and succession |

#### Rivalry and Spillover Across Circles
- **Sibling rivalry can predate the business:** childhood competition for toys becomes competition for title, role, money and inheritance.
- Conflict occurs in all three circles: family (favouritism, blame, credit); management (strategy, resources, decision rights); ownership (right to exit, control, dividends).
- A dispute in one circle spills over to the others (e.g., a dividend dispute spills into the family). Conflict can predate the business (sibling or father-son rivalry).

### 4.3.3 Types of Conflict

#### Four Types
- The first three are not family-specific but have a unique flavour; **succession conflict is family-business specific**.

| Type | About | Symptoms/Effect |
|------|-------|-----------------|
| **Relationship** | The person, not the issue | Personal attacks disguised as business feedback; dispute persists after issue settled; arguments return to how someone was treated; tension in unrelated meetings. **Almost uniformly harmful**: reduces satisfaction and performance regardless of task; worse in family firms as it crosses into other circles |
| **Cognitive (task)** | Ideas, judgments, interpretation of facts | Functional; strengthens decision quality |
| **Affective** | Cognitive disagreement turned personal | Dysfunctional; lowers trust; cognitive often slides into affective once old history is pulled in |
| **Process** | How and who, not the what | Who has authority to decide (e.g., CapEx above agreed threshold: board sign-off or CEO alone); who reports to whom; how information flows (family room hears of strategy shift before/after boardroom) |
| **Succession** | Who leads next | Cognitive layer (who is most capable), relational layer (old rivalry resurfaces), process layer (no criteria, timelines, decision-maker). **Worst type**: a family, management and ownership question at once, so no single room can resolve it alone |

#### Harmony Matrix

| | High family harmony | Low family harmony |
|---|--------------------|--------------------|
| **Cognitive/process** | Improve decisions by testing assumptions | Spill into relationship conflict |
| **Relationship** | Still harmful | Severely harmful |
| **Succession** | Manageable with agreed pre-built process | High risk of full-blown relationship conflict |

- **Protect family harmony first** so task conflict does not become relationship conflict.

### 4.3.4 Manifestations of Conflict

#### Fake Harmony and the Iceberg
- Leaders say "we get along perfectly", yet below the surface: unequal contributions and pay, avoided decisions, no voice for resentment, no governance mechanism.
- A rupture looks sudden outside but inevitable within.

#### Seven-Stage Conflict Spiral
- Conflict spirals from disagreement to family war; **each stage is avoidable and a choice**; identify the stage and stop it there.

| Stage | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **1. Interests diverge** | Roles create different, rational interests (e.g., finance cuts costs, marketing wants branding); can be stopped if discussed |
| **2. Positions harden** | Specific demands; resource decisions feel zero-sum; larger picture lost |
| **3. Communication breaks down** | Usually silence before fights |
| **4. Alliances form** | Splinters across generations, family and outsiders |
| **5. Proxy wars** | Alliances draw in non-family managers and employees; rewards and incentives for furthering an agenda |
| **6. Advocates called in** | Moves from the four rooms to the courtroom; litigation; lawyers and consultants profit; value drops |
| **7. Family war** | Open, public war |

- **Fault lines for alliances:** family branch, owners (shareholders), generation, gender, and others (five in total).
- Examples: Ambani; Singhania family father-son dispute, where advocates were called in and the son prevailed.
- Competitive moves invite competitive responses (e.g., one side hiring a lawyer prompts the other to do so), tightening the spiral.

### 4.3.5 Communication and Negotiation

#### Meeting Ground Rules
- Presume good intent; speak only for yourself; use a **parking lot** for unresolved issues; **stay in the right room** (family room topics do not belong in owner room); everyone is heard; express appreciation.

#### ABC Format
| Letter | Content |
|--------|---------|
| **A** | When this situation occurs (context) |
| **B** | I feel / experience (emotion) |
| **C** | I would like (preference) |

- Replaces "You always ignore me".

#### Principled Negotiation (Four Moves)
1. **People and problem:** address the issue, not the person's character.
2. **Interests, not positions:** what need is the demand satisfying?
3. **Options for mutual gain:** brainstorm and trade across interests before choosing.
4. **Objective criteria:** settle by external standard, not by who holds out longest.

#### Moving from Fake Harmony to Constructive Conflict
- Six moves are named; five are described:
1. **Set ground rules first.**
2. **Agree a shared purpose.**
3. **Beachheads:** start with one small, contained change.
4. **Principles, not people:** build the rule before a person becomes the test case.
5. **Graft the new into the old:** link new ideas to accepted wisdom; explain why the old approach made sense.

#### Thomas-Kilmann Model (Assertiveness x Cooperativeness)

| Style | Assertiveness | Cooperativeness | Note |
|-------|---------------|-----------------|------|
| **Avoiding** | Low | Low | Fake harmony is a strong engine |
| **Accommodating** | Low | High | |
| **Collaborating** | High | High | Fully satisfy both; better outcomes |
| **Competing** | High | Low | Somebody wins, somebody loses; worse outcomes |
| **Compromising** | Middle | Middle | Split the difference |

- Choose by situation, readiness of individuals and existing governance.

#### Four Steps to Escape a Feud
1. **Create alignment for change:** conflicts end when interests change, usually through suffering. Agree "**the status quo is not an option**"; "let's work out our differences so we don't poison the next generation."
2. **Put every option on the table:** sale to outsider, branch buyout, division, or a **grand bargain** (trades across several issues at once: restructured firm, ownership, communication, estate plan equalising ownership across branches).
3. **Rebuild trust in small, repeated acts:** competence, openness, concern for the other's outcome, small commitments before big ones. Trust is produced by resolving conflict well; it is not a precondition.
4. **Get outside help:** a trusted neutral third party. **Ambani 2005 mediation:** the mother, Kokilaben, reportedly chose the broker; K.V. Kamath with two trusted advisers brokered it in an 18-hour session; announced on the third anniversary of the founder's death.

#### Conflict Across the Three-Circle Model

| Circle | Typical conflict | Room |
|--------|------------------|------|
| **Family only** | Belonging and fairness | Family room |
| **Ownership only** | Dividends, valuation, exit rights | Owner room |
| **Business only** | Pay, promotion, proxy wars; strategy and CEO disagreements | Boardroom; day-to-day process in management room |
| **Centre (all three)** | **Succession conflict** | Governance needed |

- Fewer than **40%** of firms had a formal constitution or family council in some studies; most run conflict prevention informally until crisis.
- **Write the rules before you need them.**

## 4.4 Case Simulation: The Mehta Succession

### 4.4.1 Case and Model Recommendation

#### Setting
- Rajan Mehta, 68, built a Rs 400-crore firm (Mehta Polymers) from one workshop; steps down as MD in twelve months without naming a successor.
- Vikram (eldest son) expects the job and wants a three-year dividend cut for a new plant; Ananya (daughter, chartered accountant) has kept the finances on track without title, salary or board seat; Rohit (youngest son) has an outside career and needs dividends; Sunita (wife) privately tells each child "your father has his reasons".
- Nine decisions across three stages: **Who leads and how; the first rule; reading the conflict.**

#### Model Recommendation

| Stage | Recommendation |
|-------|----------------|
| **Leadership** | Do not name a successor yet. Set up a small succession committee with at least one independent member; define what the next MD must achieve; agree criteria; assess all candidates including Vikram, Ananya and an external professional; first formalise Ananya's role, title and pay. **Process first, then the person** |
| **The first rule** | Separate family member, owner and manager roles; state which decisions belong to which forum (succession = management; dividends = ownership). **Agree who decides first**, so people accept outcomes they did not want |
| **Conflict** | Next flashpoint: Vikram vs Rohit over the dividend cut. Family is at hardening stage, edging into coalitions, with Sunita's private messages replacing open conversation. Hold one facilitated family meeting now, before camps form |

## Quick Revision Summary

### Key Definitions
- **Three systems:** family trades in **emotion** ("Do we belong?"); business in **competence** ("Can you do the job?"); ownership in **power** ("Who decides?").
- **Four rooms:** owner, board, management, family. The family room does not have others reporting to it.
- **Fairness:** distributive, procedural, interactional, retributive. **Fair process:** engage, explain, set expectations.
- **Fredo effect:** incompetence, opportunism or dubious ethics of a relative harms the firm.
- **Tunnelling:** value moves out of the firm to controllers. **Propping:** value moves into a struggling firm.
- **Parent roles:** referee, mediator, **architect (preferred)**.
- **Primogeniture:** eldest-born succeeds; in practice eldest son.
- **Position** = stated demand; **interest** = underlying need.
- **Relationship conflict** (person) is almost always harmful; **cognitive** (ideas) can improve decisions; **process** (who/how); **succession** (family-specific, worst).
- **Goldilocks zone:** hard issues raised and resolved without lasting damage; too hot (public, litigation); too cold (fake harmony).
- **Mirrored ceiling:** heiress chooses not to pursue top role (only if a male rival was offered the same options); **transparent/glass ceiling:** blocked by policy, bias, networks.

### Important Numbers and Lists
- 5 members = 10 relationships; 14 = 91.
- **Six employment decisions:** attract, entry, career path, feedback, pay, exit.
- **Three money flows:** salary (job), board fees (governing), dividends (capital).
- **Five RPT safeguards:** define, disclose, approve independently, arm's-length price, monitor.
- **Eight sibling foundations:** purpose, board, code of conduct, decision process, transparency, written agreements, working relationships, exit plan.
- **Five sibling-team styles:** dominant leader, first among equals, complete equals, divergent interests, dysfunctional.
- **Five in-law stages:** excluded, informed, family room, employed, owner.
- **Five forms of invisibility:** legal, economic, organisational, ownership, narrative.
- **Five women's roles:** invisible, figurehead, manager, entrepreneurial, strategic leader.
- **Six CEO-spouse roles:** jealous spouse, chief trust officer, business partner, back office, senior adviser, free agent.
- **Widowhood readiness (5):** advisers, accounts/documents, will/agreements, 90-day authority, emergency succession plan.
- **Heiress types:** invisible force, professional, anchor. **Push vs pull.**
- **Three couple models:** primary-secondary, turn-taking, double-primary; **five conversations:** priority, money, boundaries, title, review.
- **Eight conflict sources**, **four conflict types**, **seven-stage spiral** (interests diverge, positions harden, communication breaks down, alliances, proxy wars, advocates, family war).
- **Five conflict styles:** avoiding, accommodating, collaborating, competing, compromising.
- **Four steps to escape a feud:** align, all options, rebuild trust, outside help.
- India: women own 1 in 5 enterprises but about a tenth of turnover; 397 of 573 women executive directors are promoter family (about 69%); US 2025 women: 49% entry vs 29% C-suite; 39% more likely to outperform (correlation).
- India law: Companies Act (one woman director), Hindu Succession Act 2005 (daughters coparceners), maternity, labour codes, POSH, PM Mudra, 3% MSE procurement, 90% CGTMSE cover.

### Must-Remember Points
- Relationships, not strategy or capital, bring down family firms; the same features can be strength or liability.
- Ask which circle a person sits in before calling them difficult (avoid attribution error).
- Ownership follows equality, pay follows equity, hardship follows need.
- Most families write only the entry rule; coddling is the most destructive employment system.
- Related-party deals are legitimate only if at arm's length, disclosed and independently approved.
- Birth order has no lasting effect on personality; scripts and rules matter; use a written merit-based path.
- Rivalry is structural; fix it with structure; separate spheres beat shared responsibility.
- Role (the job) is not control (the decision); role without control causes conflict.
- Gender-blind is not gender-neutral; roles follow capability and preference, not category.
- Break the invisibility cycle: name, pay, record, seat. Ethical case must not depend on the business case.
- Keep two questions apart: who gets the shares, and who gets the chair.
- Law sets the floor, policy sets the practice, constitution sets the promise.
- Protect family harmony first; every spiral stage is a choice and avoidable.
- Process before person; decide who decides before deciding what.
- **Write the rule down before you need it.**

# Module 5: Management of Family Businesses

## 5.0 Module Overview

### 5.0.1 Welcome to the Module

- Growth forces family firms to change **how they are managed**, **how they innovate** and **how they expand beyond existing markets**.
- Three interconnected areas: **professionalisation**, **innovation**, **internationalisation**.
- **Central idea:** family control is both a **resource** and a **constraint**; its impact depends on how the family chooses to professionalise, innovate and internationalise.

| Area | Family control as resource | Family control as constraint |
|------|---------------------------|------------------------------|
| Professionalisation | Trust, speed, long-term relationships | Informal, person-driven decisions; resistance to outsiders |
| Innovation | Patient capital, long-term orientation, tacit knowledge, stakeholder networks | Desire to preserve control, identity and legacy discourages experimentation |
| Internationalisation | Patient capital, reputation, relationships, stewardship | Control concerns, capability gaps, unfamiliar markets, governance structures |

### 5.0.2 Learning Objectives

- Identify key **features, drivers, benefits and challenges** of professionalisation, innovation and internationalisation in family businesses.
- Explain how family ownership and control can both **enable and constrain** professional management, innovation and international expansion.
- Compare approaches: roles of **family members, non-family professionals, governance structures and external resources**.
- Apply frameworks such as the **willingness-ability perspective**.

## 5.1 Professionalisation in Family Business

### 5.1.1 Professionalisation: Global Evidence

#### Informal vs professionally run firms

| Dimension | Not professionally run | Professionally run |
|-----------|-----------------------|--------------------|
| Decisions | Informal; by founder or family | Clearly defined **roles, rules and management team** |
| Hiring and promotion | Family and personal networks | Against **stated criteria**; open to non-family talent |
| Information | Held closely by the family | Shared with managers who need it |
| Succession | Unplanned | **Planned years ahead**; explicit criteria and process |

#### Global and Indian evidence

- Many family firms have a board, largely due to regulation.
- Only about **15%** of board seats are independent or non-family.
- **Board is common, but real outside voice is not**: family systems take priority over business systems, so voice stays low.
- Indian (PwC survey) evidence: strengths in some areas; scope to improve **cross-industry board experience**, **inclusion of non-family members on the board** and **transition planning**.
- The stated barrier is **resistance from senior generations**, not absence of talent or rules.
- The issue is **voice and diversity on the board**, not merely having or populating a board.

#### Why professionalisation is urgent

| Reason | Explanation |
|--------|-------------|
| Competitive complexity | Markets move faster than one person can track; professional structure helps navigate it |
| External capital and resource persons | Banks, private equity and corporate partners expect professionalisation and **penalise** its absence |
| Cross-generation transition | Owners need a process trusted for **fairness, equity and equality**, not one person's discretion |
| Talent | Merit-based advancement retains family and outside talent, including **women** |

- Professionalisation is **not about removing the family**; it is about **changing how decisions get made** as firm and family grow.

### 5.1.2 Defining Professionalisation

#### Definition

- **Professionalisation:** the **deliberate replacement of informal, person-driven decisions with formal, rule-based, criteria-based ones** across the firm's **markets, resources, operations, management systems and culture**, carried out by people who meet the standard of a **professional manager**.
- A **process or journey, not an event or checklist**; requires formal top-down direction, not just intentions.
- It changes **how** decisions are made, not **who** makes them; a family member can be a professional manager.

#### Entrepreneurial vs professional management

| Aspect | Entrepreneurial / family management | Professional management |
|--------|------------------------------------|--------------------------|
| Basis | One person's judgment and relationships; centralised, informal | **Specialised roles**; defined decision process at the right delegated level |
| Scales by | Founder working harder and longer | **Adding capacity** and systems |
| Breaks down when | Business outgrows the capability or bandwidth of one person | **Systems outrun the trust needed to use them** |

#### Pyramid of organisational development (six steps)

| Step | Layer | Focus |
|------|-------|-------|
| 1 | Market | Customer needs; size of the market |
| 2 | Products and services | Meeting customer needs; customer satisfaction |
| 3 | Resources | Financial, human, factory set-up, capital |
| 4 | Operational systems | Accounting systems, marketing systems |
| 5 | Management systems | Management systems |
| 6 | Corporate culture | Culture |

- Focus first on steps 1 to 3 (the basics); steps 1 and 2 (a product or service meeting a customer need) are the foundation of any business.

#### Why a CEO or top management team is needed

- **Decision bandwidth:** how much one person can handle.
- **Succession readiness:** a defined role, skills and need that a successor can step into.
- **External credibility:** banks, investors and suppliers see a firm that will outlive one person.
- **Specialised judgment:** finance or operations specialists rather than a generalist.

### 5.1.3 Benefits of Professionalisation

#### Nine benefits in three clusters

| Cluster | Benefits |
|---------|----------|
| **People and talent** | (1) Merit-based selection, pay and promotion build **high-commitment work systems** and in-house training pipeline; (2) a real path for merit-based talent including **women** (removes glass or "mirrored" ceilings); (3) stronger **non-family commitment** through equal reward |
| **Governance and discipline** | (4) **Independent evaluation and incentives** with the same appraisal for family and non-family; (5) **institutional pressures** for transparency and disclosure; (6) **rules that bind family and non-family alike** |
| **Strategy and capital** | (7) Handling **competitive complexity** through specialised functions and credible partners; (8) **diversity of viewpoints** in growth and strategy choices; (9) easier **access to external capital** (banks, private equity, capital markets) |

- The clusters are interdependent: **people systems make governance credible, and credible governance unlocks external resources**.

#### People and talent

- Merit-based pay linked to performance rewards better performance, with a long-run payoff.
- Non-family members stay because they know they will be rewarded equally for similar talent and outcomes.
- **Example:** Godrej Consumer Products had a non-family CEO (Vivek Gambhir) for over a decade to 2020; market capitalisation rose from about **USD 700 million to about USD 9 billion** before family member **Nisaba Godrej** succeeded him. Professional structure allows moves from family CEO to outside CEO and back.

#### Governance and discipline

- A rule that binds only non-family staff is a **double standard**, not a rule.
- External pressure comes from banks, regulators, large customers and the **next generation**, who expect governance like peer firms (**isomorphic pressure**).
- Pressure can arrive through a **loan covenant** or a vendor expecting professional reporting.

#### Strategy and capital

- Good professional governance **lowers perceived risk**; opacity is itself a risk and external parties price risk.

### 5.1.4 Why Family Firms Resist Professionalisation

#### Resistance is rational

- Resistance is linked to **socio-emotional wealth (SEW)**, not stubbornness; it can last decades.
- **Olive-mill evidence:** family-controlled mills were less likely to join a cooperative that reduced failure risk, because joining meant **surrendering control**. The non-economic loss outweighed the economic gain; families knowingly choose the economically lesser option to protect SEW.
- Outside managers and boards are, on average, good for performance.

#### FIBER dimensions threatened

| FIBER dimension | What professionalisation threatens |
|-----------------|-----------------------------------|
| **F**amily control and influence | Board or professional CEO takes away the family's authority to decide |
| **I**dentity | Firm carries the family name; outside decisions may feel like loss of identity |
| **B**inding social ties | Close links with staff, suppliers and community replaced by impersonal formal systems |
| **E**motional attachment | Founder's memory and vision give way to criteria-based change |
| **R**enewal of family bonds through dynastic succession | Leadership assumed to pass to next generation vs merit-based succession |

#### Incentive problems

| Type | Nature | Remedy |
|------|--------|--------|
| **Type 1 (classical agency)** | Conflict between owners (principals) and hired non-family managers (agents); manager may act in own interest | Monitoring, performance incentives, independent monitoring board |
| **Type 2 (family-specific)** | **Altruism** (reluctance to discipline an underperforming relative), **entrenchment** (family managers not replaced regardless of performance), **self-control** (rule-setter must enforce rules against family) | Not fully addressed by conventional monitoring |

#### When resistance wins

- **Loss of family culture:** informality and speed that built the firm; rules and process appear to slow things.
- **Trust deficit:** sharing financial and strategic information with outsiders feels risky.
- **Career and status loss:** merit can expose a family member who leads only because of family.
- **Example:** the Tata Sons case (Cyrus Mistry's appointment) which reached the Supreme Court.

### 5.1.5 Who Is a Professional Manager?

#### Definition

- A professional manager is defined by **how they make decisions and exercise authority**, not by family membership.
- **Professional does not mean non-family.**

#### Five criteria

| # | Criterion | Professional | Not professional |
|---|-----------|-------------|------------------|
| 1 | Basis of decisions | **General principles**, transferable knowledge | Case-specific intuition, gut feeling |
| 2 | Client relationship | Client **trusts** expert judgment | Client merely obeys |
| 3 | Relationships | **Objective**, not clouded by personal or emotional ties | Personal or emotional ties |
| 4 | Status | Driven by **achievement** | Driven by family ties |
| 5 | Community | Belongs to a **voluntary professional community** that sets and reinforces standards | No external standard-setting peers |

- Authority built on accomplishment lasts; authority from relational ties can evaporate.

#### Family and professionalism matrix

| | Non-professional | Professional |
|---|------------------|--------------|
| **Family** | Placed by **birthright** or family ties; defers to informal family judgment over data | Inherits the role but **earns it** through repeated accomplishment; data-based; holds all to the same standard (**Harsh Mariwala at Marico**) |
| **Non-family** | Placed for loyalty rather than track record; defers to family wishes | Brought in for **track record or academic accomplishment**; expert whose judgment is trusted (**Vivek Gambhir at Godrej Consumer Products**) |

### 5.1.6 Professionalism Inside the Firm

#### Three-circle placement of a professional manager

| Position | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| Centre (family + ownership + business) | Family founder who is also CEO and shareholder |
| Business circle only | Non-family external manager; no family tie and no ownership (not even ESOPs) |
| Family + ownership only | Family shareholder not working day to day; passive or occasional expert |

- A person sits in these positions as a professional only if they meet the professional-manager criteria.

#### Four-room view

| Room | Basis | Role |
|------|-------|------|
| **Family room** | Love, unity, development | Manager's authority cannot come from here |
| **Owner room** | Power and influence of shareholding | Sets the way forward; not management authority |
| **Board room** | **Wisdom and judgment** | Hires and evaluates management; mostly family plus one or two independent directors |
| **Management room** | **Competence and execution** | Where the professional sits; hired for skill set |

#### Failure and fix

- **Failure:** authority in the management room granted on **family room or owner room standing alone**, driven by SEW and Type 2 agency incentives.
- **Fix:** grant authority to those who meet the five professional criteria; **match authority to competence**. Problems arise when competence lies in one room and authority in another.

### 5.1.7 The Professionalisation Process: The Variables

#### Six variables

| Variable | Examples |
|----------|----------|
| **Environment** | Competitive context, regulatory pressure, capital-market complexity, pace of change |
| **Family characteristics** | Family size, number of branches, generational stage, ownership structure |
| **Business characteristics** | Size and complexity |
| **Managerial approach** | Entrepreneurial vs functional leadership style |
| **Performance outcomes** | Results vs peers and own history; shape urgency and appetite for change |
| **Stakeholder effects** | Banks, customers, employees, family expectations |

#### Family ownership structure

| Structure | Effect on professionalisation |
|-----------|-------------------------------|
| **Controlling owner** (one person, couple or siblings) | Closer to a personal choice; other owners' agreement not needed |
| **Sibling partnership** | More co-owners to persuade |
| **Cousin consortium** | Most distant co-owners; costs, egos and financial and non-financial considerations differ |

- The **more loosely held the ownership, the harder it is to professionalise**.

#### Hardest combination and feedback loop

- **Hardest:** high complexity + weak building blocks + an already entrepreneurial style.
- **Performance outcomes and stakeholder effects form a feedback loop:** stakeholders watch performance and governance together and adjust expectations, which become the next round's pressure.

#### Progression

- First strengthen **market understanding, products and services, and resource base**; then build **operational systems, management systems and finally culture**.

### 5.1.8 Why the "Be More Like a Non-Family Business" Analogy Breaks Down

#### Core argument (Stewart and Hitt)

- Treating professionalisation as copying a non-family firm is a **mistake**; it is not a straight line, not a transferable template and is **context-dependent and multimodal**.
- Models should blend **agency theory and socio-emotional wealth theory**; professionalisation lacks a single meaning.

#### Kinship vs commercial logic

| | Kinship domain | Commercial domain |
|---|----------------|-------------------|
| Logic | Obligation, trust, emotion, reciprocity | Formal contracts, market demand, customer needs, credentials, market discipline |
| Strengths | Fast, informal, **patient long-term capital** | **Scalable**, impersonal, economic logic, legible to outsiders |
| Limits | Family alone is neither large enough nor sufficiently skilled | Cannot generate the family firm's own advantage |

- The professional's opportunity lies at the **interface of the two domains**.

#### Principal-to-principal conflict

| Conflict | Description | Can non-family governance address it? |
|----------|-------------|----------------------------------------|
| **Type 1** | Principal vs agent | Yes; designed for it |
| **Type 2** | Family principal's entrenchment and altruism | Partially at best |
| **Principal-principal** | Two or more family owners (active or passive) disagree on direction or strategy | **No**; employees take sides |

#### Four reasons the analogy fails

- Non-family models cannot recognise **socio-emotional wealth** and non-financial goals.
- They cannot resolve **principal-principal conflict**.
- Tacit **kin-based judgment** can be fast and outperform formal routines under uncertainty.
- The **domain interface**: the best outcome is an adviser who helps clean up the family business.
- Not every family business must go professional; there is **no single template**.

### 5.1.9 Three Paths to Professionalisation

#### Overview

- Same destination: a family business that runs like a professional company.
- Paths are **not mutually exclusive**; different parts of the firm may use different paths.

| Path | Best when | Strengths | Risks and safeguards |
|------|-----------|-----------|----------------------|
| **1. Family members** | Current or next generation is both **able and willing** | Years of exposure; understand risks and past mistakes | **Leniency in evaluation** (Type 2 agency); demand accountability against the same criteria as a professional |
| **2. Non-family insiders** | Long-serving, trusted, talented people who lacked authority or exposure | Firm-specific knowledge; low trust risk | Objectivity must be earned by title and mandate; **external benchmarking** still needed; may retain old habits of deference |
| **3. External hires** | Expertise missing in both family and insiders (new technology, science, market); capability gap, **turnaround or change management** | Independent track record; expertise | Lack firm-specific knowledge; must earn trust; usually learn faster than expected |

#### Examples

- **Path 1:** Nisaba Godrej succeeded the non-family CEO only after building her own track record (professional development first, then family move-in).
- **Path 2:** **T. K. Kurien** became Wipro CEO as a long-term insider while the Premji family kept board control; evaluated like an outside hire.

### 5.1.10 Types of Professionalised Family Firms

| # | Type | Key features |
|---|------|--------------|
| 1 | **Minimally professional firm** | Holds back **capacity**, not just willingness (cognitive, cultural, emotional barriers); long tenure, less formally educated family leadership, strong kinship obligations; single person at the helm is the **bottleneck**; near-term financial performance may still be fine, but scaling needs change |
| 2 | **Wealth-dispensing private firm** | Family's own capital used to extract **private benefits** of control; resists independent governance as a threat to privacy; with several branches, a **race to the bottom** as each branch seeks to maximise extraction |
| 3 | **Entrepreneurially operated firm** | Informal by **deliberate choice**; bricolage and tacit knowledge; advantage where family and business logic overlap; not a capacity failure; must watch the environment and professionalise when it stops rewarding this |
| 4 | **Entrepreneurial family business group** | Legally separate affiliated firms sharing kinship, trust, reputation and **internal markets for capital and labour**; works especially with **weak external institutions**; dominant form of medium and large business outside the Anglo-Saxon context (e.g., Tata, Murugappa, Birla; also Korea, China, South America, Europe) |
| 5 | **Pseudo-professional public firm** | Appears professional but extracts private benefits using **other shareholders' capital** |
| 6 | **Hybrid professional firm** | Managed by family **and** hired professionals (often without ownership stakes); ownership stays concentrated in the family; very high family control run as a professional organisation |

#### Warning signals in a pseudo-professional public firm

- Reputed vs small, family-loyal **auditors**.
- **Dividend policy** (pay-out vs ploughing back).
- **Related-party transactions** and **transfer pricing** enriching other family-owned firms.
- **Above-market pay** to family members.
- **Pyramid structures**.
- **CSR** funded partly with minority shareholders' money, building the family's SEW at their cost.

### 5.1.11 Impact on Performance

#### Three studies

| Study | Finding | Caveat |
|-------|---------|--------|
| **Denmark** | Family succession lowers return on assets by about **4 percentage points** vs an outside CEO (using firstborn child's gender to address self-selection) | Not always true; some family CEOs strengthened the firm. When the environment rewards professional structure, firms appointing an outside CEO when needed perform better |
| **US (Villalonga and Amit)** | **Descendant-run** family firms are valued lower than founder-run or professionally run ones | Context-specific |
| **Belgium (Dekker et al.)** | Several professionalisation dimensions help | Effects are **conditional** |

#### Dekker et al. dimensions

- **Non-family governance involvement:** positive only with real **decentralisation** and fewer financial controls.
- **Formal HR control systems:** positive.
- **Decentralisation of authority:** positive.
- **Formal financial control systems:** no strong results.
- Top-management-team activeness is part of the construct but not separately discussed.
- Net: formal professional governance, especially independent external members, plus good HR systems and decentralisation give positive results.

#### Overall

- Gains are **not automatic**; appointing an outside CEO or adding structure does not guarantee results.
- Benefits are strongest when professionalisation fits context, enables merit-based decisions and gives managers authority and resources.

### 5.1.12 Balancing Family Control and Professionalisation

#### Three kinds of control

| Control | Treatment in professionalisation |
|---------|----------------------------------|
| **Ownership control** | Generally stays with the family (not at issue) |
| **Strategic control** | Some is shared with a professional (insider or outsider) |
| **Operational control** | Much is delegated first, to an outsider or another family member |

- Delegation must be matched with **accountability**: who is accountable, to whom, and whether it is driven by family ties or business logic.
- Accountability covers the twin goals: financial wealth **and** the family's non-financial wealth goals.

#### Evidence

- Firm value rises with professionalisation, but the **curve flattens** when more control is transferred without the right balance of accountability.

#### Facilitators

| Facilitator | Explanation |
|-------------|-------------|
| **Willingness** | Firm may have ability (talent) but willingness must be addressed; family must accept need for external talent and systems |
| **Pace** | Map to business stage and family stage (which generation, current requirements) |
| **Competence matched with authority** | Clarity on which room or institution takes which decisions; no overriding across rooms |
| **Governance** | Strength of governance underpins intent, process and later strengthening |

## 5.2 Innovation in Family Business

### 5.2.1 The Innovation Paradox

#### The paradox

- Family firms are built around **concentrated control, patient capital and protection of what exists**, the opposite of what innovation seems to require.
- Yet many innovate across generations: a firm that cannot renew products, processes or business model gives the **next generation less reason to join**. Innovation and succession are linked.

#### Four tensions

| Family tendency | Innovation requirement |
|-----------------|------------------------|
| Concentrated decisions and rights | Willingness to bring in **outside expertise** |
| Multi-decade horizon, patient capital | Risk-taking, quick turnaround, **failure tolerance**, repeated trying |
| **SEW**, continuity and identity | Willingness to **cannibalise** the firm's best-known product |
| Deep knowledge of one business | **Openness** to outside knowledge and models |

#### Four reasons innovation is now pressing

| Pressure | Explanation |
|----------|-------------|
| **Technology pace** | Less time and room for hard innovation choices; the firm can be left with nothing to hand over |
| **Multi-generation overlap** | Each generation has a different view of and tolerance for risk |
| **Global competition** | Well-funded, well-networked competitors (e-commerce, quick commerce) on risky rather than patient capital |
| **Talent expectations** | Employees expect investment in their skills and future |

### 5.2.2 Theories of Innovation

| Concept | Meaning |
|---------|---------|
| **Creative destruction** (Schumpeter) | New ways of organising continually emerge and destroy old ways; survival depends on willingness to destroy the old base to create value in the new order |
| **Incremental innovation** | Improving what already exists by a few degrees |
| **Radical innovation** | Creating something **qualitatively new** |
| **Exploration** | Searching for new possibilities |
| **Exploitation** | Refining and executing existing activities better |
| **Sustaining innovation** | Innovating for existing markets and customers |
| **Disruptive innovation** (Christensen) | Entrants serve **overlooked segments**, stabilise there, then move into the mainstream and alter industry structure |

- Firms need **both exploration and exploitation**.
- These ideas apply to family firms (family tensions, generational patterns, external environment).

### 5.2.3 The Ability-and-Willingness Paradox

#### Definitions

| Dimension | Question | Drivers |
|-----------|----------|---------|
| **Ability** | Can the firm marshal the resources to innovate? | Access to resources including capital; **decision-making discretion**; freedom from short-term investor pressure |
| **Willingness** | Does leadership want to take the risk? | Control, identity, continuity and other **non-financial (SEW)** concerns |

- Differences among family firms arise from the **mismatch** between the two, not from both being high or both low.
- Family ability is often higher: concentrated ownership gives **patient capital** and multi-year commitment. Professionally run firms under AGM, board and investor pressure may have money but not patience.
- Willingness is **inconstant**: the same control creates SEW stakes (prestige, identity, commitments, e.g., supporting existing employees).
- Result: greater **heterogeneity** across family firms than non-family firms.
- **Example:** Reliance's Campa Cola challenging the soft drinks duopoly shows how patient capital and multi-year backing can break conventional wisdom.

#### Ability-willingness matrix

| | Low willingness | High willingness |
|---|-----------------|------------------|
| **Low ability** | **Greatest survival risk**; no resources and no wish to change ("this is how my grandfather did it"); innovation becomes a necessity, not a choice | Wants to innovate (often the next generation) but faces **resource, capability or capacity constraints** |
| **High ability** | Resources exist but held back by perceived risk to SEW; identity ("we have always been this kind of company"), or senior generation not letting go | **Innovation leader**: resources, networks, talent, discretion and appetite |

- Firms can sit in any quadrant and **move across quadrants** as financial or non-financial circumstances change.

### 5.2.4 Family Control Suppressing Innovation

#### Socio-emotional wealth and loss aversion

- **Olive-mill analogy:** joining a risk-pooling cooperative corresponds to funding a risky R&D project. Both lower expected failure and raise long-run return but require shedding some control; **aversion to losing control and identity overrides aversion to losing money**.
- **Above-aspiration pattern:** when performance is satisfactory (not near a survival threshold), family firms are cautious and spend **less on R&D** than non-family firms. Behaviour changes near a survival threshold, when passing the business on is in question.

#### Type 2 agency in innovation

| Problem | Innovation form |
|---------|-----------------|
| **Parental altruism** | Underperforming family-led project not evaluated by outsider standards |
| **Entrenchment** | Family member's pet project continues; better performers cannot be promoted above a family member |
| **Self-control** | The champion also decides whether to continue or kill the project; identity linked to it |

- Outcome: resources go to familiar people or projects instead of the strongest opportunities.

### 5.2.5 Family Control Enabling Innovation

#### Patient capital and related capitals

| Enabler | How it helps innovation |
|---------|-------------------------|
| **Patient financial capital** | Supports long-payback projects (e.g., a decade); less suited to quick-turnaround venture-style bets |
| **Human capital and tacit knowledge** | Deep business knowledge; same R&D spend goes further; right team assembled efficiently |
| **Social capital** | Trusted network of customers, suppliers and stakeholders to test products and give honest feedback; resources combined at lower cost than open market |
| **Survivability capital** | Buffer through uncertain tenure and hard stretches until milestones are reached |

#### Long-term orientation

| Element | Meaning |
|---------|---------|
| **Futurity** | Extended decision horizon; invest now for results years away (e.g., a product that becomes mainstream in 7 to 8 years) |
| **Continuity** | Long-standing values carried across generations keep R&D investment going |
| **Perseverance** | Small innovation steps continue through setbacks and recessions |

- The same features that restrict innovation can, under different conditions, enable experimentation and renewal.

### 5.2.6 Exploration vs Exploitation Across Generations

| Aspect | Exploitation | Exploration |
|--------|--------------|-------------|
| Focus | Extract more value within the firm's current activities | Seek new opportunities outside current activities |
| Fit with family firms | **Natural**: efficiency, cost focus, **income stability** for the family | Often **forced** on them as exploitation reaches its limits |

#### What drives exploration

- **Generational change:** by the second generation, the first has already optimised the business and the environment has moved on; **younger siblings** often have more freedom (ownership and strategic control tend to move to the elder sibling); some families run **family incubators** within the entrepreneurial family galaxy.
- **Professionalisation:** professionals and outside talent bring fresh ideas.
- **Formal strategic planning:** formalises the search process and multigenerational involvement.
- **Willingness** is the precondition for exploring within and outside.
- Goal: **ambidexterity**, doing both exploration and exploitation, institutionalised through multi-generational involvement, professionalisation and strategic planning.

### 5.2.7 What Facilitates Innovation in Family Firms

#### Professionalisation targets specific failure modes

| Type 2 problem | Professionalisation response |
|----------------|------------------------------|
| Parental altruism | Independent board or outside member, loyal to the firm rather than the family, holds family members to the **same standard** |
| Entrenchment | Non-family or independent director/peer with no stake in the **legacy product** can evaluate it |
| Self-control | **Staged review** that separates the project's champion from the **kill decision**; limits losses |

#### Governance mechanisms

| Mechanism | Function |
|-----------|----------|
| **Formal strategic planning** | Channels generational energy into real innovation; replaces gut-driven roles with described contributions |
| **Chartered innovation committee** | Real authority; includes outside expertise (may hold no formal firm position); unbiased, broader scope |
| **Ring-fenced exploration budget** | Protects exploration from being crowded out by safer exploitation returns; limits budget escalation (chasing bad money with good); dedicated budget with a **kill switch** |

- Each mechanism targets a specific failure mode, not general good-governance advice.

#### Open innovation

| Form | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Sourcing in** | Deliberately bringing in outside ideas, technology and partnerships ("why should all innovation happen under my roof?") |
| **Sourcing out** | Letting an internal idea leave via **licensing or spin-off** so others can develop it |

- Open innovation is a direct countermeasure to **insularity**; it supplies new ideas at the seed stage or commercialises assets the firm cannot fully exploit.

### 5.2.8 Succession and Innovation: Passing the Torch Without Losing the Edge

#### What must pass on

- Innovation is passed on as both **project** and **outlook** (why R&D matters).
- **Institutionalised capacity:** governance structures, milestones, measurement points, decision rights on further funding, outcomes expected at each stage, and guardrails.
- **Relationships and risk appetite** do not transfer automatically and must be actively transferred.

#### What changes hands

| Side | Transfers? |
|------|-----------|
| **Ability resources** (ownership, capital, board, governance, monitoring, management) | Easier to transfer |
| **Willingness** (successor's wish to take on the role, including innovation) | **Not inherited automatically**; built over time and shaped by the successor's own reading of the changed environment |

- A succession plan that assumes willingness is automatic will run into problems.
- **Authority must outlive the person who chartered it**; for example, a ring-fenced budget carried on regardless of who started it.
- **Early involvement** of the next generation in new projects builds belief and ownership of the legacy.
- Governance for innovation must **survive a leadership change**; the goal is continuity without resistance to change.

## 5.3 Internationalisation of Family Business

### 5.3.1 Benefits and Costs of Internationalisation

#### Four motives

| Motive | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| **Market seeking** | Larger or faster-growing, underserved foreign market; family strengths can be marketed |
| **Efficiency seeking** | Scale or replicate an existing footprint for growth or specialisation |
| **Strategic-asset seeking** | Acquire a brand, technology or network, usually through acquisition, to use abroad and at home |
| **Resource seeking** | Natural resources or low-cost inputs unavailable at home (e.g., magnets from China) |

#### Costs

| Cost | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Capital commitment** | Capital tied up overseas that may be needed at home |
| **Managerial complexity** | Different skill sets; coordination across time zones |
| **Integration risk** | Acquisitions may not deliver as promised |
| **Liability of foreignness** | Extra costs of operating in an unfamiliar place |

#### Family firm twist

- **Ability is often high** (financial strength, patient capital, long horizon) and is assessed at **firm level**.
- **Willingness is variable** and often differs by individual family member; going abroad may mean **shedding some control**.

### 5.3.2 Theories of Internationalisation

#### Uppsala model (process theory)

| Stage | Mode | Commitment |
|-------|------|------------|
| 1 | Sporadic export | Lowest |
| 2 | Export via independent agent | Low; firm begins learning |
| 3 | Sales subsidiary | Direct presence; deeper market learning |
| 4 | Production subsidiary / FDI | Highest; once knowledge and trust are established |

- Core idea: **risk is reduced through learning**; commitment rises as knowledge grows.
- **Liability of foreignness (outsidership):** host customers see the firm and its products as an outsider's.
- **Liability of origin:** additional discount in perceived quality when an emerging-market firm enters a developed market.

#### Dunning's OLI (eclectic paradigm)

| Letter | Advantage | Examples |
|--------|-----------|----------|
| **O** Ownership | Firm-specific assets | Technology, brand, managerial skill |
| **L** Location | Host-country (and home-country) factors favouring production | |
| **I** Internalisation | Better off coordinating activities inside the firm than contracting out | |

| Advantages held | Predicted mode |
|-----------------|----------------|
| O only | **Exporting** |
| O + L | **Licensing** |
| O + L + I | **FDI** (higher commitment) |

#### Transaction cost economics (TCE)

- Protecting valuable knowledge from partner **opportunism** is costly (monitoring, incentive alignment), so the firm may **own the operation** rather than contract.
- The firm is a nexus of contracts; relative cost of external contracting vs internalising explains mode and extent.

#### Dimensions of internationalisation

| Dimension | Meaning |
|-----------|---------|
| **Speed** | How early in the firm's life cycle |
| **Intensity / degree** | Share of revenue from foreign activity, or share of resources committed abroad |
| **Scope** | Number of countries; complexity rises with scope |

#### Network model

- Firms leverage existing relationships; entering a new country requires building host-country networks and deeper commitment.
- Coordinating national networks as one system is costly; firms often **follow an existing partner** abroad.

#### Institutional and accelerated views

| Theory | Idea |
|--------|------|
| **Born globals** | Internationalise from inception, often within the **first 3 years**, in multiple and distant markets (psychic, cultural, political, regulatory distance); often driven by founder's international experience and networks; early entry brings shock costs and learning advantages |
| **Springboard theory** | Firms from less advanced home environments internationalise aggressively (high risk tolerance, possibly undervalued or distressed targets) to acquire strategic assets unavailable at home |
| **Institutional support** | Supportive, facilitative home institutions help firms internationalise |
| **Institutional escapism** | Voids or constraints in home institutions push firms to invest abroad |

- Escapism is one mechanism within the institution-based view and does not necessarily rival the support mechanism; firms may adopt either.

### 5.3.3 Prior Research and Performance Evidence

#### Three broad influences

1. **Extent of family control** (ownership, CEO or board presence).
2. **Type of internationalisation decision**: speed, degree, scope and entry mode (resources at risk).
3. **Home-country conditions**.

- Evidence shows both positive and negative family vs non-family relationships.
- Earlier view: family firms internationalise less to protect resources; but if **survival is at risk**, they may internationalise.
- **Upper echelons:** younger generation educated abroad with professional networks; **peer effect** from other internationalised families.
- Family control alone does not fully explain; family firms internationalise **under certain conditions**.

#### Emerging markets

- Mainly **inward FDI** (lower risk for family firms), which can act as a stepping stone to exporting; export is lower risk than outward investment.
- Global family firms (a large share of the world economy) invest in growing markets such as India.
- Example: when inward FDI was restricted in Indian automobiles, foreign firms entered through **joint ventures** with family businesses, which later separated and began exporting independently.
- Growing trend of **outward FDI** from emerging markets (e.g., Samsung, Hyundai, and Indian firms).

### 5.3.4 Advantages for Family Firms in Internationalisation

| Advantage | Explanation |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Patient capital and long-term horizon** | Can fund years of investment before a new market turns profitable; **no need for many outside approvals**; risk that patience turns into **ultra-caution**, so metrics and close monitoring are needed |
| **Reputation and relational capital** | The family name substitutes for a local track record; firms **follow an existing relationship** (anchor client) abroad; relationships do not transfer automatically across sectors or markets |
| **Stewardship** | Committed, patient expansion plans across multiple quarters; no short-term financial engineering |
| **Unified decision-making** | Concentrated ownership gives continuity and consistency; once the family is aligned, few stakeholders remain to convince, so resources stay committed even over a decade or two |

### 5.3.5 Factors Influencing Internationalisation

#### Four levels

| Level | Factors |
|-------|---------|
| **Firm** | Resources (e.g., unique IP); size and slack; **product transferability**; **capital intensity** of the entry mode (export low, FDI high); ownership: extent of family ownership, **blockholders** (e.g., foreign institutional investors bringing networks), dispersion of ownership across members and branches; financial and non-financial goals |
| **Industry** | Industry-level advantages; competitors' behaviour; **peer pressure** and **mimetic isomorphism**; other families in the family's network |
| **Country** | Home-country institutions (regulatory, cognitive, normative): support, escape, springboard; host-country institutions conducive to inward investment; **institutional distance** (regulation, policy, culture, quality perceptions, geographic distance) |
| **Upper echelons** | CEO or chair attributes: foreign education, travel, prior foreign exposure, networks (family, professional, business), strategic-planning routines; family member as CEO is aware of financial and non-financial goals |

- Levels **interact**; these shape export vs FDI, speed and degree.

#### Additional family layer

- Firm: **familiness** as a resource. Industry: other family firms in the social network as peers. Country: norms unique to family behaviour. Leadership: family CEO, or family chair with professional CEO.
- Outcomes differ from non-family firms because family and business systems are integrated.

### 5.3.6 Challenges and Obstacles of Internationalisation

| Obstacle | Explanation | Mitigation |
|----------|-------------|------------|
| **Control dilution and resistance to outside capital** | Foreign set-up needs capital beyond internal accruals; loans, private equity or a partner mean shared control or accountability. The firm has the **ability** to attract capital but may lack the **willingness**; opportunity is weighed first against risk of control loss | Structures can be designed by the owners (e.g., a separate company where investors hold stakes) |
| **Insularity** | Long-tenured talent applies the home-market playbook to a market needing a different one | A **local partner** with deep market knowledge |
| **Succession timing** | A foreign commitment during a leadership transition is heavy for the successor | Decision should be taken by the **incoming** person rather than the one leaving; no single answer |

### 5.3.7 The Risk-Investment Trade-off

#### Two-dimensional risk-return

- Non-family firms weigh risk and return on **financial wealth** only.
- Family firms weigh **financial wealth** and **socio-emotional (non-financial) wealth**: control, identity, continuity, family income stability.
- Hence a tendency to prefer **domestic growth and diversification**, because internationalisation implies control loss and external capital.

#### Mixed gamble

- When there is a genuine threat to domestic survival or **sustained underperformance**, staying home also threatens financial wealth **and** non-financial goals (lifestyle and income stability of family members).
- The family may flip its risk preference to a **more tolerant approach** and internationalise. This is the **mixed gamble** of internationalisation.

### 5.3.8 Reasons for Family Firms to Under-Internationalise

- Under-internationalisation reflects **willingness gaps** (risk-return calculus) and **ability gaps**.

| Resource or capability gap | Explanation |
|----------------------------|-------------|
| **Financial resources** | Desire to retain ownership control limits outside equity, giving a smaller resource pool and weakening the patient-capital advantage |
| **Managerial capability** | Recruiting from or accommodating the family narrows the talent pool; thin bench to run foreign operations |
| **International knowledge** | Tacit and experiential; absent without prior exposure |
| **Alliances and networks** | No existing network to follow makes entry costly |

- **Professionalisation** helps by widening access to external talent, capital, international knowledge and networks.

### 5.3.9 Governance and Its Impact on Internationalisation

| Governance feature | Effect on internationalisation |
|--------------------|-------------------------------|
| **Family member as board chair** | Strongly associated with **less** internationalisation; can slow or deny outside capital and foreign entry because of dilution concerns |
| **Experienced independent directors** | Do not worry as much about control dilution; show how control can be maintained through governance and monitoring, structures and risk mitigation; give the family confidence; effect stronger for **very distant markets** |
| **Cross-generational family involvement** (multiple generations and branches) | **Positively** associated: broader support for expansion and for feeding the growing family from one company |

- Internationalisation is shaped by **how governance is structured and authority distributed**, not family involvement alone.

### 5.3.10 Internationalisation Process and Strategy

#### Entry modes

| Entry mode | Control | Commitment and risk |
|-----------|---------|---------------------|
| **Exporting** | Least | Least |
| **Licensing / franchising** | Relatively low | Relatively light |
| **Joint venture** | Shared control and resources | Moderate |
| **Wholly owned subsidiary: acquisition or greenfield** | Greatest | Greatest resource commitment and risk |

- Each step up trades **more control and expected return for greater resource commitment and more risk**.
- **Family CEOs favour lower-commitment modes**, limiting financial and socio-emotional exposure.
- **Joint ventures** suit firms needing local knowledge, capabilities or alliances while retaining some control.
- Entry mode is where **strategy and structure** become an observable choice; it is determined by **resource gaps, governance and risk calculus**.

## 5.4 Case Simulation: Arunachalam Pumps

### 5.4.1 Case Set-up

- **Firm:** a pump maker in Coimbatore, founded 1979 by S. Arunachalam ("Ayya", 76), turnover about INR 640 crore, EBITDA margin falling from 14% to 9.5%; two plants, 1,400 staff, 3,200 dealers.
- **Bank covenant (12 months):** appoint independent directors and a qualified CFO, and adopt a board-approved succession plan, to fund a new plant.
- **Family:** founder owns 58%; son Karthik (sales, assumed successor, never run a P&L) 14%; daughter Meera (accounts, MNC finance background) 14%; nephew Prakash and his branch 14% (Plant 1 rejects 4.8% vs 1.9% at Plant 2); non-family GM Operations JR; granddaughter Nila (EV start-up engineer).
- Board: Ayya, Karthik, Meera and a family auditor; no independent directors and no family charter.
- Three stages: **who signs after Ayya (professionalise)**, **the smart pump that eats the old one (innovate)**, **Nairobi or Brescia (go abroad)**. Twelve decisions, scored out of 36.

### 5.4.2 Stage 1: Professionalisation Decisions

| Decision | Sound response | Principle applied |
|----------|----------------|-------------------|
| Who leads | Assess **all** candidates (family, insider, external) against the same professional criteria; do not name Karthik by default | Five criteria; three paths as one open choice; professional does not mean non-family |
| Where to start | Start with the founder's visible **willingness**, then participative family decisions, written policies, managers, strategy, structure; the **professional board is the capstone (step 7)** | A board added first is decorative and reversible |
| Plant 1 under-performance | Same appraisal and improvement plan as Plant 2, reviewed by someone other than Ayya | One standard for family and non-family; removes the self-control problem |
| Passive branch seeking dividends and information | **Owners' forum and written family charter** (dividend policy, information rights), separate from the board, with a **buy-back option** for exit | Principal-principal conflict; independent directors address Type 1 agency, not owner disputes |

- Raising dividends to buy quiet invites a **race to the bottom**; giving a manager a board seat mixes the **owner room with the board room**.
- Ownership stage shifts from controlling owner towards sibling partnership with a cousin branch, so participative agreement becomes necessary.

### 5.4.3 Stage 2: Innovation Decisions

| Decision | Sound response | Principle applied |
|----------|----------------|-------------------|
| Reading low R&D spend with comparable design output | Efficiency is real but **conditional** (tacit knowledge, tight monitoring); it reflects **exploitation** and says nothing about ability to explore | Spending measures input; look at activities and outputs |
| Position on ability-willingness grid after margins recover | **High ability, low willingness** | Resources and cash exist; legacy product and SEW hold willingness down |
| Why the family changed its mind after a lost tender | Performance fell **below aspiration**, so family and economic goals converged and willingness rose | Willingness driven by threat can fade, so lock it into governance |
| Single governance change | A **chartered innovation committee** chaired by an independent director with stage gates, so the champion and her mother (CFO) do not decide funding | Separates champion from fund-or-kill decision; handles altruism, entrenchment, self-control; survives succession |

- A ring-fenced budget alone secures money but leaves decision problems; a formal plan alone does not say who decides; leaving decisions to the founder fails when he leaves.

### 5.4.4 Stage 3: Internationalisation Decisions

| Decision | Sound response | Principle applied |
|----------|----------------|-------------------|
| Motives of two offers | Nairobi (Kenyan irrigation partner) is **market-seeking**; Brescia (loss-making Italian pump maker, about EUR 38 million) is **strategic-asset-seeking** | Springboard logic: acquire certifications, engineering and brand |
| Primary move | **51:49 joint venture** with the Kenyan partner for assembly and service, with an option to raise the stake (Godrej-Darling route: 51% in 2011 to 90% by 2015) | Network model; fills knowledge and alliance gaps at moderate commitment without outside equity |
| Why the family prefers Kenya | **Mixed gamble**: financial return vs SEW; the Italian deal needs outside equity in a distant, unfamiliar market | Control dilution; flips only when staying put is riskier |
| Next chairman | A family chair is acceptable if balanced by **experienced international independent directors** and active third-generation involvement | Family chair associated with less internationalisation; independents and cross-generational involvement counter it |

- Full acquisition with outside equity while resource gaps are open is premature (Corus and SsangYong are cautionary parallels); greenfield with no local knowledge wastes the firm's best asset, its alliance; export-only leaves knowledge and alliance gaps unfilled.

### 5.4.5 Model Recommendation

1. **Professionalisation:** assess every candidate against the same criteria; combine paths (e.g., external CEO now while family members build records); start with founder willingness, then participation and policies, with the board as capstone; hold relatives to the same standard; give the passive branch an owners' forum and charter.
2. **Innovation:** separate ability from willingness; willingness rose only when performance fell below aspiration; lock it in with a chartered committee and stage gates.
3. **Internationalisation:** go where the advantage travels and an alliance exists (joint venture with a path to majority); treat the acquisition as a later springboard; balance a family chair with international independent directors and third-generation involvement.

## 5.5 Module Synthesis

### 5.5.1 Closing Synthesis

- Across all three areas, family control acts as **both resource and constraint**: patient capital, deep knowledge, trusted relationships and concentrated ownership vs control concerns, insularity, Type 2 agency problems and reluctance to use outside talent or capital.
- **Outcomes depend on conditions, not family ownership alone.**

| Area | Works when |
|------|-----------|
| Professionalisation | Competence and authority are aligned |
| Innovation | Ability is matched by willingness and supported by governance |
| Internationalisation | Long-term resources combine with managerial capability, networks, governance and risk tolerance |

- **Governance is the integrating mechanism** that turns distinctive family resources into sustainable growth.

## Quick Revision Summary

### Key Definitions

- **Professionalisation:** deliberate replacement of informal, person-driven decisions with formal, rule-based, criteria-based ones across markets, resources, operations, management systems and culture, carried out by professional managers; changes **how**, not **who**.
- **Professional manager:** decides by general principles and objective criteria; status from achievement; member of a voluntary professional community. **Professional does not mean non-family.**
- **FIBER (SEW):** Family control, Identity, Binding social ties, Emotional attachment, Renewal of bonds through dynastic succession.
- **Type 1 agency:** owner vs hired manager. **Type 2:** altruism, entrenchment, self-control. **Principal-principal:** conflict among family owners, beyond non-family governance.
- **Ability:** can the firm marshal resources, discretion and patience. **Willingness:** does the family want to take the risk given control, identity, continuity.
- **Exploration** (new possibilities) vs **exploitation** (refine existing); **ambidexterity** = both.
- **Mixed gamble:** family weighs financial return and SEW; flips to tolerate international risk when domestic survival is threatened.
- **Liability of foreignness / outsidership;** **liability of origin** (emerging to developed market).

### Important Relations (no numerical formulas)

- Authority should match competence: $\text{Management room authority} \Leftarrow \text{competence (five criteria)}$, not family or owner standing.
- OLI entry choice: $O \Rightarrow \text{Export}$; $O+L \Rightarrow \text{Licensing}$; $O+L+I \Rightarrow \text{FDI}$.
- Entry-mode ladder: Exporting < Licensing/Franchising < Joint venture < Wholly owned subsidiary (acquisition or greenfield) in control, commitment and risk.
- Uppsala: sporadic export -> independent agent -> sales subsidiary -> production subsidiary/FDI.

### Must-Remember Points

- **Nine benefits** in three clusters: people and talent; governance and discipline; strategy and capital.
- **Resistance is rational** (olive-mill cooperative: control valued over economic gain). Triggers: loss of culture, trust deficit, status loss.
- **Five criteria** of a professional; **three paths** (family, non-family insider, external hire; not mutually exclusive).
- **Six variables** shaping the process; ownership structure: controlling owner, sibling partnership, cousin consortium (looser ownership is harder).
- **Six types:** minimally professional; wealth-dispensing private; entrepreneurially operated; entrepreneurial family business group; pseudo-professional public; hybrid professional.
- **Impact on performance is conditional** (Denmark: about 4 percentage point ROA drop with family succession; US: descendant-run valued lower; Belgium: HR systems and decentralisation help). Value rises then flattens without accountability.
- **Three control types:** ownership stays with the family; strategic shared; operational delegated first.
- **Innovation paradox** with four tensions; four pressures: technology pace, generation overlap, global competition, talent expectations.
- **Innovation concepts:** creative destruction, incremental vs radical, exploration vs exploitation, disruptive innovation.
- **Ability-willingness matrix:** low-low = greatest survival risk; high ability with low willingness = capacity held back by SEW; high-high = innovation leader.
- **Suppressors:** loss aversion, above-aspiration caution, altruism, entrenchment, self-control. **Enablers:** patient, human/tacit, social, survivability capital; futurity, continuity, perseverance.
- **Facilitators:** independent evaluation (separate champion from kill decision), formal strategic planning, chartered innovation committee, ring-fenced exploration budget, open innovation (sourcing in and out).
- **Succession:** ability resources transfer; willingness does not automatically; governance for innovation must outlive the leader.
- **Four motives** (market, efficiency, strategic-asset, resource); **four costs** (capital, managerial complexity, integration risk, liability of foreignness).
- **Theories:** Uppsala, OLI, TCE, network model, born globals (first 3 years), springboard, institutional support vs escapism.
- **Advantages:** patient capital, reputation and relational capital, stewardship, unified decision-making.
- **Four factor levels:** firm, industry, country, upper echelons (plus family layer).
- **Four capability gaps:** financial, managerial, knowledge, alliances.
- **Governance:** family chair lowers internationalisation; experienced independent directors (especially distant markets) and cross-generational involvement raise it.
- **Family CEOs prefer lower-commitment modes;** joint ventures give local knowledge with partial control.

# Module 6: Family Business Governance

## 6.0 Module Overview

### 6.0.1 Welcome to the Module

- As a family business grows, informal arrangements become insufficient for **decision-making, family relationships and ownership responsibilities**.
- The challenge is not to create more structures, but to ensure the **right decisions are made by the right people in the right forums**.
- The module links the **three-circle model** (family, business, ownership) with the **Four-Room model** (Board Room, Management Room, Family Room, Owner Room).
- Governance structures **define responsibilities, establish accountability and coordinate the interests** of family members, business leaders and owners.

| Circle | Structures covered |
|--------|--------------------|
| **Business circle** | Board of directors, composition, independent directors, board committees, advisory board |
| **Family circle** | Family forum, family council, family constitution, family office, trusts, family bank, holding companies |
| **Ownership circle** | Ownership choices, ownership strategy, shareholders' agreement, owner council, shareholders' assembly |

- For each structure the focus is on **what it does, why it matters, how it relates to other structures and when it becomes necessary**.

### 6.0.2 Learning Outcomes

- Explain the roles of governance structures across the family, business and ownership circles using the **Three-Circle and Four-Room models**.
- Differentiate the responsibilities of **boards, family councils, owner councils and management structures**.
- Evaluate **board composition, independence and committee structures** in relation to effective oversight and accountability.
- Analyse the purpose and interrelationships of **family forums, family constitutions, family offices and trusts**.
- Assess **ownership structures and governance arrangements** for suitability to a family business's objectives and stage of development.

## 6.1 Governance in the Business Circle

### 6.1.1 Governance Across the Three Circles and Four Rooms

#### Definition: Governance

- **Governance** establishes **how decisions are made, who holds authority and where different responsibilities belong**.
- The **three-circle model** identifies the **systems** involved (family, business, ownership).
- The **Four-Room model** gives those interests a **place to make decisions** and distinguishes the conversations and decisions appropriate to each room.
- Problem case: founder discusses strategy at the dinner table, family disagreements influence board decisions, ownership questions are settled informally.

#### The four rooms

| Room | Main responsibility |
|------|---------------------|
| **Owner Room** | Who may own, how ownership rights are exercised, which decisions owners reserve |
| **Board Room** | Appointing and overseeing the CEO, approving strategy, overseeing risk |
| **Management Room** | Running the business and executing strategy day to day |
| **Family Room** | Family unity, education, shared values, next-generation development |

- The dinner table should not automatically become the boardroom.
- A decision should be made by the people with the **right responsibility**, not the most senior or loudest family member.
- When boundaries are unclear: **family status substitutes for competence, ownership is confused with management, important decisions are made in the wrong room**.
- Governance becomes more important as the family, ownership group and business grow in size and complexity.

### 6.1.2 The Board's Purpose

#### Key Concept: Board purpose

- Most jurisdictions require a board by law, but a legal board does **not automatically mean good governance**.
- The board sits **between owners and managers**.
- Its purpose is to **oversee the business, not to run it**; it guides and challenges management.
- Members' responsibility is to the **business, not personal relationships**; they must understand owners' interests and be **willing to challenge them** rather than just agree.

#### Litmus test for board-level decisions

- Ask: **"Is this decision significant enough that a bad choice could place the business at substantial risk?"** If yes, it belongs at board level.

| Board-level decision | Note |
|----------------------|------|
| **Hiring or firing the CEO** | Often considered the single most important decision |
| **Succession planning** | Management succession should be planned well ahead (family or non-family firm) |
| **Executive compensation** | Includes separating pay for management from returns to owners |
| **Strategy and material decisions** | Approving direction, acquisitions, significant resource access including loans |
| **Regulatory and legal compliance** | Ensuring statutory obligations are met |
| **Annual dividend** | Board sets it against the owner room's policy, the year's performance and business needs |

#### Four types of family business board

| Type | Legal authority | Advantage |
|------|-----------------|-----------|
| **Informal advisory board** | None | Easy to implement, very flexible, very limited liability |
| **Formal advisory board** | None | Structured advice; builds **institutional memory** |
| **Fiduciary board** | **Full and binding** | Empowered to make tough decisions; legal responsibility to all shareholders |
| **Hybrid (fiduciary + advisory)** | **Partial**: only members with fiduciary responsibility hold legal authority | Mixes the advantages of both |

- The appropriate structure depends on the firm's **needs, readiness and legal responsibilities**.
- A family firm may begin with an advisory board and move towards a fiduciary board as stakes and need for accountable oversight increase.

#### Three broad board functions

| Function | Nature |
|----------|--------|
| **Service** | Advice and counsel to owners and management, drawing on directors' prior experience |
| **Resource provision** | Legitimacy, networks, access to capital, talent and customers |
| **Control** | Monitoring performance and risk; the classic oversight function |

- Each of the three roles shapes and strengthens the effectiveness of the board.

### 6.1.3 The Board's Service Role

#### Definition: Board capital

- **Board capital** is the resource backing the role a director plays. Two types:

| Type | Components |
|------|------------|
| **Human capital** | Prior experience, functional expertise, industry knowledge, reputation, prior governance experience |
| **Relational (social) capital** | Ties with capital providers (banks, private equity), customers, talent pools, regulators, other firms |

- A board populated only with relatives tends to be rich in **either human or social capital alone**; this is a **common, fixable gap**.

#### How the service role works in practice

| Contribution | Description |
|--------------|-------------|
| **Strategic sounding board** | Pressure-tests strategic plans and expansion decisions **before** hard commitment of resources |
| **Succession mentoring** | Coaches next-generation leaders; adds credibility the family alone cannot supply |
| **Outside (detached) view** | Judges a new hire, partner or risk that the founder is too emotionally close to judge rationally |

- The service role is available even on an **informal advisory board** with no legal authority (typically a family firm's first governance step); formalising through a fiduciary board strengthens it.

#### Insider vs outside director

| Aspect | Inside / family director | Outside director |
|--------|--------------------------|------------------|
| **Firm-specific knowledge** | Deep, built over many years | Builds over time, starts much later |
| **Willingness to challenge the founder** | Constrained (e.g. challenging one's father) | Structurally freer to say harder truths |
| **Stake in preserving status quo** | Very high (career, identity, relationships) | Lower; own reputation is at stake if firm performs poorly |

#### Service-role self-check

- Does the director's **background match the firm's actual strategic questions**?
- Has a director **shifted the CEO's mind on something material** this year? (No purely "yes people".)
- Is at least one director **able and willing to mentor the next generation** and actively develop a rising family leader?
- Is the environment **safe to raise hard questions**, including a family member's own performance?
- Test of value: has the board's advice influenced important decisions, and can directors raise difficult issues openly?

### 6.1.4 The Board's Resource Provisioning Role

#### Definition: Resource dependence theory

- **Resource dependence theory:** organisations are **not self-sufficient**; survival depends on managing dependence on external resources, and the **board is a strategic tool** for this.

#### Resources a board can open up

| Resource | How |
|----------|-----|
| **Capital** | Director with lender or investor relationships eases access to debt or equity |
| **Talent** | Well-networked director helps recruit senior hires the family cannot reach |
| **Customers and partners** | Opens commercial doors, joint ventures, distribution deals |
| **Regulatory and reputational standing** | Credible outside name reassures regulators, lenders and the market |

#### Four channels through which a board provides resources

| Channel | Meaning |
|---------|---------|
| **Advice and counsel** | Strategic input drawing on resource-relevant expertise |
| **Legitimacy** | Outside director's reputation reassures lenders, customers, regulators, stakeholders |
| **Channels of communication** | Director's network surfaces partnerships and opportunities early |
| **Preferential access to resources** | Better financing terms, stronger hires, warmer introductions |

#### Legitimacy: when an outside name matters

- **Before a listing or capital raise:** independent directors signal readiness for public or institutional investor scrutiny.
- **Before a cross-border partnership:** an internationally credible name reassures a foreign counterparty.
- **Before regulatory scrutiny intensifies:** a respected name can influence how closely a regulator watches and reads the firm's actions.

#### Limit and design question

- **Limit:** borrowed legitimacy must be **backed by real governance**; otherwise it is a **reputational risk** for both the firm and the director.
- **Design question:** Where are the firm's real resource dependencies, and do directors' networks actually reach them, or merely overlap with what the family already knows?

### 6.1.5 The Board's Control Role

#### Two principal-agent problems

| Type | Conflict | Level in family firms | Board's job |
|------|----------|-----------------------|-------------|
| **Type 1** | Owners vs managers | Typically **lower**, especially when family members manage the firm | Monitor management on the owners' behalf |
| **Type 2** | **Controlling family vs minority shareholders** | Often **very high** (concentrated control, fewer checks) | Protect minority shareholders from extraction of profits by the controlling family |

#### Four functions of the control role

| Function | Detail |
|----------|--------|
| **Performance monitoring** | Evaluate financial results against history, peers and **what was planned** |
| **Related-party transactions oversight** | Transactions with firms owned by the family or relatives must be priced and approved at **arm's length** and by **non-conflicted directors** |
| **Compensation oversight** | Fairness and equity of pay; separate pay for the management role from returns to ownership |
| **Compliance and risk oversight** | Regulatory, financial, operational, reputational and succession risk |

- Two risks needing special attention in family firms: **tunneling** and **related-party transactions**.

#### Definition: Tunneling

- **Tunneling:** a controlling shareholder extracts **private value from the firm at the expense of minority shareholders**, very often through related-party transactions.
- **Example:** an item available at arm's length for 10 lakh rupees is bought from a brother's or brother-in-law's firm at 15 lakh; everyone's money, including minority shareholders', is transferred to the family.

#### Board's role on related-party transactions

| Step | Action |
|------|--------|
| 1. Define and disclose | Define who counts as related; maintain and **annually update a disclosure register** |
| 2. Approve independently | Only **independent directors or non-conflicted owners** approve; never the interested party |
| 3. Price at arm's length | Use **competitive bids** or **outside valuation**, not purely internal estimates |
| 4. Monitor continuously | Internal audit tracks related-party exposure and reports to the board, which evaluates it |

- Effective control must address **managerial accountability** and the risk that controlling owners disadvantage minority shareholders; clear disclosure and independent review matter most for related-party transactions.

### 6.1.6 How Big Should a Family Firm's Board Be?

#### Evidence on board size

- **Jensen's rule of thumb** (from research): effectiveness tends to **decline once a board grows past roughly 7 or 8 members**.
- Why bigger is not automatically safer: **individual accountability shrinks, coordination costs rise, and genuine debate gives way to polite, diplomatic consensus**; exactly what a controlling family least needs.

#### Family-specific pressure

- The trap: ensuring **every family branch gets a seat**, treating the board as a **representation body** rather than a decision-making body.
- A 15% or 20% representation body cannot do a 7-person board's job.
- Give branches voice through a **family council or owner council** (family-level governance) and **reserve board seats for competence and business demand**.

#### Practical guidelines

| Guideline | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Legal minimums** | In India, set by the **Companies Act** and **SEBI-related thresholds** |
| **Effective size** | **5 to 9 members** for most family firms |
| **New seat** | Add for a **specific, nameable gap** (expertise or oversight need), not entitlement |
| **Revisit at transitions** | Generational handoff, major acquisition, outside capital, significant decision or other transition |
| **Test effectiveness** | Can everybody speak candidly? Is the **board** deciding, or the **owner room**? |

- A board should not be too small to play its role, nor so large that the liabilities of largeness make it ineffective.

### 6.1.7 Board Capital and Diversity

#### Four types of board diversity

| Type | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Demographic** | Representation across demographic characteristics |
| **Functional / expertise** | Different functional skills |
| **Experiential** | Experience across different business situations |
| **Network** | Access to wider professional networks |

- A family board is drawn from a **small, related pool**; each diversity type fills specific gaps.

#### Evidence on diverse boards

- Diverse boards tend to **monitor more rigorously**.
- They bring **wider perspectives**, surfacing risks a homogeneous board would miss, and **reduce groupthink**.
- The link between board diversity and **market performance** is **weaker and context-dependent**; diversity does not guarantee better performance.
- Treat diversity as a **governance quality lever**, not a silver bullet; the strongest case is **monitoring, risk detection and perspectives**.

#### Why family firms struggle to diversify

| Barrier | Explanation |
|---------|-------------|
| **Socio-emotional cost** | An outside seat feels like loss of family identity and control, not a governance gain |
| **Succession customs** | Seats inherited along existing family career paths reproduce existing patterns |
| **Privacy and trust concerns** | Outsiders are seen as risky; uncertainty about what they will learn about the family firm |

#### Discipline to fix it

- **Separate** the question of **who deserves a seat** (family inheritance fairness) from **what the board needs to do its job** (competence, board capital, diversity).

### 6.1.8 Types of Board Members

#### Chairman and CEO duality

- The **chairman** leads the **board's process and culture**, not the company's operations.
- **CEO duality:** the same person is both **chairman and CEO** (founder or family member). The person running the company then runs the meeting meant to oversee them.
- It is the **single most common way a family firm board quietly loses its control function**.

#### Types of directors

| Type | Contribution | Risk / test |
|------|--------------|-------------|
| **Promoter directors** (founders and controlling shareholders) | Deep firm-specific knowledge, founding vision, personal commitment | A board of promoters alone has **no structural source of challenge**; fix by balancing with **independent and non-family professional directors** |
| **Family directors (non-promoters)** | Ownership interest, business knowledge, family context | Test: **"Would this person earn this seat if they were not family?"** If no, the seat is **family room standing borrowed into the boardroom** |
| **Executive directors** | Participate in running the business as well as serving on the board | Roles must be clearly understood so governance is not confused with management |
| **Non-executive directors** | Focus on board responsibilities; can strengthen oversight when their role is substantive | Roles must be clear so oversight is not confused with management |
| **Independent directors** | Expertise, challenge, credible control | See below |

#### Independent directors

- Statutory definitions vary by jurisdiction; in India the **Companies Act** specifies who is qualified (or disqualified) to be an independent director.

| Benefit | Explanation |
|---------|-------------|
| **Professionalising the boardroom** | Family members prepare and behave more formally in front of a credible outsider |
| **Managing family careers honestly** | Can raise hard truths about a family member's performance where emotions otherwise drive decisions |
| **Complementary expertise** | Fills gaps the family's own career paths never produce |
| **Lending credible control** | No related-party interest to protect (which legal definitions require), so the control role gains credibility |

| Characteristic of an effective independent director | Detail |
|-----------------------------------------------------|--------|
| **Willingness to disagree** | Not merely technical independence; no currying favour |
| **Relevant expertise** | Matched to the firm's **named gaps**, not a generic CV |
| **Real-time commitment** | Reads papers closely, visits operations, knows the business, engages in decisions |
| **Comfort inside a family system** | Neither **deferential** to family authority nor **dismissive** of it; respects the family's role without being overridden |

#### Symbolic vs substantive independence

- **Symbolic:** independent on paper only; **substantive:** independent in the room.
- If directors **check the chairman's face or look for behavioural cues before speaking**, independence has been lost whatever the paperwork says.

### 6.1.9 Why Family Firms Resist Independent Directors

#### Reasons for resistance

| Reason | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **Confidentiality** | Keep financial and compensation details within the family |
| **Loss of control** | Fear of outsiders' influence |
| **Loss of freedom** | Restrictions on informal decision-making |
| **Tenure limits** | Prefer a long-term, fully trusted relationship with one outsider |
| **Secrecy** | Keep family conflict and succession doubts private |

#### Effectiveness of independent directors

- **Work best** when appointment and reappointment are **genuinely independent of the controlling family**.
- **Work worst** under **pure symbolic compliance**: selected and retained at the family's pleasure, familiar to the family, and likely to toe the family line.
- **Effectiveness tracks the appointment process more than the director's individual credentials.**

#### Governance failures

- **Satyam, IL&FS** (and the **Tata-Mistry dispute**) are used to question independence and effective oversight.
- Shared lesson: **independence on the roster is not independence in practice**.
- **Structure, information and culture** decide whether independence is symbolic or substantive.
- When a family trust, family governance mechanism or ownership structure effectively controls board appointments, how independent can a director's vote be?
- Genuine independence requires: appointment process, access to information, willingness to question the controlling family, and freedom to exercise judgement.

### 6.1.10 Board Committee Structures

#### Typical committees

- **Audit committee**, **nomination and remuneration committee**, **stakeholder committee**, **risk management or strategy committees**, **IT committees**; some are mandatory, others vary across boards.

| Committee | Purpose |
|-----------|---------|
| **Audit Committee** | Financial reporting, internal controls, related-party transactions |
| **Nomination and Remuneration Committee** | Director appointments, succession, executive pay |
| **Stakeholder Relationship Committee** | Investor and shareholder grievances |
| **Risk Management Committee** | Enterprise risk oversight where applicable |
| **CSR Committee** (India) | Mandated in India |

#### Power vs symbolic committees

| Feature | Power committee | Symbolic committee |
|---------|-----------------|--------------------|
| **Authority** | Real delegated authority | Exists to satisfy a checklist |
| **Meetings** | Meet substantively | Ritualistic |
| **Effect** | Findings and recommendations significantly influence or change decisions | **Ratifies** decisions made elsewhere |

- Power committees are typically mandated ones (audit, nomination and remuneration; CSR in India), but mandated status does not guarantee performance.
- **One-year test:** has the committee **influenced or changed a real decision** recently, or only ratified one already made?
- Hard to answer from outside; a family member joining the board should keep asking it.

#### Management committees

- Used to **delegate without losing oversight**.
- Discipline: **written delegation of authority with clear limits**, clear reporting structures, and anything above the line **still goes to the board**.

#### Design principles

- Design committees **deliberately, not out of habit**; periodically review whether each still has a **real and current mandate**.
- Staff for **substantive roles**, with a chair who insists on **real agendas**.
- **Few well-run committees beat many hollow symbolic ones.**

### 6.1.11 Family Members on the Board

#### Family presence across ownership stages

- Ownership stages: **controlling owner, sibling partnership, cousin consortium, family federation**.
- Typical board composition varies by stage, so **governance requirements change with each stage**.
- As the family grows, giving every branch a board seat becomes increasingly difficult; a **family council** provides a separate channel for family representation so the board stays focused on governance and business competence.

#### Family presence across firm size and complexity

- **Small, simple structures:** an all-family board can genuinely work at any generation.
- Consider the number of businesses and geographies; ask whether **outside expertise** (human or social board capital the family cannot supply) is needed.
- Track growth of complexity; **pace board change with business requirements**.
- Do **not** populate seats from **generational pride** ("my father had a seat") or equal branch representation.

#### Interaction of board, top management and family council

- Three decision areas: **succession, strategy and the family constitution**.
- For each, clarify who **generates, advises, validates and approves**; the roles differ by decision, but **none of the three can work in isolation** and responsibilities should not be duplicated.

#### Generational self-check

| Question | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| **Does it match the complexity?** | Does composition reflect today's business complexity? |
| **Is representation answered?** | Is there a written answer for managing growing shareholder numbers, rather than every branch on the board? |
| **Is there a real council relationship?** | Does family voice reach governance through a proper channel, separating family/ownership governance from business governance? |

### 6.1.12 Key Governance Questions

#### Questions before a family member takes a board seat (answer honestly, preferably in writing)

| Area | Questions |
|------|-----------|
| **Readiness and competence** | Does the person want the **responsibilities** or the **title and standing**? Do they understand the accountability? |
| **Process and accountability** | **How is the person evaluated and by whom?** Use a written process applied as rigorously as to anyone else. Can they be **removed in practice**, or does family standing make non-renewal impossible? Are **boundaries respected**? (Board disagreements resurface as family grievances.) |
| **Compensation and exit** | Is pay **set and disclosed the same way** for family and non-family directors? Is there a **defined term with a real decision point**? Is there an **honourable way to step down**? |

- Being family may explain why someone is considered, but does **not** establish readiness; a seat needs preparation, competence, accountability and willingness to act in the interests of the business and its shareholders.

### 6.1.13 Board of Advisors

#### Definition: Advisory board

- A **board of advisors** is a **lighter-weight first step**: it brings **service and resource provision value without fiduciary exposure and with no binding authority ceded**.

#### How it works

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Cadence** | Regular meetings, commonly **quarterly**, frequent enough to build continuity |
| **Topics** | Clear scope: strategy, major decisions, succession progress, external market developments |
| **No binding vote** | Allows candid input; the owner is free to accept or decline |
| **Light charter** | Scope, meeting frequency, confidentiality, modest compensation |

- Many firms retain **both** an advisory board and a fiduciary board; often the same person serves on both.

#### Who serves

| Advisor type | Value |
|--------------|-------|
| **Retired operating executives** | Relevant industry experience; have run something comparable |
| **Functional specialists** | Domain expertise, e.g. finance, technology, international expansion, digital transformation |
| **Family business consultants** | Recognise patterns across family systems; show a problem is not unique and how others solved it |

#### Benefits, challenges and graduation

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **Benefit** | Low risk, real value; owner more comfortable than with a binding constraint |
| **Challenge** | Advice is non-binding and can be **quietly set aside** when inconvenient to the founder |
| **When to graduate to fiduciary or hybrid board** | When stakes and **outside capital** justify binding, accountable oversight: more geographies and businesses, multiple people involved, outsiders brought in, accountability to more people; it can no longer be run in the ownership circle alone and must run as a professional organisation |

## 6.2 Governance in the Family Circle

### 6.2.1 Why Formalise Family Governance?

#### Purpose of family governance

- No law requires family governance (unlike a board in the business circle).
- It provides a structure for managing **family relationships, expectations, education and continuity**; it **complements business governance rather than replacing it**.
- It lets the board focus on the business.

| Dimension | Firm (business) governance | Family governance |
|-----------|---------------------------|-------------------|
| **Structures** | Board, vision, strategic decisions, top management team | Family forum, family council, constitution, family office |
| **Focus** | Strategy, performance, operations | Communication, harmony, education of the next generation, transfer of legacy, family values and culture |
| **Shared goal** | **Longevity and continuity** across generations | **Longevity and continuity** across generations |

- The **interface**: clear communication between owners and the firm's members; keeping the family's **entrepreneurial ability and prosperity** across generations.

#### Cost of staying informal

- **Early stage, founder-led:** governance is the founder's decision; formal structure is not really needed.
- **Building governance mid-crisis:** emotions run high, trust deficit, structure missing, things can spiral.
- **Cost of deferral:** no clear **decision rights**, and conflicts easily trigger as the family grows.
- Best developed **before a crisis**; small first-generation families need not build a full system immediately.

### 6.2.2 Family Governance: Growth Stages and Breakdown Patterns

#### Governance by growth stage

| Stage | Complexity | Governance needed |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| **Generation 1: controlling owner** | One founder, single business, simple | Founder's judgment; informal conversations and a working board are enough |
| **Generation 2: sibling partnership** (or controlling owner with rising complexity) | Multiple functions, lines or companies | **First formal step**: regular family meetings, early **family council**, understanding recorded (ideally written) |
| **Generation 3: cousin consortium** | Multiple entities, geographies, products, cousins running lines | **Full-blown architecture**: family forum, family council, constitution, often a family office; no longer optional |

- Build governance **one level ahead**: founder stage plans for sibling stage; sibling stage plans for cousin stage.
- Governance should neither formalise everything immediately nor rely on informal practice indefinitely.

#### Four breakdown patterns

| Pattern | What it looks like | Why it recurs |
|---------|-------------------|---------------|
| **Loft** | Unclear rules and roles; decisions made at the dinner table; no boundary between family and firm | "We have managed so far"; ignores that what worked at one scale fails as complexity grows ("what has got you here will not take you there") |
| **Silo** | Each branch runs its own "kingdom" with its own rules, values and culture; little collaboration, nothing holds them together | No cross-connection; each branch thinks it is better off alone |
| **Missing room** | A room is absent, most often the **owner room**; unclear who decides, nobody "driving the ship"; owners do not meet or agree | Non-managing and managing owners do not meet; no natural forum; governance failure moves into the business |
| **Messy room** | A room has **lost its purpose** and adds no value; people come and go without understanding the purpose | Business and family change at different paces, so one room becomes messy |

- These patterns explain most family governance failures: what happens in the dining room eventually goes to the boardroom.
- **Diagnostic questions:** How do fundamental decisions get taken or avoided? Do branches collaborate or function separately?

### 6.2.3 The Family Forum and Council

#### Definition: Family forum

- A **family forum** is a **meeting or event, not a standing body**, for the **entire family**.
- **Inclusive:** all family members, typically including spouses; no election, competence bar or educational minimum.
- Decisions to be made explicitly: an **age limit** and whether **in-laws** participate.

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **Purpose** | Connection, bonding, relationship; much less business, more bonding (e.g. a family picnic or getaway) |
| **Content** | Business changes and leadership updates, suggestions for the council, education of the next generation, family history and legacy |
| **What good forums do** | Ensure **inclusivity**; leadership shares **updates directly with every member** (transparency); build **bonding across branches**, countering silos; create unity and identity that bridge distance between generations |
| **Why more important later** | **Cousin bonds do not form automatically** (not raised together); regular meetings build bonding |

#### Definition: Family council

- A **family council** is a **smaller, elected group** that does the work a full forum cannot.

| Feature | Family forum | Family council |
|---------|--------------|----------------|
| **Participants** | Everyone; no size limit | Small elected group, usually about **4, 5 or 7** (large councils are unwieldy) |
| **Election** | None | Elected by the forum |
| **Form** | Meeting or event | Ongoing governance body |
| **Eligibility** | All family members | Anyone the forum elects, including non-business members, non-owners or outsiders |
| **Main purpose** | Connection, education, updates, history, legacy | Leads continuing family governance work and coordinates family-level matters |

- Election by a forum representing multiple branches gives the council stronger weight.

#### Four mandates of the family council

| Mandate | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Next-generation development** | Educate as shareholders, future professionals and family members; development across **all three circles**, to safeguard business and legacy |
| **Communication** | Keep branches informed on family, business, management changes and future plans (can use the forum) |
| **Integration and legacy** | Document family history; pass on legacy |
| **Family constitution and succession** | Generate the constitution (needed particularly at cousin consortium stage) and **advise the board** on succession |

#### How the council runs

- **Intensive early on:** meets regularly, sets policies, establishes the **purpose** of family and business (carried across generations with only minor modification).
- **Steady state:** meetings become less frequent.
- **Complex stage (cousin consortium, multiple business units):** the council mirrors what a board does in a large or listed company, with **committees and a core team**.
- The forum and council should **not replace the board or owner council**; each has a distinct remit.

#### Examples

- **Dr. Reddy's Laboratories:** distinct family council rules that separate family aspirations from board-level governance decisions.
- **GMR Group:** multiple branches; a constitution plus a family council, and clear employment rules for how future generations join, enshrined in the constitution.

### 6.2.4 The Family Constitution

#### Definition: Family constitution

- A **family constitution** is a **written document of the family's values, rules, policies and shared principles**, agreed by the whole family.

#### How it is created

- Drafted by a **working group** nominated by the forum and overseen by the family council (or the council itself acts as the working group).
- Rules and policies are **discussed with all family members before drafting**, brought back to the forum, iterated; dissent is worked through to common ground and **consent by everyone** to the final draft.

#### Legal status

- **Not legally enforceable**: in a dispute between branches, no one can take it to court.
- It should not automatically be treated as legally equivalent to a shareholders' agreement.

#### Why it still has value

- The **process** is more valuable than the final print ("the journey is more important than the destination"); substantial iteration between council and forum, and the document can last a couple of generations.
- **Moral commitment:** every member has given it, and there is a **social cost to breaking it** (visible to the whole family); this links to socio-emotional costs in a family business.

#### Constitution vs shareholders' agreement

| Aspect | Family constitution | Shareholders' agreement |
|--------|---------------------|-------------------------|
| **Legal enforceability** | **Not** legally enforceable | **Legally enforceable**, especially if reflected in the company's articles of association |
| **Covers** | Ground rules, family-level policy, values, culture | **Ownership only**: transfer, voting rights, buying, selling, exit |
| **Basis of compliance** | Moral commitment and social cost | Contract law and company law; court can examine it |
| **Examples of clauses** | Employment, conduct, governance bodies | First right of refusal, tag-along rights, stake that can be sold |

### 6.2.5 What Goes into a Family Constitution

#### Scope

- Because it is not a legal document but a **social contract**, there are **no boundaries** on what it includes; it should reflect the family's circumstances rather than a **generic template**.
- Throws up questions branches must answer; reveals aspirations others did not know existed, so issues are **resolved earlier**; once agreed, it becomes binding on all in practice.

#### Typical contents

| Area | Contents |
|------|----------|
| **Values and purpose** | Family values, vision and mission; purpose for which the family and business exist |
| **Employment, titles, compensation, conduct** | See below |
| **Governance bodies** | Rules for the family forum, council, and board nomination |
| **Escalation** | Process when family council and board disagree: who convenes, how it is discussed, how it moves forward |
| **Ownership and liquidity** | Cap on money taken out of the company; permitted purposes (personal use, assets outside the company); awareness of the shareholders' agreement |
| **Family meetings and decision-making processes** | Frequency, convening, attendance |
| **Succession and ownership expectations** | Principles and procedures for succession |

#### Employment policy

- Codifies **entry requirements** (minimum education, outside work experience), the **selection method** and role (structured interview, entry test, starting level).
- **No one is parachuted into a role**; being family does not guarantee a job.

#### Compensation policy

- **Family scale vs market scale**, and the link between them. Matching or exceeding market scale means **taking money out of the business** that could be reinvested; family may gain more as owners by growing firm value than by dividends or salary.
- Also covers how increments are shared and how annual bonuses are decided; these increase family liquidity but are choices enshrined in the constitution.

#### Performance and conduct

- How each family member's **performance is rated**; consequences of over-performance (money or higher holding) and under-performance (lesser role or responsibility); whether family and non-family are **held to the same standard**.
- **Code of conduct** within the family and in the business: communication norms, conflict handling, rules on **employing spouses**.

#### Rules for governing bodies

| Body | Rules the constitution can set |
|------|-------------------------------|
| **Family council** | Size (e.g. maximum 7 or 8), eligibility, election rules, composition (multiple branches, gender balance, mixed ages) |
| **Family forum** | How often it meets, who convenes, who arranges, who attends, age limit, in-law participation |
| **Board** | Which family members can be nominated, how nominated, elected and selected, tenure, rotation, independent directors and their tenure, pay structure |

### 6.2.6 Building a Family Constitution

#### Process

- A **deliberative, iterative** process; the first draft is easy, but discussion starts when it reaches the forum.
- Like a country's constitution, it will need **amendments** over time.

| Step | Detail |
|------|--------|
| **1. Starting point** | What the family currently does, what it is satisfied with and what it is not |
| **2. Define the mandate and drafting group** | Mandate; members, size and qualifications; term limits and length of terms; meeting frequency; what it can decide, recommend or must escalate; funding and whether members are paid |
| **3. Involve family members** | Discuss with the whole family; iterate between the group and the forum |
| **4. Ratification** | Present to those with real authority and get agreement; not an afterthought |
| **5. Review and revision** | Set a review cycle; reviewed through the same deliberative process by whoever holds authority later |

- **Ratification:** if the people with real authority are not involved at every stage, they see it as **foisted on them** and it is bound to fail; even if the forum agrees, owners can block it.
- **Review:** **GMR** is the example of a built-in review cycle; as authority holders change, the constitution is revised to meet the requirements of the new owners, so it stands the test of time.
- A constitution is more likely to remain useful when members participate meaningfully, understand the reasoning behind provisions and agree on how it will be reviewed; the **process should create shared understanding**, not just produce a document.

### 6.2.7 The Family Office

#### Definition: Family office

- A **family office** is an institution that coordinates **financial, administrative and governance responsibilities** when family wealth has grown in size and complexity beyond what any one member can manage.

#### Three core functions

| Function | Detail |
|----------|--------|
| **Investment** | Professionally managing diversified family wealth: movable assets (financial wealth, possibly across borders), immovable/hard assets, and assets in between (e.g. a charter flight or yacht) |
| **Support** | Tax, legal, insurance, family philanthropy, administration and family tax services, centralised for the whole family |
| **Governance** | **Administering** the family council, constitution and family meetings (administering, **not deciding**) |

- It is a governance institution with a more financial ambit.

#### When a family needs one

- **Typical trigger:** wealth **outgrows the operating business**, e.g. after a **partial sale or IPO**, a large capital infusion, or members selling stakes to an external party.
- Before that, if wealth is managed by individuals or largely reinvested in the business, a family office may not be needed.

| Readiness signal | Detail |
|------------------|--------|
| **Diversified wealth** | Assets have grown well beyond the operating business |
| **Complexity** | More branches and generations; informal coordination of investments, insurance and tax becomes hard (e.g. cross-border branches) |
| **Structural complexity** | Multiple entities, cross-border holdings, trusts, philanthropy |
| **Recurring unanswered questions** | Administrative questions repeat and there is **no clear owner** of family office tasks |

#### What it is not

- An **administrative setup that supports the council and the board**; **not a decision-making authority**.
- Not simply an investment department, and **not a substitute for the board, family council or other governance bodies**.
- It carries out the decisions of the family and board and takes administrative overload from key decision-makers; structure should match the family's actual needs and resources.

### 6.2.8 Types of Family Office

#### Selection

- No single best model; depends on **stage of business, scale and amount of wealth served**.

#### Types

| Type | Description | Key risk / feature |
|------|-------------|--------------------|
| **Embedded** | Team inside the operating business, usually 1 or 2 members of the **CFO's team**; staff still part of the company | Thin lines between family finances and business finances; confusion and transfer of funds between family office and operating company |
| **Virtual** | **Outsourced** to a highly trusted provider with its own dedicated staff | Needs trust; completely separate staff |
| **Single-family office (SFO)** | Separate team serving **one family exclusively** | Entire cost borne by one family; can **customise** heavily, including for different branches and members |
| **Multi-family office (MFO)** | Separate office serving **multiple, usually unrelated, families** | Fixed costs shared, so each family pays a fraction; **standard templates**, less customisation |

### 6.2.9 Family Trusts

#### Definition: Trust

- A trust **separates legal ownership from beneficial ownership**: the trust (via trustees) holds legal title, while beneficiaries receive the benefits, according to the trust deed and applicable law.

#### Why families use trusts

- **Prevents fragmentation of assets:** shareholding splits each generation (e.g. 100% to 50% to 25% to ever smaller shares), making a controlling block impossible and weakening negotiation.
- Assets are transferred to a trust, keeping the **block together** as one large controlling block usable in negotiation.
- The trust **transfers economic benefit without transferring legal title**; returns flow to beneficiaries.
- The **trust deed** enshrines terms; trusts offer **ring-fencing of assets**, management of **capital gains exposure**, and possible **tax advantages**, depending on the legal and tax framework.

#### Parties

| Party | Role |
|-------|------|
| **Settlor** | Transfers the asset to the trust |
| **Trustees** | Manage the trust; hold ownership control; professionals run operations |
| **Beneficiaries** | Family members; beneficial (not legal) owners |

- Some beneficiaries can be trustees, but not all trustees should be owners: an **independent voice** is needed.

#### Four kinds of trust

| Kind | Feature | Trade-off |
|------|---------|-----------|
| **Discretionary** | Trustees decide distribution among a class of beneficiaries | Very flexible, **not very transparent** |
| **Specific (fixed)** | Each beneficiary's allocation fixed in the trust deed | Very transparent, **less adaptable** |
| **Revocable** | Settlor can reclaim assets | Protection benefits much weaker |
| **Irrevocable** | Transfer is permanent; title always with the trust; beneficiaries get only economic gains | Makes sense for **real asset protection** |

#### Example: Tata Trusts and Tata Sons

- **Tata Sons** is the holding company of the Tata group; **Tata Trusts hold about 66%** of Tata Sons and nominate directors to its board.
- Shareholding percentage alone may not ensure alignment: if the trustees disagree, the percentage means little.

### 6.2.10 The Family Bank, Investment Committee and Strategic Planning Committee

#### Overview

- Three specialised vehicles between the family and ownership governance, each solving a distinct practical problem.

#### Family bank

- An **internal capital allocation mechanism** for family members' funding needs (e.g. start a venture, step outside, run an adjacent venture while employed).
- Aims: **encourage next-generation entrepreneurship**; build **financial literacy** (P&L, company financials); keep **family capital rotating within the family system**, with returns coming back to the bank.

| Design element | Requirement |
|----------------|-------------|
| **Eligibility** | Clear written criteria and policy; decisions not arbitrary or relationship-based |
| **Formal underwriting** | Judge on **merit** as the market would, not as a favour to a family member |
| **Pricing** | Interest at **prevailing market rates**, not as a gift |
| **Loan vs gift** | A loan treated as a gift will **never be repaid** |
| **Independent oversight** | Prevents **favouritism**; absence leads to loss of family trust |

#### Family investment committee

- Moves investment decisions from whoever runs the business today (assumed to know where the family should invest) to **broader branch representation, professionalised execution and real transparency**.

#### Strategic planning committee

- Runs **parallel planning**: the business strategic plan must take account of the **family's values, goals and constraints**, and the business plan and family plan must stay **in sync**.

#### Common safeguards

- **Written policies, transparent criteria and appropriate oversight** reduce perceived favouritism and preserve trust; unclear responsibilities are a risk.

### 6.2.11 Family Holding Companies, Business Groups and Investment Arms

#### Holding company

- Needed when the enterprise has **diversified beyond one business** and the family needs a structure to govern the **whole portfolio**.
- A **holding company** owns stakes in multiple companies; a very important **governance vehicle**.

| Function | Detail |
|----------|--------|
| **Capital reallocation** | Moves capital between entities with different growth and risk profiles |
| **Risk and strategy management** | Manages differing strategic requirements of operating companies |
| **Wider governance structure** | Coordinates ownership, authority and accountability across the group |

- Its usefulness depends on how **authority, accountability and the holding-operating relationship** are defined.

#### Example: Tata Sons

- Holding company of the Tata group; group companies grow differently (e.g. Corus struggling, JLR stable, Tata Steel cyclical, Titan doing well, TCS the main funder of new enterprises, with risk questions as AI emerges).

#### Why holding companies are more common in emerging markets

| Reason | Explanation |
|--------|-------------|
| **Underdeveloped capital markets** | Holding company acts like a bank, moving capital between companies |
| **Lack of developed labour market** | Centralised shared support/talent across group companies |
| **Weaker contract enforcement** | Internal structure substitutes for weak institutions |

#### Family investment and venture arms

- Growing in India, e.g. **Premji Invest** (belongs to the Premji family): a PE and investment arm **completely separate from Wipro**, with its own management, team and decisions, which can even invest in companies that may compete with Wipro.

## 6.3 Governance in the Ownership Circle

### 6.3.1 Ownership Choices: The Four Types of Family Ownership

#### Key Concept

- Ownership is **not a single arrangement**; different structures create different expectations of control, participation, decision-making and continuity, and distinct governance requirements.
- Ownership should be a **deliberate long-term choice**, not merely an inheritance outcome.

#### Two dimensions

- **Ownership criteria:** exclusive vs inclusive.
- **Decision control:** unified vs shared.

| Type | Ownership criteria | Decision control | Example |
|------|--------------------|------------------|---------|
| **Sole Owner** | Exclusive | Unified | **House of Camus**: ownership and control concentrated in one successor per generation for over 150 years |
| **Partnership** | Exclusive | Shared | **COFRA / Brenninkmeijer family (C&A)**: shared but very exclusive; roughly **15-year apprenticeship** before admission as owner |
| **Concentrated** | Inclusive | Unified | **Vitamix**: inclusive ownership; decision control unified through collaborative leadership |
| **Distributed** | Inclusive | Shared | **Bacardi Limited**: about 7 generations; inclusive; stakes near 1% each; shared decision-making |

- Survey figures cited (describe that study only, not all family businesses): about **13% Sole Owner, 24% Partnership, 27% Concentrated, 36% Distributed**.
- Each family firm must choose the model that works best for its unique needs.
- The choice is **not permanent**: firms may move between types as business or family needs change.
- **Legal vs enacted type:** what documents say vs how ownership actually functions daily; the divergence can be significant, and **closing that gap is itself a governance act**.

### 6.3.2 The Ownership Alternatives Matrix

#### Ownership strategy

- Choosing an ownership type is not enough; owners need an **explicit strategy** for what they want from ownership (control, liquidity, continuity, participation).
- Framework: **De Massis and Rondi** (ownership alternatives matrix).

| Dimension | Elements |
|-----------|----------|
| **Ownership competencies** | Matching competence, timing competence, governance competence |
| **Ownership approach** | Custodian vs entrepreneur |
| **Ownership rights** | Voting vs non-voting |
| **Ownership activity** | Active vs passive owner |

| Choice | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| **Custodian vs entrepreneur** | Preserving what exists (exploitation) vs actively growing and reshaping it, creating new vehicles in the family galaxy (exploration) |
| **Voting vs non-voting** | Separates **economic ownership** (dividends, valuation) from **formal decision control**; voting shares carry both |
| **Passive vs active** | Merely holding a stake vs genuine engagement in ownership and governance of ownership |

#### Why an explicit strategy

- Owners must weigh **financial return against control, legacy and family employment**, over a chosen **time horizon**; balance financial and non-financial wealth as business and family complexity changes.
- Comparing alternatives makes **trade-offs explicit**; a single structure may not satisfy all branches equally (no universally correct answer).

#### Sequence

1. Agree the **ownership strategy** first (it changes with external and family realities).
2. Then choose the **ownership type and structure** to serve it.
- Not the other way round (do not fit strategy to an existing structure).

### 6.3.3 The Shareholders' Agreement

#### Definition: Shareholders' agreement

- The **legally enforceable backbone** of the ownership circle; it binds in law exactly what happens to the shares (the family constitution expresses values; the shareholders' agreement does the opposite).

#### Contents

| Area | Detail |
|------|--------|
| **Decision rules** | Majority, supermajority or unanimity thresholds (by branch or decision type) |
| **Transfer and exit mechanics** | What a shareholder can do with shares; can they go outside the family; process to exit |
| **Valuation methodology** | How shares are priced when exit or transfer is triggered; who values; external party; method |

#### Decision rules

| Rule | Used for |
|------|----------|
| **Simple majority** (e.g. one share, one vote) | Routine operational matters |
| **Supermajority** (often **75%**) | Consequential decisions: amending the agreement, acquisitions, creating new share classes with differential voting |
| **Unanimity** | Most fundamental matters: core purpose of the firm, nature of control |

- Share classes matter: some classes carry additional or weaker voting rights.

#### Transfer and exit provisions

| Provision | Meaning |
|-----------|---------|
| **Right of first refusal** | Existing shareholders get first offer; no sale to an outsider unless they refuse |
| **Put right** | Minority can force a buyback under specified conditions (like a put option, triggering exit or transfer) |
| **Drag-along** | Majority can compel minority to sell alongside them |
| **Tag-along** | Minority can join a majority sale on the same terms |

#### Enforceability trap (India)

- Case: **V.B. Rangaraj v. V.B. Gopalkrishnan**. A transfer restriction only in the shareholders' agreement and **not also in the articles of association** may be **unenforceable against the company**.
- **Lesson:** articles of association must reflect and align with the shareholders' agreement. Obtain current legal advice before relying on this point.

### 6.3.4 The Shareholders' or Owner Council

#### Definition: Owner council

- The **owner council** is the **owner room's working body** (Four-Room model): a forum for matters connected specifically to **ownership** (dividends, ownership strategy, future of investments).
- **Membership follows the ownership stake, not family membership**; this is why its decisions carry **legal weight** compared with the family council.

#### Who sits on it, by ownership type

| Ownership type | Owner council |
|----------------|---------------|
| **Sole owner** | No council needed; the owner decides directly |
| **Partnership** | Admitted partners (per shareholders' agreement and articles of association) constitute the council |
| **Concentrated** | Family branch or group holding matters, with unified decision control |
| **Distributed** | A subset of the many owners: **elected or rotating representatives** |

#### Owner council vs board

| Decision | Owner council | Board |
|----------|--------------|-------|
| **Major acquisition or sale** | **Approves** | May decide / recommend |
| **Selecting or removing board members** | **Decides** | Only recommends |
| **Hiring or firing the CEO** | **Approves** | **Decides** |

#### Owner council vs family council

- **Practical test:** *"Is this decision being made with owner council authority, or has a family council conversation quietly been treated as if it had that authority?"*
- Carryover from family council to owner council blurs the line between rooms and is **bad governance**.
- Family council: family relationships, education, values. Owner council: ownership interests and responsibilities. Each needs its own mandate and space.

### 6.3.5 The Shareholders' Assembly

#### Definition: Shareholders' assembly

- The **full body of owners**; in a listed company even a one-share holder is part of it.
- Must meet **at least once a year** (legal requirement).

#### Functions

- Approves accounts; appoints or removes **directors** and **auditors**, and discusses their terms; approves **dividends**; votes on **special resolutions** at the AGM.
- Minority shareholders have a right to vote (and to postal ballots), so dissent is recorded even if the majority passes the resolution.

#### Why it matters more in a family firm

- For family members with small shares who are not on the owner council, the assembly is the **one legally guaranteed annual moment** to be heard before all family members and owners.
- Skipping it costs a family firm more than a widely held firm.

#### Family vs widely held firm

| Dimension | Family ownership | Widely held ownership |
|-----------|-----------------|-----------------------|
| **Ownership structure** | Concentrated | Dispersed |
| **Time horizon** | Very long-term | Short-term (liquidity today; current quarter) |
| **Agency risk** | **Type 2**: controlling family vs minority shareholders | **Type 1**: owners vs managers |
| **Owner goals** | **Financial and socio-emotional wealth**; control, identity, legacy and values often weigh at par or above pure financial return | Predominantly **financial return** |

- Formal responsibilities (approving accounts, appointing directors) coexist with concerns about family relationships, control, continuity and minority shareholder interests.

### 6.3.6 Ownership Governance in the Entrepreneurial Family Galaxy

#### Definition: Family galaxy

- A **constellation** of operating firms, family offices, foundations, holding companies, trusts and investment vehicles around the family (at the centre); governance is no longer about one company.

#### Four dimensions

| Dimension | Meaning | Example |
|-----------|---------|---------|
| **Mass** | Capital or strategic weight of an entity | **TCS** carries far more weight in Tata Sons than a capital-consuming newer business |
| **Luminosity** | Visibility to outsiders | Listed TCS is highly visible; the privately held Dorabji Tata Trust (an owner of Tata Sons) is hardly known |
| **Distance from the centre** | **Control distance**: how closely the family controls the entity vs lets it run independently | Foundation runs at a distance |
| **Regeneration rate** | How quickly entities are created or retired as the family changes | PremjiInvest participates in many new entities |

#### Premji galaxy example

| Entity | Features |
|--------|----------|
| **Wipro** | Listed, highly luminous, high mass, very close to centre |
| **PremjiInvest** | Family office and investment vehicle; visible to some extent; somewhat distant; capital derived from Wipro; part of many new entities |
| **Foundation** | Philanthropic; fairly distant; vision and mission defined by the family, then runs independently; not luminous |
| **Controlling trust** | Controls the Wipro stake; very invisible |

#### Governance implication

- **Each node has different four-room boundaries**, so the galaxy needs a different four-room structure per entity; one common room would become a **messy room**.
- Effective governance requires clarity about **how entities relate, where decisions are made and how the family's long-term objectives are translated into practice**.

### 6.3.7 Governance Across All Three Circles

#### Three distinct jobs, three sets of structures

| Circle | Central concern | Structures |
|--------|-----------------|------------|
| **Business** | Direction, oversight, performance and risk | Board, committees, independent directors, board of advisors |
| **Family** | Relationships, communication, values and continuity | Forum, council, constitution, family office, trusts, legacy structures (richest set of vehicles) |
| **Ownership** | Ownership rights, strategy, transfer and exit | Ownership model, shareholders' agreement, owner council, shareholders' assembly, galaxy |

- Left informal, **each circle fails in its own way**.
- Across all circles: call out **at what stage** each mechanism is required, **document** it, ensure the **entire family agrees**, and **revisit at frequent intervals** as generations and markets change.
- Fundamental objective: ensure **succession and longevity** of the family and the firm.
- Governance gives clearer responsibilities, better decisions and greater continuity across generations.

## 6.4 Module Summary

### 6.4.1 Governance in the Three Circles: Consolidated View

#### Staging logic

| Stage | Structures |
|-------|-----------|
| **Controlling-owner family** | Minimal formal structures |
| **Sibling partnership** | Council and the beginnings of a constitution |
| **Cousin consortium** | Fuller set: forum, council, constitution, often a family office |

#### Vehicles for internal capital and legacy

| Vehicle | Core purpose |
|---------|--------------|
| **Family trust** | Separates legal ownership from beneficial interests |
| **Family bank / investment and planning bodies** | Coordinate internal capital allocation with family-business strategy |
| **Holding companies, business groups, investment arms** | Govern and fund a diversified family enterprise |
| **Next-generation development** | Builds future capability through exposure, education, external experience and merit-based progression |
| **Family archive** | Preserves family history and legacy across generations |

#### Recurring discipline

- **Name it, write it, agree it and revisit it.**
- Governance keeps decisions in the right room; unclear boundaries let family status replace competence and ownership be confused with management.

## Quick Revision Summary

### Key Definitions

- **Governance:** how decisions are made, who holds authority and where responsibilities belong.
- **Board:** sits between owners and managers; **oversees, does not run** the business; litmus test is whether a bad decision could place the business at substantial risk.
- **Board capital:** **human capital** (experience, expertise, reputation) plus **relational/social capital** (networks, access).
- **Resource dependence theory:** organisations are not self-sufficient; the board manages external dependence.
- **Tunneling:** controlling shareholder extracts private value at the expense of minority shareholders, often through related-party transactions.
- **CEO duality:** one person is both chairman and CEO; weakens the control role.
- **Symbolic vs substantive independence:** independent on paper vs independent in the room.
- **Power committee:** real delegated authority and influence on decisions; **symbolic committee:** ratifies decisions made elsewhere.
- **Family forum:** inclusive meeting/event; **family council:** small elected standing body.
- **Family constitution:** written, moral (not legally enforceable) agreement on values, rules and policies; value lies in the process.
- **Shareholders' agreement:** legally enforceable ownership contract (decision rules, transfer/exit, valuation).
- **Family office:** administrative support for investment, support and governance administration; **not** a decision-making authority.
- **Trust:** separates legal ownership (trustees) from beneficial ownership (beneficiaries); settlor transfers assets.
- **Owner council:** owner room's working body; membership follows ownership stake.
- **Shareholders' assembly:** full body of owners; meets at least annually; legally guaranteed moment for every owner to be heard.
- **Family galaxy:** constellation of entities around the family; dimensions mass, luminosity, distance from centre, regeneration rate.

### Important Relations

- Board-level decisions: CEO hire/fire, succession, executive pay, strategy and material decisions, compliance, annual dividend.
- Four board types: informal advisory (no authority), formal advisory (no authority), fiduciary (full), hybrid (partial).
- Board roles: **service + resource provision + control**.
- Board size: effectiveness declines beyond about 7 or 8 (Jensen); practical range **5 to 9**.
- Shareholding fragmentation across generations: $100\% \rightarrow 50\% \rightarrow 25\% \rightarrow \dots$, which a trust counters by holding the block together.
- Ownership types: Sole owner (exclusive, unified); Partnership (exclusive, shared); Concentrated (inclusive, unified); Distributed (inclusive, shared).
- Decision thresholds: simple majority (routine), supermajority (often 75%, consequential), unanimity (fundamental).
- Family governance by stage: Gen 1 founder judgment; Gen 2 regular meetings and early council; Gen 3 forum, council, constitution, family office.

### Must-Remember Points

- **Related-party transactions:** define and disclose, approve independently, price at arm's length, monitor continuously.
- **Four types of board diversity:** demographic, functional, experiential, network; strongest case is monitoring and risk detection, not guaranteed performance.
- **Four benefits of independent directors:** professionalise boardroom, manage family careers honestly, complementary expertise, credible control.
- **Independence effectiveness tracks the appointment process**, not just credentials (Satyam, IL&FS, Tata-Mistry).
- **Family director test:** would this person earn the seat if not family?
- **Do not give every branch a board seat**; use family or owner council for representation.
- **Advisory board:** low-risk first step, no binding vote; graduate to fiduciary when stakes and outside capital justify.
- **Four breakdown patterns:** loft, silo, missing room (often owner room), messy room.
- **Council mandates:** next-generation development, communication, integration and legacy, constitution and succession.
- **Constitution:** not legally enforceable; process, moral commitment and social cost give it force; ratification by authority holders is essential; review cycle (GMR).
- **Family office:** three functions (investment, support, governance administration); trigger is wealth outgrowing the operating business; four types (embedded, virtual, single-family, multi-family).
- **Four trust kinds:** discretionary, specific, revocable, irrevocable; Tata Trusts hold about 66% of Tata Sons.
- **Family bank safeguards:** written criteria, formal underwriting, market interest, independent oversight.
- **Holding companies** are more prevalent in emerging markets (weak capital, labour markets, contract enforcement).
- **Ownership strategy comes before ownership structure**; legal vs enacted ownership gap is a governance issue.
- **Enforceability:** transfer restrictions should be in both the shareholders' agreement and the articles of association.
- **Owner council vs board:** owner council approves acquisitions/sales and decides board membership; board decides CEO hire/fire with owner council approval.
- **Assembly matters more in family firms** because it is the guaranteed moment for minority family members to be heard; family firms face Type 2 agency and socio-emotional wealth considerations.
- **Core discipline:** name it, write it, agree it, revisit it.
