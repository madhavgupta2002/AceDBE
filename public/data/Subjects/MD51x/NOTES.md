***Behavioural Economics***
# Module 1: Heuristics, Biases and Risk Preference

## 1.0 Overview
Rationality forms the cornerstone of classical economics. In this classical framework, agents are defined as **Homo Economicus** (or "Econs"). 
- **Econs:** Maximize narrowly defined material payoffs, possess perfect foresight, are willful (stick to plans), and can perfectly evaluate complex alternatives.
- **Humans:** Boundedly rational, psychologically nuanced, and rely on empirical, realistic behaviours. They frequently deviate from the rational actor model, making analysis harder. 

## 1.1 Heuristics and Biases
**Cognitive Psychology** defines how information is represented and processed in the mind. 
- **Heuristics:** Mental shortcuts, informal algorithms, or "rules of thumb" (System 1 / reflexive thinking) used to reduce cognitive load and speed up decision-making. 
- **Biases:** Systematic, predictable errors or deviations from rational decisions caused by the reliance on heuristics. 

Four key judgment heuristics drive human decision-making:
1. Representativeness
2. Availability
3. Anchoring
4. Affect

### Biases resulting from representativeness
**Representativeness Heuristic:** Judging the probability of an event by its similarity to a prototype or mental model (using "looks like" or "seems like" as a proxy for statistics).

- **Conjunction Fallacy:** A psychological phenomenon where specific conditions (a joint occurrence of two events) are judged more probable than a single, general component. 
  - *Example:* The "Linda Problem". Judging Linda to be a "feminist bank teller" is seen as more likely than her just being a "bank teller", violating the basic logic of probability where a subset cannot be larger than the entire set.

### Insensitivity to base rates
Ignoring prior statistical probabilities (base rates) in favour of rich, detailed, but statistically less likely scenarios.
- *Example:* Judging an earthquake causing avalanches in a specific small state (Uttaranchal) as more likely than a general earthquake in the entire country of India, simply because the detailed scenario feels more representative.
- *Example:* Assuming a mathematically inclined person randomly sampled from a room of 70 lawyers and 30 engineers is an engineer at a 90% probability, completely ignoring the 70/30 base rate.

### Insensitivity to sample size
Ignoring the sample size when estimating the likelihood of an outcome. 
- Smaller samples have higher variance, meaning deviations from the population parameter are much more likely in small samples.
- *Example:* Believing a small hospital and a large hospital have the same probability of recording days with >60% boy births. Statistically, the smaller hospital is much more likely to see this extreme deviation. 

### Misconceptions of chance
The false belief that a short, random sequence will exhibit the same characteristics as a long-term random sequence.
- **Gambler’s Fallacy:** Believing a certain random outcome is "due" because it hasn't happened recently. 
  - *Example:* Expecting a 'Head' on a coin toss simply because the last 5 tosses were 'Tails'.
  - *Real-world implication:* Son preference in fertility decisions. Parents may continue having children (or aborting) based on the statistical misconception that a son is "due" after having daughters.

### Regression to mean
The statistical phenomenon where extreme outcomes are naturally followed by more moderate outcomes.
- People mistakenly assign **causal interpretations** to random events.
- *Example:* A flight instructor praises a great landing, and the next landing is worse. They punish a terrible landing, and the next landing is better. The instructor falsely concludes punishment works better than praise, failing to realize the pilot's performance was simply regressing to their average skill level.
- *Example:* The "Sophomore Jinx" or "Beginner's Luck". 

## 1.2 Types of Biases
Beyond representativeness, other heuristics produce significant judgment errors.

### Overconfidence
Often called the "mother of all biases," this occurs when a person's subjective confidence in their judgment is reliably greater than the objective accuracy of those judgments. It leads to stock market bubbles, unnecessary lawsuits, and strikes.
It manifests in three forms:
1. **Over precision:** Being too sure about estimates. Creating extremely narrow confidence intervals that fail to capture the true value. 
   - *Correction:* "Wisdom of crowds" (asking for multiple estimates) or explicitly asking decision-makers to consider the probability of failure.
2. **Overestimation:** Believing one's performance or ability is greater than it actually is. Leads to the **planning fallacy** (underestimating time needed for a project) and **optimism bias** (believing the future will be excessively positive).
3. **Over placement:** The "better-than-average" effect. The tendency to believe one is better than others. 
   - *Example:* 93% of US drivers report being more skillful than the median driver. Leads to bad M&A decisions by CEOs.

### Exponential growth bias
The pervasive tendency to linearize exponential functions when assessing them intuitively. It leads to severe underestimation of future values given a present value.
- *Examples:* Boiling milk spilling over, exponential traffic growth outstripping infrastructure, compounding interest rates.
- *Policy Implication:* During COVID-19, people with high Exponential Growth Bias (EGB) underestimated future case counts. This lowered their risk perception, leading to lower compliance with WHO safety norms (masks, social distancing). 
- *Correction:* Step-by-step forecasting and visual feedback graphs can help mitigate EGB.

### Other biases
- **Availability Bias:** Judging probability based on the ease with which instances can be brought to mind. Driven by **salience** (vividness, personal experience, recent events).
  - *Example:* Overestimating deaths by war/starvation (highly reported) vs. cancer/respiratory diseases. Managers giving too much weight to a recent mistake during an annual performance review.
- **Anchoring Bias:** Making estimates by starting from an initial value (anchor) and adjusting insufficiently to reach the final answer. 
  - *Example:* A high pre-MBA salary anchors future salary negotiations. Suggesting a high initial price increases a consumer's willingness to pay.
- **Affect Heuristic:** A quick "good" or "bad" emotional gut response to a stimulus that drives judgment before higher reasoning kicks in. 
  - *Example:* Tobacco ads using positive affect (Marlboro Man) vs. policymakers using negative affect (gory images on cigarette packs).
- **Confirmation Bias:** The tendency to search for, interpret, and favor information that confirms one's pre-existing beliefs. Mental cost of processing a contrarian viewpoint is higher.
- **Hindsight Bias:** The "I knew it all along" effect. Perceiving events as more predictable after they have occurred. Eliminates the ability to objectively evaluate decisions and learn from the past.
- **Curse of Knowledge:** The inability to "unknow" available information when trying to imagine how much an uninformed person knows. 

## 1.3 Risk Preference of Humans

### Expected Value vs. Expected Utility
- **Expected Value $E(V)$:** The average value of outcomes, weighted by probabilities.
- **St. Petersburg Paradox:** A coin-flip game with an infinite expected value, yet humans are only willing to pay a small amount to play. This proves humans care about **Expected Utility $E(U)$**, not just monetary value.
- **Risk Aversion:** Most people prefer a certain income over a gamble with the same expected value. 
- **Risk Premium:** The maximum amount of money an individual is willing to give up to avoid risk (the foundation of the insurance industry).

### Loss Aversion: changes vs levels
Classical economics assumes satisfaction depends on absolute **levels** of wealth. Behavioural economics proves that fundamental senses of pain and pleasure depend on **changes** relative to a reference point (e.g., status quo, expectations, past outcomes). 

### Loss aversion: gains vs losses
Losses matter more than gains. The psychological pain of losing an amount is significantly greater than the pleasure of gaining the same amount. 
- **Loss Aversion Parameter ($\lambda$):** Estimated to be approximately 2. Losses hurt twice as much as equivalent gains.
- **Endowment Effect:** Valuing an object more simply because one owns it. The Willingness to Accept (WTA) for sellers is much higher than the Willingness to Pay (WTP) for buyers. For the seller, giving up the item operates in the painful *loss domain*.

### Prospect Theory
Developed by Kahneman and Tversky, Prospect Theory replaces Expected Utility Theory as a model for human decision-making under risk. 
**Three Key Principles:**
1. Changes matter more than levels (Reference Dependence).
2. Losses matter more than gains (Loss Aversion).
3. People are **risk-averse in the gain domain** (concave value function) and **risk-seeking in the loss domain** (convex value function).

*Preference Reversal:* Because of the curvature of the value function, humans will choose a sure gain over a gamble, but will choose a gamble to avoid a sure loss, even if the expected values are identical.

### Probability weighting function
Humans do not treat probabilities linearly (violating the expectation principle). 
- **Possibility Effect:** Highly unlikely outcomes (low probabilities) are **overweighted** (e.g., a 0% to 5% jump creates a disproportionate hope of winning). Explains why people buy lottery tickets.
- **Certainty Effect:** Almost certain outcomes (high probabilities) are **underweighted** relative to actual certainty (e.g., a 95% to 100% jump). 
- The resulting curve is S-shaped, intersecting the objective 45-degree line only once.

## 1.4 Mental Accounting
Coined by Richard Thaler, **Mental Accounting** is the process by which people organize, evaluate, and keep track of financial activities by assigning funds to exogenously created subjective categories.
- This violates the economic principle of **fungibility** (the idea that money is interchangeable and has no labels).
- *Implications:* People will simultaneously borrow at high interest rates (credit cards) while saving at low interest rates, simply to avoid "mixing" accounts.

### Bundling gains and losses
Because of the diminishing sensitivity of the value function, how managers "bundle" outcomes changes total human satisfaction.
| Strategy | Action | Formula logic | Reason |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Segregate Gains** | Separate multiple wins | $v(25) + v(50) > v(75)$ | Resets the reference point to zero, maximizing utility from each gain. (e.g., Give multiple smaller bonuses). |
| **Integrate Losses** | Combine multiple losses | $v(-100) + v(-100) < v(-200)$ | One large loss hurts less than the continuous pain of multiple small losses. |
| **Silver Lining Principle** | Segregate a large loss and a small gain | - | Provides a small sliver of hope amidst a large loss. |

### Acquisition and transaction utility
Total utility for a human consists of two parts:
1. **Acquisition Utility:** The conventional economic utility. Value of the good minus the actual price paid.
2. **Transaction Utility:** The psychological value derived from the perceived quality of the deal. Computed as **Reference Price minus Price Paid**. 
- *Implications:* A high Maximum Retail Price (MRP) creates a high reference point. Discounts, sales, and coupons artificially generate high Transaction Utility, sometimes prompting people to buy useless items just for the thrill of the "deal."

### Sunk Costs
A subset of mental accounting. Econs ignore sunk costs. Humans factor in unrecoverable past investments because closing a mental account with a negative balance triggers the psychological pain of a loss. 
- *Examples:* Enduring physical pain to play tennis because the membership is paid; the **payment depreciation effect** where gym attendance spikes immediately after fee payment and wanes over time.

  - *Bias:* Systematic deviation from rational decision-making.
  - *Loss Aversion:* The principle that losses cause greater psychological pain than equivalent gains ($\lambda \approx 2$).
  - *Mental Accounting:* Subjectively organizing money into separate, non-fungible categories.
  - *Endowment Effect:* Higher valuation of an asset simply because one owns it (WTA > WTP).
- **Important Formulas & Concepts:**

  - Availability relies on memory ease, driven by salience and vividness.
  - Exponential Growth Bias leads to underestimation of compounding impacts (debt, disease spread).
  - People overweight small probabilities (Possibility effect) and underweight high probabilities (Certainty effect). 
  - Sunk costs drive human behavior because accepting them means permanently closing a mental account at a loss.

# Module 2: Impatience, Self-control and Strategic Thinking

## 2.0 Overview
Economic decisions frequently involve a trade-off between the present and the future. Humans often exhibit behaviors that deviate from the standard rational model, particularly regarding time preference and self-control. This module examines how individuals discount the future, the psychological struggle between immediate gratification and long-term planning, and the mechanisms used to overcome self-control issues. It also contrasts the strategic thinking of fully rational "Econs" (using standard Game Theory) with the boundedly rational strategic thinking of "Humans" (using Level-k and Cognitive Hierarchy models), and explores the profound economic impact of social preferences and fairness.

## 2.1 Impatience and Self Control

### Discounting the future
Intertemporal choice involves the trade-off between consumption today and consumption tomorrow (e.g., savings, studying, exercising). People generally prefer to smooth their consumption over time rather than consuming everything at once. 
- **The Marshmallow Experiment (Walter Mischel):** Tested children's patience by offering one marshmallow immediately or two after a 10-minute wait. The amount of time a child waited served as a valid predictor of future life outcomes (SAT scores, income, health).
- **Time Preference:** The degree to which an individual prefers current benefits over future benefits. 
- **Discount Factor ($\delta$):** The rate at which the future is discounted. It typically lies between 0 and 1 ($0 < \delta < 1$). 
  - **High $\delta$ ($\approx 1$):** Indicates a patient person who values future rewards almost as much as present ones (e.g., saving for the future, regular exercise).
  - **Low $\delta$ ($\approx 0$):** Indicates an impatient person who heavily discounts the future (e.g., substance abuse, impulsive spending).

**Exponential Discounting (The "Econ" Model):**
Developed by Paul Samuelson, this is the standard classical approach to intertemporal choice. 
- **Formula:** $U_0 = u_0 + \delta u_1 + \delta^2 u_2 + \delta^3 u_3 + \dots = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty} \delta^i u_i$
- **Dynamic Time Consistency:** The hallmark of exponential discounting. Preferences are dynamically consistent if choices between two options do not change simply due to the passage of time. The "present self" and "future selves" agree on the optimal plan.

### Anomalies
Real human behaviour routinely violates dynamic time consistency. 
- **Dynamic Time Inconsistency:** The optimal plan for a human changes simply because of the passage of time. 
- Humans make optimal plans for the future (e.g., "I will start dieting next week") but fail to stick to them when the future arrives because the present self seeks instant gratification.

### Present-biased discounting
Also known as **hyperbolic discounting** or **beta-delta ($\beta-\delta$) discounting**. 
- Discount rates are much higher in the short run than in the long run. Humans are very patient regarding long-run trade-offs but extremely impatient regarding immediate trade-offs.
- **Formula:** $U_0 = u_0 + \beta (\delta u_1 + \delta^2 u_2 + \delta^3 u_3 + \dots)$
  - $\beta$: Represents present-bias (short-run discount factor). Typically, $\beta$ is a fraction (e.g., $\frac{1}{2}$).
  - $\delta$: Represents the standard long-run discount factor (often close to 1).

**Preference Reversals:**
Present-bias explains why preferences reverse as time passes, depending on the nature of the good:

| Type of Good | Characteristics | Human Behaviour | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Vices** | **Immediate benefit**, Delayed cost | **Pre-poned** consumption | Junk food, smoking. People plan to quit tomorrow, but consume today. |
| **Virtues** | Immediate cost, **Delayed benefit** | **Postponed** consumption | Exercising, studying. People plan to start tomorrow, but avoid it today. |

### Naifs, partial naifs and sophisticates
Individuals differ in their awareness of their own self-control problems. Let $\beta$ be the true present-bias parameter, and $\hat{\beta}$ be the person's *perceived* future present-bias.

| Type | Definition | Parameter | Behavioural Outcome |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Naifs** | Believe their future self will perfectly follow their current plans. They do not realize they have a self-control problem. | $\hat{\beta} = 1$ | Repeatedly make plans they fail to keep (e.g., buying a gym membership and never going). |
| **Sophisticates** | Fully aware they have a present-bias and self-control problem. | $\hat{\beta} = \beta$ | Pre-empt mistakes by limiting future choices. They solve a dynamic game against their future self using **backward induction**. |
| **Partial Naifs** | Somewhere in between; they underestimate the severity of their self-control problem. | $\beta < \hat{\beta} < 1$ | Partially anticipate failures but still overestimate future willpower. |

### Commitment contracts
Sophisticates use commitment strategies to manage their self-control problems by removing cues or limiting future choices (akin to Ulysses tying himself to the mast to resist the Sirens).
- **Commitment Devices:**
  - *Shreddy / Snooze and Luz:* Alarm clocks that shred money or donate to charity if you don't wake up.
  - *Stickk.com:* A platform where users set a goal and face financial penalties (donations to anti-charities) if they fail.
  - *CARES Program:* A savings account for smokers where funds are forfeited if they fail a nicotine test after six months. 
- **Dominated Contracts:** A contract that is objectively worse under standard economic theory but is voluntarily chosen by humans for self-discipline.
  - *Example (Michael Kremer's factory study):* Workers chose a contract paying $w/2$ per unit if they failed to hit a target $T$, rather than a flat $w$ per unit. This self-imposed penalty increased production effort by $2.3\%$ and equivalent earnings by $7\%$.
- **Macroeconomic Impact:** Patience ($\delta$) is positively correlated with the Wealth of Nations. Higher national patience correlates with higher log GDP per capita, greater net adjusted savings, and more years of schooling globally.

## 2.2 Strategic Thinking among Econs
### Strategic thinking among econs
Standard Game Theory models the strategic interaction of fully rational "Econs".
- **Core Components:**
  1. **Players:** Decision-makers who affect payoffs.
  2. **Strategies:** A complete plan of action for every possible contingency.
  3. **Payoffs:** The utility/well-being resulting from the chosen actions.
- **Key Assumptions:** 
  - **Rationality:** Players maximize their welfare and form correct beliefs.
  - **Common Knowledge:** Every player is rational, knows others are rational, knows that others know they are rational, ad infinitum.

**Game Classifications & Equilibria:**
- **Simultaneous Games:** Players move at the same time without knowing the other's choice (e.g., Prisoner's Dilemma).
  - *Dominant Strategy:* A strategy that yields a higher payoff regardless of what the other player does.
  - *Nash Equilibrium (John Nash):* A state where no player can improve their payoff by unilaterally changing their strategy, given the other players' strategies.
- **Sequential Games (Extensive Form):** Players move in turns, represented by decision trees with nodes.
  - *Subgame Perfect Nash Equilibrium (SPNE):* Found using **backward induction**. It ensures the strategy constitutes a Nash Equilibrium in every subgame.

## 2.3 Strategic Thinking among Humans
### Strategic thinking among humans
Humans rarely perform infinite strategic reasoning. They exhibit bounded rationality.
- **Level-K Thinking:** Models human behaviour based on finite steps of reasoning.
  - **Level 0:** Chooses randomly without strategic thought.
  - **Level 1:** Assumes all others are Level 0, and best-responds to that assumption.
  - **Level 2:** Assumes all others are Level 1, and best-responds to that.
  - **Level $\infty$:** The fully rational "Econ" who plays the pure Nash Equilibrium.
- **The Beauty Contest Game (Keynes):** Guess a number between 0 and 100. The winner is the person closest to $\frac{2}{3}$ of the average guess.
  - The dominant strategy/Nash Equilibrium is **0** (achieved by infinite iteration).
  - Empirical results show mass clusters around **33** (Level 1 thinkers assuming average is 50) and **22** (Level 2 thinkers).
- **The Centipede Game:** A sequential game testing backward induction. 
  - *SPNE prediction:* Stop at the very first node.
  - *Human behaviour:* Players frequently continue cooperating deep into the game. Students play differently against other students compared to playing against Chess Grandmasters. Humans adjust their strategy based on the perceived rationality of their opponent.

### Cognitive hierarchy model
A generalization of Level-k thinking by Camerer, Ho, and Chong.
- Assumes that a Type $k$ player believes all other players are distributed across lower types (Type $0$ to Type $k-1$).
- The distribution of player types follows a **Poisson distribution** parameterized by $\tau$ (tau).
  - As $\tau$ increases, higher-order (more sophisticated) strategic thinking emerges in the population.
- **Application (ISP Market in 1997):** Goldfarb and Yang estimated $\tau$ for Internet Service Providers.
  - Firms in highly educated, urban, competitive markets had higher $\tau$ values.
  - Higher $\tau$ firmly correlated with higher profitability and higher survival rates in the market.

## 2.4 Social Preferences
### The idea of fairness
Unlike Econs, Humans care deeply about social preferences, inequity aversion, and fairness.
- **Monkey Fairness Experiment (Frans de Waal):** Capuchin monkeys reject a perfectly good reward (cucumber) if they see another monkey receiving a better reward (grapes) for the exact same task.
- **Human Fairness Perceptions:**
  - *Endowment Effect & Status Quo:* Deviations from the established norm or status quo are viewed as unfair. 
  - *Framing:* Removing a discount is perceived as acceptable, whereas adding a surcharge of the exact same monetary value is viewed as highly unfair.
  - *Price Gouging:* Standard economics dictates prices rise with excess demand. However, raising the price of essential goods during a crisis (e.g., snow shovels after a blizzard, bottled water after a cyclone) is heavily punished by consumers as "unfair" predatory behaviour.
- **Nominal Wage Rigidity:** 
  - Wages do not fall easily during economic recessions. Cutting nominal wages makes workers angry, severely reducing productivity. Firms prefer to lay off a few workers while maintaining the *status quo* nominal wage for the rest.
  - Humans focus on *nominal* wages rather than *real* wages (adjusted for inflation) when evaluating fairness.
- **Caselets in Pricing Strategy:**
  - *First National Bank of Chicago:* Attempted to charge a $\$3$ fee for using human tellers to encourage ATM use. It caused massive backlash. Incentivizing ATM use (bonus) would have been perceived fairly, while disincentivizing teller use (penalty) was perceived as greedy.
  - *Coca-Cola:* Attempted to use weather-responsive vending machines (higher prices on hot days). The CEO was forced to resign due to consumer outrage over perceived unfairness.
  - *Uber Surge Pricing:* Often viewed negatively as price gouging during emergencies, requiring careful public relations to explain the supply-demand mechanism.

## 2.5 Summary
Module 2 establishes that human decisions across time and strategy differ drastically from classical models. Due to present-bias, humans act inconsistently, requiring commitment devices to maintain self-control. In strategic settings, humans apply limited steps of reasoning (Level-k/Cognitive Hierarchy) rather than infinite backward induction. Finally, unlike purely self-interested Econs, human economic interactions are heavily constrained by strong social preferences regarding fairness and equity.

# Module 3: Nudges and Public Policy

## 3.0 Overview
This module explores behavioural public policy, focusing on discrimination, gender economics, and choice architecture. It examines why equal individuals receive unequal outcomes in the labour market and how psychological traits drive these gaps. Finally, it introduces nudges—subtle changes in decision environments—as powerful tools for public policy to improve welfare without restricting freedom of choice.

## 3.1 Discrimination

### 3.1.1 Types of discrimination
Discrimination occurs when members of a minority group are treated less favourably than members of a majority group with identical characteristics.

#### Taste-based discrimination
Proposed by Gary Becker, this implies that individuals (e.g., employers) have a strict preference or "distaste" for interacting with certain groups.
- **Psychic Cost ($d$):** The psychological disutility an employer feels when hiring from a discriminated group.
- **Wage Impact:** The effective cost of hiring a minority worker becomes $w^* (1 + d)$, where $w^*$ is the competitive wage. Because the perceived cost is higher, the employer hires fewer minority workers.

#### Statistical discrimination
Proposed by Arrow and Phelps, this arises from incomplete information rather than prejudice. 
- **Mechanism:** In the absence of perfect information about an individual's true productivity or traits, decision-makers use the **group average** as a proxy.
- **Example:** If a specific group statistically has lower average education levels, an employer may assume an individual from that group is less educated and deny them employment.

### 3.1.2 Evidence of discrimination
#### Empirical Studies
- **Blind Auditions (Goldin & Rouse):** Concealing the gender of violin players during orchestra auditions increased the hiring of women from 10% to 35%. 
- **Stop and Search (Knowles et al.):** Data showed Maryland police searched Black men more often. This could be taste-based (prejudice) or statistical (inferring higher average criminality).
- **Correspondence Studies (Bertrand & Mullainathan):** Researchers sent identical CVs varying only by name (White-sounding vs. Black-sounding). White-sounding names received a 9.65% callback rate, compared to 6.45% for Black-sounding names.

#### Post hoc justification
When confronted with identical observable data (like identical CVs), decision-makers often use unobservable traits to justify biased decisions after the fact. For instance, an employer might claim a minority candidate lacked "soft skills" or "self-confidence" to rationalize a discriminatory hiring choice.

### 3.1.3 Consequences of discrimination
#### Stereotype Threat
The risk of underperformance by a minority group due to the anxiety of confirming a negative stereotype.
- **Maze Solving (Hoff & Pandey):** Dalit and Upper Caste students performed equally well in a maze task when castes were kept secret. When castes were publicly revealed, Dalit students' performance dropped by 20% due to the anticipation of being discriminated against.
- **Math Performance (Steele & Aronson):** Women performed worse on a math test when told it measured mathematical ability (activating the stereotype) but performed equally to men when told the test showed no gender differences.

#### Self-fulfilling prophecies
False beliefs that lead to their own fulfillment.
- **Mechanism:** If employers believe a minority worker is less productive, they invest less in their training. Consequently, the worker's skills remain low, fulfilling the initial false belief.
- **Pygmalion Effect (Rosenthal & Jacobson):** Teachers were falsely told a random 20% of students were "gifted." Due to the teachers' increased attention and encouragement, those randomly selected students gained significantly more IQ points than their peers.

### 3.1.4 Combatting discrimination
#### Policy Interventions
- **Diversity in Leadership / Quotas:** Mandating representation forces the majority group to evaluate the performance of minority members. However, women on evaluation committees do not always favour other women; they may judge them harshly to avoid appearing biased.
- **Minority Role Models:** Exposure to successful minority leaders shatters stereotypes, increases self-belief, and raises the effort levels of minority individuals.
- **Intergroup Contact Theory (Allport):** Interpersonal contact reduces prejudice effectively if four conditions are met:
  1. Equal status between groups.
  2. A common goal.
  3. Intergroup cooperation.
  4. Support of authorities, law, or custom.

## 3.2 Economics of Gender

### 3.2.1 Gender gaps
Labour market outcomes differ significantly by gender, even after adjusting for instrumental factors like education and experience.
- **Wage Gap:** A persistent, unexplained wage gap remains (e.g., women earning ~9% less than men with identical qualifications).
- **Representation Gap:** The "Glass Ceiling." Women are severely underrepresented in high-ranking corporate positions (e.g., comprising only ~5% of Fortune 500 CEOs).

### 3.2.2 Gender differences
#### Competitive environments
- **Performance:** In mixed-gender tournament payment schemes (winner-takes-all), the gender gap increases substantially compared to piece-rate schemes. However, in single-sex tournaments (women competing only against women), women's performance improves significantly. Women's underperformance in mixed tournaments is driven by a lack of belief in winning against men, not an inability to compete.
- **Competition Entry (Niederle & Vesterlund):** When given the choice between piece-rate and tournament pay, men overwhelmingly choose the tournament, while women shy away. Even when controlling for actual performance and self-confidence, women exhibit a pure preference against competing with men.
- **Low Promotability Tasks (Babcock et al.):** Tasks that benefit the organization but do not lead to career advancement (e.g., committee work, organizing office events). Women are 15 percentage points more likely to volunteer for these tasks than men. In single-sex groups, this gap vanishes. The gap is driven by the mutual belief that men will not volunteer, forcing women to step up.

### 3.2.3 Policies to reduce gender gaps
#### Incentive Structuring
- **Monetary Incentives:** Cannot be used for non-promotable tasks, as these tasks do not generate direct revenue.
- **Social Recognition:** Using non-monetary rewards to alter behaviour.
  - *Positive Recognition:* Publicly praising volunteers significantly reduces the gender gap by incentivizing men to compete for the social reward.
  - *Negative Recognition:* Publicly shaming non-volunteers increases total volunteering but maintains the existing gender gap.

## 3.3 Nudges: What, Why and How

### 3.3.1 Nudges
#### Definition
A **nudge** is any aspect of the choice architecture that alters people's behaviour in a predictable way without forbidding any options or significantly changing their economic incentives.
- **Libertarian Paternalism:** The guiding philosophy behind nudges. It is *libertarian* because individuals remain free to choose any option, and *paternalistic* because choice architects design the environment to improve the end-user's welfare.

### 3.3.2 Designing a choice architecture
#### Problem Suitability
Nudges are highly effective for specific types of decisions:
- **Self-Control Problems:** Managing the trade-off between *vices* (immediate benefit, delayed cost) and *virtues* (immediate cost, delayed benefit).
- **Rare and Difficult Problems:** Infrequent, high-stakes decisions (e.g., buying a house, choosing a mutual fund) where individuals lack practice.
- **Lack of Feedback:** Situations where the consequences of a choice are not immediately visible (e.g., unhealthy eating).
- **Mapping Choices to Welfare:** When options are complex, nudges help translate choices into understandable outcomes (e.g., using "megapixels" to communicate camera clarity).

### 3.3.3 Choice architecture
#### Key Elements
| Concept | Description | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Visual Cues vs Functionality** | The design must intuitively match the required action to avoid cognitive conflict. | A door meant to be pushed should not have pull handles. |
| **Defaults** | The pre-set course of action if the user does nothing. Humans take the path of least resistance. | Automatic enrollment in retirement savings plans or automatic subscription renewals. |
| **Active Choice** | Forcing the user to explicitly choose an option before proceeding. | Forcing a "Yes/No" selection for organ donation during license renewal. |
| **Post-Completion Errors** | Forgetting a secondary task once the primary goal is achieved. | An ATM forcing the user to take their card back *before* dispensing cash. |

### 3.3.4 Half a dozen nudges
#### Real-world Applications
1. **Save More Tomorrow:** Employees pre-commit to allocating a portion of future salary raises toward retirement, bypassing immediate loss aversion.
2. **Give More Tomorrow:** Asking donors to commit to a charitable donation two months in the future rather than today increases participation.
3. **Smart Lunchrooms:** Placing healthy foods (fruits) at eye level and junk food (fries) at the end of the line increases healthy consumption without banning options.
4. **Schiphol Urinals:** Etching a small image of a housefly near the drain of a urinal gives men a target, vastly improving aim and reducing cleaning costs.
5. **Vitality Bucks:** Health insurance programs rewarding daily gym attendance or blood pressure tests with points redeemable for movie tickets or flights.
6. **Dollar a Day Program:** Paying teenage mothers $1 for every day they do not experience a repeat pregnancy (highly efficient, though arguably crosses the line from a pure nudge to a slight economic incentive).
7. **Disulfiram / No-bite Polish:** Using the reflective system to impose a physical penalty (bitter taste, nausea) on the automatic system to stop bad habits.
8. **Civility Check:** Email prompts (e.g., Gmail) that detect angry language and force a 24-hour delay before the message can be sent, preventing impulsive "crimes of passion."

# Module 4: The Experimental Approach

## 4.0 Overview
The experimental approach in behavioral economics utilizes controlled studies to observe real human choices. By blending psychology and economics, researchers test theoretical predictions and identify behavioral facts (e.g., self-control issues, biases). Unlike observational data, experiments allow for precise causal inference by manipulating specific variables while maintaining identical environments for comparison.

## 4.1 The Experimental Ideal

### 4.1.1 History of Randomized Experiments
Causal inference through randomization originated in medicine and statistics before being adopted by social sciences.
*   **James Lind (1774):** Conducted one of the earliest randomized trials to find a cure for scurvy among sailors, administering lemons to one group and comparing them to a control group.
*   **Ronald Fisher:** Statistician who formalized the use of randomization for causal inference in his books *Statistical Method for Research Workers* and *The Design of Experiments*.
*   **Medical Evolution:** 
    *   **Austin B. Hill (1948):** Used randomized control trials (RCTs) to test streptomycin for tuberculosis.
    *   **Jonas Salk (1954):** Developed the polio vaccine through a massive double-blind RCT using Fisher's exact test to measure treatment effects.

### 4.1.2 Social and Laboratory Experiments
*   **RAND Corporation (1971–1982):** Studied the causal effect of health insurance on usage. Found that increased cost-sharing (copay) led to fewer hospital/dental visits and lower overall spending without necessarily harming health.
*   **Burtless and Hausman (1977):** The "Negative Income Tax" experiment in the US/Canada. It found that providing cash transfers to the poor had no significant effect on individual labor supply but unexpectedly increased divorce rates.
*   **Individual Decision Making:**
    *   **Thurnstone (1931):** Used lab experiments to deduce indifference curves between pairs of goods (e.g., hats and shoes).
    *   **Von Neumann-Morgenstern (1944):** Proposed using experiments to construct utility curves and test predictions regarding risk-taking behavior.
*   **Social Interactions:**
    *   **Dresher and Flood (1950):** First implemented the **Prisoner's Dilemma** in a lab setting. Found that human participants rarely reach the pure Nash Equilibrium, nor do they perfectly achieve the socially efficient outcome.
    *   **Guth et al. (1982):** Developed the **Ultimatum Game**, a cornerstone of behavioral bargaining research.

## 4.2 Scope of Experiments

### 4.2.1 Typology of Experiments
Experiments are categorized based on their environment and subject pools:
*   **Laboratory Experiments:** 
    *   **Precision:** High degree of control over the data collection process.
    *   **Incentives:** Participants are typically paid based on their decisions (e.g., the Proposer/Responder payoffs).
    *   **Criticism:** Often rely on **WEIRD** subjects (**W**estern, **E**ducated, **I**ndustrialized, **R**ich, **D**emocratic), leading to concerns about **external validity** and generalizability.
*   **Lab-in-the-field (Artefactual Field Experiments):** 
    *   Researchers set up a controlled lab environment (e.g., a village community center) with a non-standard subject pool (e.g., farmers or local residents).
    *   Bridges the gap between theory and real-world context while maintaining control.
*   **Online and Survey Experiments:** 
    *   Conducted via platforms like Amazon MTurk, Prolific, or Qualtrics.
    *   **Pros:** Low cost, large scale, high generalizability across geographic regions.
    *   **Cons:** Lack of control over environment, potential for AI/bot responses, and time constraints (20–30 minutes max).
*   **Field Experiments and RCTs:**
    *   **Natural Habitat:** Participants are observed in their everyday environments (schools, factories, hospitals) often without knowing they are being studied.
    *   **Causality:** Removes "Social Desirability Bias" and the "Hawthorne Effect."
    *   **Distinction:** Field experiments often test precise theoretical predictions; **RCTs** are more directly linked to evaluating specific policies (e.g., bed nets for malaria).

### 4.2.2 Non-Randomized Experiments
In cases where pure randomization is impossible, researchers use other designs:
*   **Natural Experiments:** Exploiting external shocks or geography (e.g., using Rwanda’s terrain to study the impact of radio nation-building programs on trust).
*   **Quasi-Experiments:** Comparing groups before and after an event (e.g., studying the cognitive bandwidth of farmers before vs. after harvest).
*   **Cross-Cultural Studies:** Comparing different social structures (e.g., Gneezy & List comparing competition preferences in matrilineal vs. patriarchal societies) to show behavior is a result of **nurture** rather than **nature**.

## 4.3 Ethics of Running Experiments

### 4.3.1 Historical Ethical Failures
Past abuses led to the establishment of strict modern protocols:
*   **Nazi Experiments (1943–45):** Medical torture of twins under the pretext of genetic research.
*   **Stanford Prison Experiment (1971):** Students assigned as guards became sadistic, causing severe stress to "prisoners."
*   **Tuskegee Syphilis Study (1932–72):** African American men were denied known treatments (penicillin) to study the progression of the disease.
*   **Milgram Obedience Experiment (1961):** Participants were pressured by authority to administer what they believed were lethal electric shocks to others.

### 4.3.2 The Belmont Report (1979)
This report established the three pillars of modern research ethics:
1.  **Respect for Persons:** Treating participants as autonomous agents. Requires **Informed Consent** and protection for those with diminished autonomy (e.g., minors or cognitively impaired).
2.  **Beneficence:** The "Do No Harm" principle. Researchers must maximize potential benefits while minimizing all possible risks.
3.  **Justice:** Ensuring a fair distribution of the burdens and benefits of research. Vulnerable populations must not be exploited.

### 4.3.3 Modern Requirements & IRBs
*   **Institutional Review Board (IRB):** An independent committee (min. 5 members) that reviews research protocols involving human subjects.
*   **Review Categories:**
    *   **Exempt:** Minimal risk (e.g., standard surveys or educational settings).
    *   **Expedited:** No more than minimal risk; reviewed by a subset of the board.
    *   **Full Board Review:** Required if risks exceed minimal levels.
*   **Deception:** A dominant norm in economics prohibits the use of objectively false information. If used in other disciplines, it requires a mandatory **debriefing** session.
*   **Data Management:** Requires a clear plan for anonymization, password protection, and a policy for data retention/destruction (usually maintained for at least 3 years).

## 4.4 Project and Assignment
To apply these principles, a project must follow specific structural guidelines:
*   **Causality over Correlation:** Simple surveys are insufficient; there must be a randomized component.
*   **Experimental Design:** Must include at least one **Treatment** and one **Control** group.
*   **Analysis:** 
    *   **Balance Table:** Compares covariates (age, gender, income) to ensure groups are statistically similar.
    *   **Summary Table:** Compares outcomes between groups using statistical tests (e.g., **t-test** for continuous variables, **Chi-Square** for binary variables).
    *   **Hypothesis Testing:** Usually written in the negative (Null Hypothesis) to be rejected at a standard alpha of 0.05.

## Quick Revision Summary
- **Key Definitions:**
    - **WEIRD:** Subjects from Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic societies.
    - **External Validity:** The extent to which experimental results can be generalized to the real world.
    - **Incentivization:** The practice of paying participants based on their choices to ensure real-world relevance.
    - **Double-blind:** Neither the participant nor the researcher knows who is in the treatment vs. control group.
- **The Three Pillars (Belmont Report):** Respect for Persons, Beneficence, Justice.
- **Must-Remember Points:**
    - Economics prohibits **deception** to maintain discipline credibility.
    - **RCTs** are the "gold standard" for policy evaluation (e.g., Banerjee, Duflo, Kremer).
    - **Balance tables** prove that randomization worked by showing treatment and control groups are identical in baseline characteristics.
    - **Informed Consent** is an ongoing process, not a one-time signature.
