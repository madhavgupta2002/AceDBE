# Module 1: What is Design Thinking?

## 1.1 Welcome to the module
- Design thinking is a **human-centred, creative approach** to problem-solving.
- It applies across sectors to tackle business challenges, spark innovation, and enable strategic thinking.
- Focuses on understanding needs, generating meaningful ideas, and creating user-focused solutions.
- **Real-world success stories:** Maruti Suzuki, BigBasket, PhonePe, Apple, Ford, and Netflix.

## 1.2 Understanding the Fundamentals of Design Thinking
### Definition
Design thinking is a **human-centric, systematic model of problem-solving**.

### Core Components
- **Human-centric:** Focuses on the human behind the problem (can be a consumer, supplier, partner, employee, or investor). It is broader than just "customer-centric." 
  - *Example:* Nestle Cerelac has multiple stakeholders (baby, mother, father, maid, grandparent, pharmacist, doctor). A solution must balance the needs of all humans involved.
- **Systematic:** Replaces ad-hoc, undisciplined approaches (*jugaad*) with structured methods. Discipline ensures methods are repeatable and scalable.
- **Problem-solving:** It is not about designing physical products; it is about designing your thinking to solve complex, everyday challenges effectively.

## 1.3 Traditional Model of Problem-Solving
- **Stage-Gate / Linear Models:** Traditional models often fail when faced with complex, unpredictable challenges.
- **Idea/Problem First:** Conventional models jump straight from a known problem to an idea or solution.
- **Policy/Product Centric:** Traditional problem-solving often prioritizes regulations or product features over the user.
  - *Example:* An air hostess rattling off emergency exit instructions prioritizes regulatory compliance over ensuring the human actually understands the process. 

## 1.4 Understanding the Design Thinking Model
Unlike traditional models, Design Thinking is heavily **front-loaded** and starts with inspiration, not an idea.

| Stage | Key Concept | Description |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Inspire** | Start with **Why** | Driven by **Aspiration** (e.g., Disney, Mahindra XUV 500) or **Desperation** (e.g., NASA, ISRO). Inspiration galvanizes teams. |
| **2. Empathize & Define** | **Bounded Empathy** | Empathy is proactively understanding what people think/feel. It must be bounded by **Technical Feasibility** (can it be built?) and **Business Viability** (does it make business sense?). |
| **3. Ideate** | **Quantity > Quality** | Focus on generating a massive volume of ideas. High quantity eventually yields high quality (e.g., reducing 300 average ideas to 30 top ideas). |
| **4. Prototype & Test** | Build Confidence | A prototype is a provisional model. **Aesthetic prototypes** test looks/feel; **Functional prototypes** test workings (e.g., Titan Edge watch, Dyson bagless vacuums). |
| **5. Scale** | True Innovation | Innovation only happens when a validated idea is successfully scaled (e.g., Pablo Picasso painting daily). |

## 1.5 The Double Diamond Model of Design Thinking
Design thinking oscillates between expanding your thinking (**Divergent**) and narrowing it down (**Convergent**).

- **Empathize (Diverge):** Talk to many stakeholders to gather wide perspectives.
- **Define (Converge):** Narrow down to a specific, solvable problem.
- **Ideate (Diverge):** Generate a massive, wide-ranging quantity of ideas.
- **Prototype & Test (Converge):** Validate specific ideas to find the optimal solution.
- **Scale (Diverge):** Expand the final solution to the broader market.

### Case Study: Mahatma Gandhi's Design Thinking
- **Inspire:** Thrown out of a first-class train in South Africa (1888).
- **Empathize (Diverge):** Wore Khadi to connect deeply with the common Indian.
- **Define (Converge):** Defined the path of *Ahimsa* (Non-violence) and *Satyagraha*.
- **Prototype & Test (Converge):** The Dandi March (tested the concept of non-violent law-breaking on a small scale with salt).
- **Scale (Diverge):** Expanded the movement, leading to independence in 1947.

## 1.6 Why Design Thinking Now?
### 1. Products and Services Blurred into Experiences
- The focus has shifted from standalone products to **Experience Management**.
- *Moments of Truth:* Crucial interaction points (e.g., FMCG packaging on a shelf, checkout queues, unboxing Apple products, disposal of batteries).

### 2. Multifaceted Problems
- Problems are now "wicked" and highly complex (e.g., low voter turnout vs. simple tax evasion).
- Requires **Systems Thinking** to avoid local optimization (e.g., fixing one junction only to move the traffic jam to the next flyover).

### 3. B2C / B2H Transformation
- Shift from Business-to-Consumer to **Business-to-Human (B2H)**.
- Companies must anticipate needs and adapt to users, not force users to adapt to products (e.g., Ford placing indicators on the left side failed in India; Kia introducing sunroofs forced market leaders to adapt).

### 4. Digitization of Engagements
- Mask extreme back-end complexity with extreme front-end simplicity.
- *Examples:* Digiyatra (facial recognition), UPI (QR codes replacing cash), FASTag (passive stickers). 

### 5. Knowledgeable and Restless Customers
- The power asymmetry is gone; customers rely on crowd wisdom (reviews) over corporate claims.
- **C&D (Connect and Develop):** Replaces R&D. Co-create solutions *with* customers rather than building in isolation.

### 6. Widespread Experimentation Tools
- **Exaptation:** Consumers find unintended applications for technology (e.g., using Google Lens for homework, Instagram shifting from photo sharing to brand marketing).

### 7. Clash of Business Models
- Competition is now between **Business Models**, not just products.
- *Components:* Value Generating, Value Delivery, Value Appropriation.
- *Examples:* Netflix (disrupted Blockbuster via streaming and original content), Tesla (free charging wrapped in upfront cost), Airbnb/Uber (platform models over inventory).

### 8. Unpredictable Markets
- Markets throw sudden shocks (e.g., Gillette losing sales because beards became trendy; Activa scooters dominating a market obsessed with mileage). Design thinking helps organizations remain adaptable.

## 1.7 The Sweet Spot of Design Thinking
Design thinking is not for every problem. Different problems require different thinking models based on ambiguity.

| Thinking Model | Problem State | Solution State | Best Used For |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Lean Thinking** | Known | Known | Speed, efficiency, automation (e.g., Six Sigma, ATMs). |
| **Critical Thinking** | Unknown | Known | Root cause analysis, diagnostics (e.g., 5 Whys, medical diagnosis). |
| **Lateral Thinking** | Known | Unknown | Sideways/creative workarounds (e.g., using ice to lower heavy machinery). |
| **Design Thinking** | **Unknown** | **Unknown** | High ambiguity, wicked problems, discovery (e.g., nudging behaviors via Piano Stairs). |

## 1.8 When to Adopt Design Thinking?
Design thinking is time-consuming and resource-heavy. It should only be adopted when the following **5 Conditions** are met:
1. **Audacity of Goal:** The objective must be massive (e.g., asking for a "beautiful wall" instead of a "hole in the wall").
2. **Ambiguity of Context:** There must be high uncertainty and lack of clear answers.
3. **Availability of Time:** You need time to explore dead ends, false starts, and multiple possibilities.
4. **Access to Customers:** You must be able to continuously co-create and validate with the end-user.
5. **Diversity of Team:** Requires cognitive, ethnic, generational, and temperamental diversity to prevent premature consensus.

### The Venn Diagram of Innovation
A winning design thinking idea sits at the intersection of:
- **Human Desirability:** Do people want it?
- **Technical Feasibility:** Can we build it within time and budget?
- **Business Viability:** Does it make economic sense?

## 1.9 Key Tenets of Design Thinking
1. **Human at the Center:** Focus on the subject (the human), not the object (the product). *Example:* The Embrace Baby Warmer quilt focuses on the baby's need for warmth, rather than building a complex, expensive incubator.
2. **Focus on Thinking, not Designing:** Ensure the product aligns with user aspirations. *Example:* Tata Nano failed because it focused on designing a cheap car, ignoring the human aspiration of car ownership as a status symbol.
3. **Solve WITH the Customer, not FOR them:** Co-create solutions. *Example:* The $10 Mirror Box used to cure Phantom Limb pain by tricking the brain, avoiding complex neurosurgery.
4. **Think Beyond Products:** Build an ecosystem. *Example:* Royal Enfield selling gear and accessories; Asian Paints offering home-making and interior design services.
5. **Diverge Before You Converge:** Compartmentalize thinking into three strict, sequential stages: 
   - Problem Exploration
   - Solution Generation (defer judgment)
   - Idea Validation
6. **Move from Symptoms $\rightarrow$ Problems $\rightarrow$ Root Causes:** Never stop at surface-level symptoms. (e.g., Thirst = Symptom, Diabetes = Problem, Lifestyle = Root Cause).
7. **Embrace Minimalism ("What Less?", not "What Else?"):** Avoid over-engineering. Use the **FAB Model** (Features, Advantages, Benefits). More features do not equal more benefits. *Example:* A bulky VCR remote vs. a simple Fire TV Stick; the timeless design of a pencil or safety pin.
8. **Think With Your Hands:** Physical activities (sketching, playing instruments) engage both brain hemispheres (ambidexterity) and unlock creativity. 
9. **Sell the Problem Before the Solution:** Use the **SCQA Framework** to structure business storytelling:
   - **S**ituation: The current context.
   - **C**omplication: Why the situation is now a problem.
   - **Q**uestion: The central question to be resolved.
   - **A**nswer: The proposed solutions.
10. **Visualize Your Thinking:** Draw your ideas (using doodles, stickies, Lego) instead of just speaking or writing them. Drawing forces clarity and invites collaborative refinement.
11. **Fail Often to Succeed Sooner:** The goal is to be *wrong* early to get it *right* eventually. Maximize the number of experiments per dollar/hour. *Example:* SpaceX enduring multiple rocket explosions to perfect reusable technology.

## 1.10 Key Takeaways
- Design thinking is a **human-centric systematic model of problem-solving**.
- Unlike traditional models, it starts with an **inspiration**, not an idea.
- It is highly relevant today due to factors ranging from **B2C transformation to market surprises**.
- It is best used for tackling **unknown problems with no known solutions** (high ambiguity).
- **Five key conditions** (Audacity, Ambiguity, Time, Customer access, Diversity) dictate when to use it effectively.
- The tenets provide guiding principles focused on **empathy, creativity, minimalism, and iterative failure**.

# Module 2: Inspire, Empathise and Define

## 2.1 Welcome to the module
- Explores the first three stages of Design Thinking: **Inspire, Empathise, and Define**.
- Focuses on getting inspired, understanding human needs, and framing the right problems before building solutions.
- Incorporates a practical application project based on the IIM Bangalore staff canteen.

## 2.2 Mind of a Design Thinker
### Toolset, Skillset, and Mindset
To learn any discipline, you need three components:
- **Toolset:** The available resources or instruments (e.g., paint, canvas).
- **Skillset:** The ability to use the tools effectively (e.g., painting techniques).
- **Mindset:** The most difficult attribute to hone. It dictates true innovation (e.g., Picasso inventing the *collage* by changing his worldview on art).

### The Three Attributes of a Design Thinker's Mindset
| Attribute | Meaning | Application |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Clear Head** | Knowing your priorities in life. | Solving a problem with a clear, focused purpose rather than just learning a method. |
| **Deep Heart** | Having compassion and empathy. | Caring proactively about others' pain and aspirations; being considerate without just being emotional. |
| **Thick Skin** | Being indifferent to unwarranted opinions. | Pursuing what is right without seeking constant approval or worrying about others' judgments. |

## 2.3 Honing a clear mind
### Zone of Concern vs. Zone of Influence
- **Zone of Concern:** Everything that bothers you (e.g., global wars, news, office gossip). It is limitless and drains energy. 
- **Zone of Influence:** The small subset of things you can actually control or act upon (e.g., your own behavior and reactions).
- **The Zen State:** Achieved when your Zone of Concern overlaps exactly with your Zone of Influence (worrying *only* about what you can control).
- **Action Rule:** 
  - Say **"No"** to shrink your Zone of Concern.
  - Say **"Yes"** to expand your Zone of Influence.

### The ITC Model
A framework to manage reactions and maintain a clear head:
- **I - Ignore (~50%):** Let things pass if there is no upside beyond ego (e.g., road rage). *Rule: Ignore the behavior, not the person (behavior is driven by environment).*
- **T - Tolerate (~40%):** Absorb and soak in situations if there is an upside for self-development (e.g., harsh academic/managerial feedback).
- **C - Confront (~10%):** Actively fight back *only* when the upside is very high and justifies the downside/risk (e.g., fighting abuse or cheating).

## 2.4 Inspire: The Why of a Problem
### Asking "Why" vs. "How"
- The question **"Why to solve the problem?"** is infinitely more important than "How to solve the problem?".
- Focus on the higher-order goal. *Example:* A customer asking for a "hole in the wall" actually wants a "beautiful home". 

### Three Types of Problem Solvers
| Type | Characteristics | Value & Status |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Pharmacist** | Transactional, quick fixes, solves 100 problems/day. | Low value, easily substituted (e.g., by AI). |
| **Doctor** | Relational, maps symptoms to root causes, solves 20 problems/day. | High value, tracks progress over time. |
| **Surgeon** | Last resort, high knowledge asymmetry, solves 2-5 problems/day. | Highest premium and respect; operates with full patient surrender. |

## 2.5 Prioritising the problems
### Bruise vs. Cancer Problems
Not all problems warrant Design Thinking. You must distinguish between:
- **Bruises (~80%):** Minor problems that heal with time. Trying to fix them often makes them worse (e.g., a pimple, minor relationship spats).
- **Cancers (~20%):** Sinister problems that aggravate over time if ignored (e.g., endless social media scrolling before bed).

### How to Evaluate a Problem's Gravity
- **Create Space:** Distance yourself from the problem via physical, temporal (time), and emotional space. Objectivity reduces the perceived size of the problem.
- **Pay Grade Rule:** Work at your pay grade, but **think two levels above your pay grade**. If your boss's boss wouldn't care about the problem, it might not be worth solving.

### Quick Fix vs. Perma Fix
- **Quick Fix (*Jugaad*):** Moving straight from Symptom $\rightarrow$ Solution (Band-aid approach).
- **Perma Fix (Design Thinking):** Moving from Symptom $\rightarrow$ Problem $\rightarrow$ Root Cause $\rightarrow$ Solution.

## 2.6 Design Brief
#### Definition
A brief, well-thought-out statement of intent setting the context for a project before problem-solving begins. It acts as a **Sandbox** (clear outer boundaries, complete creative freedom inside).

### Three Conditions of a Good Design Brief
1. **Inspirational:** Must provide a stretch goal to trigger creativity.
2. **Constrained:** Must have clear limitations (e.g., budget, time, size). *Creativity only happens under constraints.*
3. **Vague Enough:** Must sit at a "10,000-feet view." It should not dictate *how* the problem must be solved (e.g., avoiding prescribing "better compensation" as the only fix for retention).

*Examples:*
- JFK: "Put a man on the moon and bring him back safely by the end of this decade."
- Aravind Eye Hospital: "Eradicate needless blindness."

## 2.7 Empathy
#### Definition
Empathy is understanding others' pain proactively and being considerate. It requires stepping out of your own emotional makeup.

### Daniel Goleman's 4 Components of Emotional Intelligence (EQ)
1. **Self-Awareness:** Recognizing your own emotional state (e.g., knowing you are angry).
2. **Self-Management:** Taking corrective action based on awareness (e.g., taking a deep breath).
3. **Empathy:** Understanding and acknowledging others' emotions.
4. **Social Skills:** Modulating your behavior based on the audience's emotional state (e.g., teaching a 5-year-old differently than a 45-year-old executive).

### Three Levels of Empathy
1. **Cognitive Empathy:** Intellectual understanding ("I understand your problem").
2. **Emotional Empathy:** Feeling the pain ("I feel your pain").
3. **Empathic Concern:** The highest form—understanding, feeling, and *acting to fix the problem* (e.g., Amazon refunding unused Prime membership days without prompting).

### Listening Positions (Julian Treasure)
- **Active vs. Passive:** Active drives the conversation (probing questions); Passive allows the speaker to vent without interruption.
- **Reductive vs. Expansive:** Reductive tries to conclude and summarize; Expansive asks "what else?" to explore possibilities.
- **Critical vs. Empathetic:** Critical looks for logical coherence/contradictions; Empathetic ignores logic to prioritize the speaker's emotional release.

### The RASA Technique
A framework for active listening:
- **R - Receive:** Pay attention without judging.
- **A - Appreciate:** Acknowledge the effort the person took to speak.
- **S - Summarize:** Parrot the point back to build rapport and plug information gaps.
- **A - Ask:** Ask open, empathetic questions (e.g., "What happened?" instead of "Why are you late?").

### 3 Practical Tips for Cultivating Empathy
1. **Listen with intent:** Give people your full attention.
2. **Observe with purpose:** Look for areas of improvement actively.
3. **Defer your judgment:** Separate the person from their situational behavior (e.g., the story of Buddha and Angulimala).

## 2.8 Engineering Empathy
Empathy is a science that can be created, measured, and mapped using 6 visual tools:

### 1. Job to be Done (Clayton Christensen)
- People "hire" products to do a job. 
- You must fulfill both the **Functional component** (e.g., a watch telling time) and the **Emotional component** (e.g., a watch acting as status/adornment).
- Focus on the *desire* rather than just the *need* (e.g., Bisleri providing glass bottles/refill machines to hotels to satisfy the desire for water *without* plastic waste).

### 2. Problem Exploration Map
- A mind map treating the initial issue as a **Symptom**, branching out into **Causes**, and drilling down into **Root Causes**.
- *Example:* Declining book reading (Symptom) $\rightarrow$ Difficulty choosing a book (Cause) $\rightarrow$ Lack of trustworthy reviews (Root Cause).

### 3. Stakeholder Map
- Identifies **7 to 9 key stakeholders** involved in a problem/solution.
- Documents the **Positives (Motivations)** and **Negatives (Detractions)** for each.
- Uncovers *conflicting demands* (e.g., Shop floor automation is a Positive for a Supervisor [high productivity] but a Negative for the Labor Union [job loss]).

### 4. Customer Journey Map
- Maps the user's experience linearly across **time** and various touchpoints.
- Identifies current or potential pain points at specific stages (e.g., confiscating coconuts at airport security leading to the idea of 3D baggage scanners).

### 5. Empathy Map
- Maps the emotional and psychological makeup of the user across three channels:
  - **Input:** What is the user *Seeing* and *Hearing*?
  - **Processing:** What is the user *Thinking* and *Feeling*?
  - **Output:** What is the user *Doing* and *Saying*?
- Distills findings into a **Pain vs. Gain** equation. (Users will only engage if $Gains > Pains$).

### 6. Buyer/User Persona
- A microscopic profile acting as a "mask" to understand the typical user.
- Captures: Demographics, Objectives, Fears/Anxieties, Needs, and Channels of influence/sales.

## 2.9 Define
After gathering massive amounts of empathy data, you must narrow down what to solve.

### Bounded Empathy
Empathy cannot be limitless. It must be constrained by:
1. **Technical Feasibility:** Can we build it with current technology, time, and budget?
2. **Business Viability:** Does solving this make economic sense for the company?

### Pareto’s Law ($80/20$ Rule)
- Focus exclusively on the **20% of problems** that will deliver **80% of the positive impact**. Do not sweat the small stuff.

### MECE Framework (Barbara Minto)
How to structure defined problems:
- **Mutually Exclusive:** Problems should not overlap; they must be distinct.
- **Collectively Exhaustive:** Together, solving these distinct problems should cover the entire problem space.

## 2.10 Canteen project
- A practical application exercise observing the IIM Bangalore staff canteen.
- **Goal:** Apply the *Problem Exploration Map*, *Stakeholder Map*, and *Customer Journey Map* to a real-world setting to define critical, high-impact problems.

## 2.11 Key Takeaways
- Great problem-solving starts with understanding **why** the problem matters (Inspire).
- The right mindset (Clear Head, Deep Heart, Thick Skin) is essential.
- Empathy is cultivated through good listening (Julian Treasure's RASA).
- A **Design Brief** (Inspirational, Constrained, Vague) must be developed before defining a problem.
- Empathy can be engineered using visual mind-mapping tools (Stakeholder map, Journey map, Empathy map, etc.).
- Problem statements must be prioritized using **Bounded Empathy, Pareto’s Law, and MECE**.

# Module 3: Creativity and Ideation

## 3.1 Welcome to the module
- **Ideation** is the phase where the focus shifts from understanding the problem (empathy) to generating solutions.
- Ideation must be **deferred** until the problem is fully understood from a 360-degree perspective.
- Traditional models jump straight to solutions; design thinking enters ideation only with rich context and clarity.

## 3.2 What is creativity?
#### Definition
**Creativity** is the ability to come up with an idea that is both **novel** (new/unique) and **useful** (practical/valuable). An idea lacking either is not truly creative.

### Creativity vs. Intelligence
Intelligence and creativity are related but fundamentally different. 

| Feature | Intelligence | Creativity |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Core Function** | Ability to recognize and predict a pattern. | Ability to break a pattern and form a new one. |
| **Thinking Style** | **Convergent Thinking:** Finding the single right answer (e.g., $5 \times 5 = 25$). | **Divergent Thinking:** Generating multiple possibilities (e.g., How many ways can you make 10?). |
| **Approach** | About making choices (e.g., the best move in chess). | About generating combinations (e.g., surprising the opponent). |
| **Focus** | Deep knowledge in a specific domain. | Wide knowledge across multiple disparate domains. |

### The Role of Latent Inhibition
- High intelligence (IQ) does not automatically guarantee high creativity. 
- **Latent Inhibition:** The psychological filter that determines how a person reacts to familiar stimuli. 
- **Low Latent Inhibition** mediates high creativity. A person with low latent inhibition throws caution to the wind, fears failure less, and is not hindered by self-criticism or societal judgment.
- **IQ Formula:** $$IQ = \left( \frac{Mental Age}{Chronological Age} \right) \times 100$$

## 3.3 Testing creativity
Creativity is a science that can be systematically tested and measured. 

### 1. Alternate Uses Test (J.P. Guilford)
Tests divergent thinking by asking for multiple uses of a common object (e.g., a comb). It measures creativity across four parameters:
1. **Fluency:** The speed and volume of ideas generated without getting stuck (overcoming *Functional Fixedness*).
2. **Flexibility:** Adaptability to entirely different categories of ideas.
3. **Originality:** The uniqueness of the idea compared to others.
4. **Elaboration:** The amount of detail and flesh added to the core idea.

### 2. Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking (Ellis Torrance)
Builds on multiple intelligences to test creativity in two dimensions:
- **Verbal:** Creating new words/associations (e.g., Anagrams from "VERBAL" $\rightarrow$ "VERB", "LAB").
- **Figural:** Creating distinct visual images (e.g., drawing 10 completely different objects starting from 10 identical circles).

### 3. Remote Associates Test (Sarnoff Mednick)
Tests the ability to establish novel connections between apparently distinct, unrelated domains. 
- *Example:* What connects "Cottage", "Swiss", and "Cake"? $\rightarrow$ **Cheese**.
- *Example:* What connects "Computer", "Trap", and "Mickey"? $\rightarrow$ **Mouse**.

## 3.4 Honing creativity
Creativity is a skill that can be deliberately nurtured. Key strategies include:

- **Curiosity:** Maintaining an insatiable desire to explore beyond professional boundaries (e.g., Leonardo Da Vinci's exploration of anatomy, art, and aviation).
- **Cultivating Hobbies:** Hobbies provide self-confidence, diverse perspectives, and new networks. Engaging in physical/musical hobbies promotes **ambidexterity**, building thicker neural connections between the brain's left and right hemispheres (e.g., Albert Einstein playing the violin).
- **Multiple Affiliations:** Do not limit your identity to your job. Having multiple dimensions (artist, scientist, athlete) prevents burnout and provides purpose.
- **Networking:** Build a **wide and shallow** professional network, and a **narrow and deep** personal network. Speak to strangers to expose yourself to uncertainty and fresh perspectives.
- **Taking Half-Chances:** Say "yes" to opportunities even if you feel unready. It lowers the fear of failure and uncovers hidden competencies.
- **Sense of Humor:** Humor and creativity share the same neural circuitry. Both rely on a linear setup followed by a sudden, benign violation of logic (breaking the pattern).
- **Meditation & Mindfulness:** Centering the mind to the present moment. Three core practices:
  1. **Non-resistance:** Accepting reality as it is without psychological friction.
  2. **Non-attachment:** Aspiring for goals but not tying your identity to the outcome.
  3. **Non-judgment:** Viewing situations objectively without assigning immediate value labels.

## 3.5 Rules of ideation and brainstorming
### The 4 Rules of Effective Ideation
1. **Quality comes from Quantity:** $Few \ Good \ Ideas \propto Massive \ Quantity \ of \ Average \ Ideas$. Filtering 500 ideas down to 50 yields higher quality than just coming up with 50 ideas from scratch.
2. **Build on others' ideas:** Replace "No, but..." with "Yes, and...".
3. **Defer Judgment:** Do not evaluate or shoot down ideas during the generation phase. Judgment kills creative momentum.
4. **Sketch, don't speak:** Visual thinking provides clarity. Drawing an idea forces detail and invites constructive collaboration rather than argument.

### Why Traditional Brainstorming Fails
Research shows group brainstorming often yields fewer and poorer ideas than individuals working alone due to:
1. **Idea Blocking:** Forgetting or holding back an idea while waiting for your turn to speak.
2. **Groupthink:** Reaching premature consensus to maintain harmony, especially when there is a power differential (e.g., agreeing with the boss).
3. **Evaluation Apprehension:** Fear of being judged or mocked for presenting a radical or contrarian idea.
4. **Social Loafing:** Free-riding behavior where a few team members carry the cognitive load of the whole group.

### The Solution: Brainwriting
- Idea generation done **anonymously** and **spatially** (e.g., using Post-it notes on a central board).
- Prevents groupthink and evaluation apprehension because ideas are separated from identities.
- Best team size for generation is **two** (pairs).

## 3.6 Methods of ideation
### 1. Challenging Assumptions
- **Process:** List all fundamental assumptions about a product $\rightarrow$ Challenge every single one $\rightarrow$ Design a solution that overcomes the assumption.
- *Examples:* iPhone removing the physical keyboard; Steve Jobs removing the cooling fan and screws from the Mac; Scooters eliminating the stepney (spare wheel).

### 2. Brainwriting & Mind Mapping
- Mimics how the brain naturally works (spatially, not linearly).
- **Best practices:** Use unruled notebooks, multiple colored pens, doodles, and think in your native language to prevent translation loss.

### 3. Value Chain Matrix
- Matrix mapping the stages of a user's journey against strategic objectives.
- *Columns:* Customer Journey Stages (e.g., Get coupon $\rightarrow$ Queue $\rightarrow$ Find seat $\rightarrow$ Eat $\rightarrow$ Dispose).
- *Rows:* Objectives (e.g., Convenience, Efficiency, Sustainability).
- Generates precise, targeted ideas for every intersection.

### 4. Tiers of Customers
Categorize the market to uncover unexplored areas for innovation:
1. **Loyal:** Frequent, happy users.
2. **Dissatisfied:** Current users looking for alternatives.
3. **Refusing:** Aware of the product but actively reject it (due to cost, experience, or stigma).
4. **Unexplored:** The largest market segment. People unaware the product exists or unaware they are allowed to use it.

### 5. Lateral Thinking Triggers (Edward de Bono)
Moving sideways away from logic to spark ideas using triggers:
- **Subtraction:** Removing a core element (e.g., removing physical coupons).
- **Task Unification:** Making one element do two jobs (e.g., the amount of food a user takes also acts as real-time feedback on food preferences).
- **Multiplication:** Duplicating elements (e.g., having two separate queues to reduce waiting).
- **Division:** Splitting an element (e.g., dividing one queue into a Veg and Non-Veg queue).

### 6. SCAMPER Technique
A shorthand checklist for idea generation:
- **S - Substitute:** Replace a part/process.
- **C - Combine:** Merge two ideas.
- **A - Adapt:** Adjust to a new context.
- **M - Modify:** Change the scale or attributes.
- **P - Purpose (Repurpose):** Use it for something else.
- **E - Eliminate:** Remove an element entirely.
- **R - Reverse:** Turn the concept upside down.

### 7. Analogous Design
- Drawing inspiration from a completely unrelated domain solving a similar generalized problem.
- *Example:* Improving a campus cafeteria's efficiency and space optimization by studying how air hostesses serve food on an airplane. 

### 8. Design for the Extreme
- Designing solutions for extreme scenarios or users, which eventually benefit the mainstream market.
- *Examples:* Subtitles created for the hearing impaired now boost foreign movie viewership; Smartphone vibration designed for the visually impaired; Quick Commerce born out of the extreme constraints of the pandemic.

### 9. Six Paths Framework (Blue Ocean Strategy)
Six triggers to break out of standard industry thinking:
1. Look across **alternate industries**.
2. Look across **strategic groups** within industries.
3. Look across the chain of **buyers/user groups**.
4. Look across **complementary product and service offerings**.
5. Rethink the **functional-emotional orientation** of the industry.
6. Participate in shaping **time and trends**.

### 10. Nine Windows (TRIZ)
A $3 \times 3$ matrix evaluating a problem across **Time** and **System Levels**:
- **Time:** Past, Present, Future.
- **System Level:** Sub-system (components), System (the product), Super-system (the environment/network).
- *Example:* Reinventing public transport by looking at the Past (double-decker buses) and the Future (electric tech) to create Electric Double-Decker buses.

## 3.7 Stories of the "Tasteholders": The canteen stakeholders
- Practical application phase involving listening to canteen managers, staff, and patrons.
- **Focus Theme:** Reducing food waste.
- Goal is to apply ideation methods (SCAMPER, Analogous Design, Value Chain) to the observational insights gathered from the canteen environment.

## 3.8 Key Takeaways
- **Intelligence** is recognizing a pattern; **Creativity** is breaking it.
- Creativity can be measured using Alternate Uses, Torrance Tests, and Remote Associates Tests.
- Creativity is a nurtured skill built through curiosity, hobbies, networking, and taking half-chances.
- **Ideation Rules:** Quantity drives quality, build on ideas, defer judgment, and use visual sketching.
- Traditional brainstorming fails due to idea blocking and groupthink; **Brainwriting** (anonymous, spatial) is vastly superior.
- Structured methods like SCAMPER, Lateral Thinking, and Nine Windows force the brain to generate ideas from unique, unconventional angles.

# Module 4: Prototype, Test and Scale

## 4.1 Welcome to the module
- Prototyping is the execution phase where design thinking moves from **ideas to action**.
- It focuses on bringing concepts to life, gathering real-world feedback, and refining solutions.
- True innovation is separated from mere creativity by the ability to **scale** ideas for lasting impact.

## 4.2 Idea shortlisting
When dealing with hundreds of ideas generated during ideation, it is crucial to use mental heuristics to sift through and identify the strongest concepts. 

### Rubric 1: The Innovation Trinity (DFV Matrix)
All ideas must be evaluated against three criteria simultaneously to find the "sweet spot" of innovation:
- **Human Desirability:** Does the human (consumer, employee, or stakeholder) truly crave this solution? It addresses implicit and future desires.
- **Technical Feasibility:** Do we have the technological capability to build this within the allocated time and budget?
- **Business Viability:** Does this idea make commercial sense? Can it be profitable? (e.g., Quick commerce startups often fail here despite high desirability and feasibility).

### Rubric 2: The Impact-Time Matrix
A $2 \times 2$ matrix evaluating the potential **Impact** of an idea versus the **Time** required to see results. Organizations need a portfolio of ideas across the first three quadrants:

| Quadrant | Impact | Time | Description & Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Starters** | Low / Moderate | Short | Quick wins. Builds instant confidence and rallies the team. |
| **Stars** | High | Short | Highly lucrative ideas that allow technological leapfrogging over competitors. |
| **Pipeline** | High | Long | Long-term R&D projects requiring 2-3 years to yield massive returns. |
| **Pipe Dreams** | Low | Long | High effort, low reward. These ideas should be **parked indefinitely**. |

## 4.3 Why prototyping and rules
#### Definition
A **prototype** is a provisional, physical, or conceptual manifestation of an idea. In design thinking, a prototype is worth a thousand meetings.

### Why Prototyping?
- **Develops a "Prototyping Mentality":** Cultivates comfort with imperfection.
- **Offers Clarity:** Physical models reveal hidden constraints (e.g., IDEO's steel shopping cart prototype revealed weight and bulkiness issues).
- **Provides Visibility:** Gives stakeholders and investors confidence that progress is being made.
- **Identifies Issues Early:** Prevents failure at the final realization stage by avoiding "Big Bang" implementations.
- **Solicits Timely Feedback:** Allows for continuous tweaking before heavy investments are made.
- **Enables Soft Selling:** Helps customers visualize the future (e.g., an architect's 3D model).

### Types of Prototypes
1. **Functional Prototype:** *Works* like the product, but does not *look* like it. Used to test core mechanisms (e.g., Titan Edge testing a raw watch module underwater for 50-meter resistance).
2. **Aesthetic Prototype:** *Looks* like the product, but does not *work* like it. Used to test user interface, ergonomics, and visual appeal.

### Core Rules of Prototyping
- **Do the last experiment first:** Always test the most difficult, fundamental hypothesis first. 
  - *Example:* The first ATM prototype used a hidden human handing out cash to test if consumers were comfortable interacting with a machine, before building the complex counting technology.
- **Maximize learning per unit time and dollar:** Pack as many quick experiments into your budget as possible. Keep it **quick and dirty**. 
  - *Example:* Apple testing the ideal iPhone touch-icon size (fixed at 3 square pixels) using a simple rapid-fire tapping game.
- **Fail faster to succeed sooner:** Treat failure as an accelerated learning curve. 
  - *Example:* James Dyson built 5,000 prototypes before perfecting the bagless vacuum. 
  - *Note:* Failed ideas should be stored (like IDEO's "Tech Box") because an idea that fails in one context might succeed in another (e.g., Google Glass failing as eyewear but succeeding as Google Lens software).
- **Kill your darlings:** Do not become emotionally attached to your pet ideas. Be willing to kill them before the market does.

## 4.4 Prototyping methods
Prototypes must be utilized for both physical products and intangible services.

### Methods for Product Prototyping
| Method | Application |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Paper Prototyping** | Drawing software user interfaces (UI) on Post-it notes. Allows rapid rearrangement of screens to test user flow. |
| **Lego and Clay** | using simple building blocks to create 3D forms. Makes serious professionals playful and provides extreme spatial clarity. |
| **Physical Mock-ups** | Using cheap, accessible materials (wood, thermocol, coat hangers) to simulate physical dimensions and mechanics. |

### Methods for Service Prototyping
| Method | Application |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Storyboarding** | Borrowed from advertising; sketching service steps frame-by-frame to visualize the customer journey. |
| **Skits and Plays** | Role-playing a service encounter (e.g., a customer opening a bank account) to test the human interaction flow. |
| **Storytelling (SCQA)** | Narrating the idea via **S**ituation, **C**omplication, **Q**uestion, and **A**nswer to gauge customer reactions without building anything. |
| **Scenarios** | Testing the service concept against three strict conditions: **Best-case**, **Usual-case**, and **Worst-case** (e.g., cafeteria functioning during a gas shortage). |

## 4.5 Methods of scaling
Scaling is the final step that turns creativity into true innovation.

### 1. Business Model Canvas (Alexander Osterwalder)
A framework that maps out how an idea will survive in the real world by answering three questions: How does the business *generate* value, *deliver* value, and *appropriate* value?
- **Center:** Value Proposition (The unique offering).
- **Downstream (Delivery):** Target Customers, Marketing Channels, Sales/Outreach Channels.
- **Midstream (Generation):** Key Resources (staff, machinery), Key Activities (cooking, cleaning), Key Partners (suppliers).
- **Upstream (Appropriation):** Cost Structures (fixed/variable costs, rent) and Revenue Streams.

### 2. OKR Framework (Andy Grove)
A structured matrix to measure progress and growth. "Whatever cannot be measured cannot be managed."
- **Objective (The What):** The overarching goal you want to achieve (e.g., Lose 5kg in a month).
- **Key Results (The How):** The specific, measurable actions required to hit the objective (e.g., Walk 10,000 steps daily, eat 5 small meals).

## 4.6 Interview with Dr Pavan Soni (Application to Career/Life)
Design thinking is not just for designers or startups; it applies to nations, NGOs, students, and personal career navigation.

### Managing Career and Life
- **Think in Decades:** Overestimate what you can do in a decade, underestimate what you can do in a year. Focus on what **will not change** over the next 10 years (e.g., the need for education and health).
- **Own your career:** Do not outsource career decisions to parents, managers, or HR.
- **Handling Rejection:** Rejection is a learning input. You will only be hurt by rejection if you lack clarity. You can be confused about what you want, but you must be absolutely clear about **what you do not want**.

### Saying "No" vs. "Yes"
- Shrink your **Zone of Concern** by saying "No" to things outside your control to preserve mental bandwidth. 
- Expand your **Zone of Influence** by saying "Yes" only to tasks where you can operate better than anyone else.
- Maintain absolute **non-negotiables** in life to prevent being manipulated by external perceptions.

### Design Thinking in IT and R&D
- **IT Sector:** India's scalable tech success (UPI, DigiYatra, FASTag) works because it masks back-end complexity with extreme front-end simplicity. It relies on indigenous platforms and augments humans ($Tech + Human$) rather than replacing them.
- **R&D Sector:** R&D requires deferred gratification. To prevent burnout and maintain creativity, R&D professionals must have **multiple affiliations** (hobbies like art, music, or literature) to foster cross-domain associative thinking (e.g., A.P.J. Abdul Kalam as a poet and writer).
